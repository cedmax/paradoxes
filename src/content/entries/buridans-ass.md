sourcesVerified: true
problem: A hungry donkey stands exactly halfway between two identical piles of hay. What should it do?
claimA: "A rational agent only chooses when it has a reason to prefer one option."
claimB: "A rational agent facing a perfect tie should still choose and eat."
whatBreaks: "Whether rational choice requires a reason favouring one option, or can proceed when the reasons are equal."
statusNote: "the dispute is whether a rational agent can simply pick when reasons are tied, or whether a perfect tie prevents choice or never really occurs."
setup: "A hungry donkey stands equally close to two identical piles of hay. Nothing favours either pile. If it can act only for a reason that favours one, it cannot move and starves. Yet choosing either pile seems plainly better than starving."
whyWrong: "There is a reason to eat, but no reason to prefer one pile. The puzzle asks whether the first reason is enough for a rational choice. It is a thought experiment about choosing, not a prediction about real donkeys."
resolution:
  - "One response allows an arbitrary pick. Another says unnoticed preferences always break real ties. A third says free choice can decide when reasons do not."
  - "These answers differ over what a choice needs to count as rational or free. None simply discovers a difference between the two piles in the imagined case."
positions:
  - title: "Pick arbitrarily"
    body: "Weintraub argues that choosing without a reason favouring one option can be reasonable. Cost: some choices then lack an explanation of why that option was chosen."
  - title: "Real ties never occur"
    body: "Leibniz keeps the demand for a sufficient reason and appeals to tiny unnoticed preferences. Cost: a perfect tie would still block choice, and the reply assumes real cases always contain a difference."
  - title: "Free choice breaks the tie"
    body: "An agent can freely choose despite equal reasons, as Al-Ghazali argued. Cost: this requires a kind of choice not settled by the reasons, which many reject."
finePrint: "The story is not found in Buridan’s writings; the name may have begun as a parody of his account of choice. Earlier versions appear in Aristotle and Al-Ghazali. Lamport’s related engineering result concerns continuous inputs and discrete decisions under specific assumptions. It is not a proof that an actual donkey must starve."
