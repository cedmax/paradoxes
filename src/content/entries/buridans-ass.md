title: Buridan's ass
titleAccent: ass
chapter: choice-identity
status: disputed
simplification: light
order: 5
written: true
sourcesVerified: false
problem: A hungry donkey stands exactly halfway between two identical piles of hay. Which does it choose?
claimA: A rational creature only chooses an option when it has a reason to prefer it.
claimB: Between two identical options, there is no reason to prefer either.
verdict: It is arbitrary choice or starvation, and what that shows about reason and free will is argued.
whatBreaks: The idea that every choice must be backed by a reason that favours it.
statusNote: experts agree it is a real puzzle but disagree on which claim to give up.
setup: "A donkey is equally hungry and equally close to two identical piles of hay. It has no reason to go to one rather than the other. If it only moves for a reason, it never moves and starves between two meals. Nobody thinks real donkeys do this. The example is a test of what choosing is."
whyWrong: "The two claims fit together neatly and lead somewhere absurd. The usual escape is to give up the first claim and let the donkey simply pick, with no reason beyond the fact that it has to pick something. Then the question becomes whether that is a free choice, a random event, or a sign that the case is too perfect to occur in the real world."
resolution:
  - "The history is not what the name suggests. The French philosopher John Buridan lived in the 14th century, but the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says the example is nowhere to be found in his writings. Versions of it go back at least to Aristotle, who in On the Heavens used a man equally hungry and thirsty between food and drink. Wikipedia adds that the 12th-century scholar Al-Ghazali used two identical dates to argue that free will can break such a stalemate."
  - "The best explanation of the name, according to the Stanford Encyclopedia, is that it began as a parody of Buridan's account of free choice, by critics who found it absurd that freedom could consist in inaction. Buridan held that if one option is unclear or uncertain, the will can defer its choice and think further."
  - "Engineers meet a real version of the problem. A circuit that has to settle between two states when its input sits exactly in between can hang for an unpredictable time before it settles. Wikipedia credits Leslie Lamport's 1984 work with stating this as Buridan's principle."
positions:
  - title: Pick arbitrarily
    body: "An agent can choose without a reason that favours one option, because what it needs is to act, not to find a difference. Cost: it means some choices have no reason behind them, which sits badly with the idea that rational choices are always explained."
  - title: The perfect tie never happens
    body: "Real options are never exactly equal, so something always tips the balance, such as small differences in the body or the surroundings. Cost: it dodges the question of what a perfectly balanced agent would do."
  - title: Free will breaks the tie
    body: "A free agent can choose even when reasons are equal. Al-Ghazali used this reasoning. Cost: it needs a kind of freedom that goes beyond what reasons explain, which many reject."
finePrint: The Buridan attribution is a fact about the history of the story, not about his own view, and the sources agree he did not tell it. The example has also been used in arguments about determinism, and those arguments are not covered here.
