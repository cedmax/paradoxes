title: Buridan's ass
titleAccent: ass
chapter: choice-identity
status: disputed
simplification: light
order: 5
written: true
sourcesVerified: false
problem: A hungry donkey stands exactly halfway between two identical piles of hay. Which does it choose?
claimA: "A rational agent only chooses when it has a reason to prefer one option."
claimB: "A rational agent facing a perfect tie should still choose and eat."
verdict: "Picking at random looks sensible, but whether a choice with no reason behind it is rational, or free, is still argued."
whatBreaks: The idea that every choice must be backed by a reason that favours it.
statusNote: "experts disagree on whether rational choice requires a sufficient reason to prefer one option, and so on which claim to give up. Ruth Weintraub argues that it is perfectly reasonable to choose an option even when there is no sufficient reason for it, while Leibniz kept the demand for a sufficient reason and held that tiny unnoticed preferences always break the tie."
setup: "A donkey is equally hungry and equally close to two identical piles of hay. It has no reason to go to one rather than the other. If it only moves for a reason, it never moves and starves between two meals. Nobody thinks real donkeys do this. The example is a test of what choosing is."
whyWrong: "In a perfect tie the first claim says the donkey cannot choose, and the second says it must. Both sound like plain good sense. The usual escape is to give up the first claim and let the donkey simply pick, with no reason beyond the fact that it has to pick something. Then the question becomes whether that is a free choice, a random event, or a sign that the case is too perfect to occur in the real world."
resolution:
  - "The history is not what the name suggests. The French philosopher John Buridan lived in the 14th century, but the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says the example is nowhere to be found in his writings. Versions of it go back at least to Aristotle, who in On the Heavens used a man equally hungry and thirsty between food and drink. Wikipedia adds that the medieval Persian scholar Al-Ghazali, who lived from about 1058 to 1111, used two identical dates to argue that free will can break such a stalemate."
  - "The best explanation of the name, according to the Stanford Encyclopedia, is that it began as a parody of Buridan's account of free choice, by critics who found it absurd that freedom could consist in inaction. Buridan held that if one option is unclear or uncertain, the will can defer its choice and think further."
  - "Engineers meet a real version of the problem. A circuit that has to settle between two states when its input sits exactly in between can hang for an unpredictable time before it settles. Wikipedia credits Leslie Lamport's 1984 work with stating this as Buridan's principle."
positions:
  - title: Pick arbitrarily
    body: "Give up the first claim. An agent can choose without a reason that favours one option, because what it needs is to act, not to find a difference. Ruth Weintraub, writing in 2012, argued that it is perfectly reasonable to choose an option even when there is no sufficient reason for it. Cost: it means some choices have no reason behind them, which sits badly with the idea that rational choices are always explained."
  - title: The perfect tie never happens
    body: "Keep the first claim. Leibniz held, from his principle of sufficient reason, that if preferences were really balanced no decision could be made, but that tiny perceptions below the level of awareness always tip the balance in real life. Cost: it concedes that a perfectly balanced agent would not eat, and only hopes such a case never arises."
  - title: Free will breaks the tie
    body: "Give up the first claim, but not in favour of chance. A free agent can choose even when reasons are equal. Al-Ghazali used this reasoning. Cost: it needs a kind of freedom that goes beyond what reasons explain, which many reject."
finePrint: The Buridan attribution is a fact about the history of the story, not about his own view, and the sources agree he did not tell it. The example has also been used in arguments about determinism, and those arguments are not covered here.
