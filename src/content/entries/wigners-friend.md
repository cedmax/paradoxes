problem: "When a friend in a sealed lab sees a quantum measurement give one result, is that result already a fact for someone outside who has not yet asked?"
claimA: "The lab’s quantum state is a complete account of what is real and evolves without collapse, so before Wigner interacts there is no single observer-independent result."
claimB: "The friend’s measurement already has one unique, observer-independent result before Wigner interacts with the lab."
verdict: "The puzzle is how to reconcile a complete quantum description that evolves without collapse with one unique result shared by all observers. Different quantum state assignments alone do not prove a contradiction; extended versions expose conflicts among further assumptions."
whatBreaks: "The joint assumptions of a complete quantum description, evolution without collapse, and one unique result shared by all observers. Extended versions also test when one observer may adopt another’s certain conclusions."
statusNote: "each main answer faces a serious objection, as set out by its critics. Making minds special puts them outside the laws of physics, changing or adding to quantum theory brings new physics or sits badly with relativity, many-worlds is said to struggle with statistical testing, and making facts relative to each observer is said to weaken the idea of one shared world. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says there is no consensus among physicists or philosophers of physics on what quantum theory says about the world."
setup: "In 1961 the physicist Eugene Wigner, in an essay called “Remarks on the mind-body question”, described a friend working in a closed laboratory. The friend measures a quantum system, such as the spin of a particle, initially in a superposition of two possible results, 0 and 1. A superposition is a quantum combination of alternatives that can produce interference, not just ordinary uncertainty about which result occurred. The friend sees one result. Wigner waits outside. If Wigner treats the whole lab as isolated and its evolution as unitary, meaning without collapse, he assigns a superposition of “the result is 0 and the friend saw 0” and “the result is 1 and the friend saw 1”. When he asks and learns the result, he can update his description. Learning the result does not by itself establish when, or whether, a physical collapse occurred."
whyWrong: "The friend and Wigner can assign different quantum states because they have different information. Frauchiger and Renner explicitly say that this difference alone is not a contradiction. The tension arises if the quantum state is also taken as a complete description of what is real, the isolated lab evolves without collapse, and the friend’s result must be one unique fact independent of every observer. It is the puzzle of [Schrödinger’s cat](schrodingers-cat) with a twist: the thing in the box is a person, who can report what was seen and who uses quantum theory too."
resolution:
  - "Wigner’s own answer in 1961 was that the ordinary quantum rules cannot apply to a conscious being, and that consciousness makes the superposition collapse to one result. According to the physicist Leslie Ballentine, as reported by Wikipedia, Wigner had decided by 1987 that consciousness does not cause collapse. He still thought his chain of reasoning was correct, and treated the argument as a reduction to absurdity, showing that the basic rules of quantum theory need revising."
  - "In 2018 Daniela Frauchiger and Renato Renner published an extended version, with two labs and two outside observers who reason about each other’s results. They showed that three assumptions cannot all hold together: that an agent may apply quantum theory’s certainty predictions to any system, including other agents who use it; that one agent can take over another agent’s certain conclusions as its own; and that a measurement has a single outcome, from the view of whoever made it. Their theorem does not say which assumption to drop. Wikipedia reports that its meaning is highly debated, and that supporters of several interpretations have challenged its validity. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes this family of results as no-go theorems for approaches that try to keep a whole set of assumptions, not as refutations of the existing approaches."
  - "Each existing approach gives up something, and none is widely accepted. The main options are below."
positions:
  - title: "Consciousness causes collapse"
    body: "This is Wigner’s 1961 view: the quantum rules stop applying when a conscious being becomes aware of a result, so the friend’s result was settled when the friend saw it. Cost: it gives the mind an influence on the physical world that the laws of quantum theory do not capture, the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy calls it speculative, and Wigner himself later gave it up."
  - title: "Quantum theory needs changing or adding to"
    body: "Collapse theories add a rule that makes a superposition collapse to one outcome by itself, so a lab would not stay in a superposition. Bohmian mechanics adds definite positions for particles, so the friend’s result is always definite, whatever Wigner’s description says. In their analysis of the extended experiment, Frauchiger and Renner class collapse theories and Bohmian mechanics applied to the whole universe as violating their assumption about quantum certainty predictions. Their classification of hidden-variable theories depends on how those theories are applied. Cost: collapse theories change the basic law of quantum theory and depart from its usual predictions in some conceivable experiments, and Bohmian mechanics needs a special notion of distant events happening “at the same time”, which sits badly with relativity."
  - title: "Every outcome happens"
    body: "The many-worlds view says nothing collapses. The measurement splits the world into branches, and in each branch the friend sees one result. Frauchiger and Renner class this as giving up the assumption of a single outcome. Cost: the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says it is not clear whether such views can make sense of statistical testing when every outcome happens."
  - title: "Facts are relative to the observer"
    body: "Relational quantum mechanics, proposed by Carlo Rovelli in 1996, says a result is a fact relative to the system that interacted with it, so the friend’s account and Wigner’s can both be correct without matching. QBism says a quantum state expresses an agent’s own degrees of belief, so the two can rightly assign different states. Frauchiger and Renner class QBism and most Copenhagen-style readings as giving up the assumption that one agent can take over another’s certain conclusions. The Stanford Encyclopedia also explains the relational reply: comparing observers’ records is itself a physical interaction, and these comparisons can yield consistent records. It discusses later proposals linking observers’ perspectives. Cost: critics question whether relative facts preserve scientific objectivity and confirmation, including Adlam’s worry about observers being confined to their own perspectives; how far the replies answer those objections remains debated."
finePrint: "This is a simplified summary of a technical debate, and these views are only some of those on offer. The physicist David Deutsch developed the thought experiment further in 1985. In 2020 a team led by Bong reported a laboratory version of a scenario with several friends, with single particles of light standing in for the friends. Critics objected: Vaidman said the friend has to be macroscopic, that is, large, and Maudlin said “Nobody thinks a photon is an observer.” The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that Wigner’s view is sometimes wrongly credited to von Neumann as well, who did not require a conscious observer for collapse."
