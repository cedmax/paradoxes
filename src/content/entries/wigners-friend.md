problem: "When a friend in a sealed lab sees a quantum measurement give one result, is that result already a fact for someone outside who has not yet asked?"
claimA: "Quantum theory applies to everything and describes it completely, so for Wigner outside, the lab, friend included, is in a mix of both results until he asks."
claimB: "The friend saw one definite result, and it was a fact before Wigner asked."
verdict: "The thought experiment shows that some natural assumptions about quantum theory, measurement results and how observers’ accounts fit together cannot all hold. Physicists and philosophers disagree about which to give up, and each main option faces serious objections."
whatBreaks: "Whether quantum theory can describe observers, including other people who use it, from the outside, while each observer still sees one definite result."
statusNote: "each main answer faces a serious objection, and the objections come from the discussion of each view, not from the Frauchiger–Renner theorem, which only shows that its assumptions cannot all hold together. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says that making minds special gives consciousness an influence that the laws of quantum theory do not capture, that Bohmian mechanics needs a distinguished notion of distant events happening at the same time, which sits badly with relativity, and that many-worlds has an evidential problem, because it is unclear what probability means when every outcome happens. Collapse theories change the basic law of quantum theory. Emily Adlam (2022) argues that if facts are relative to each observer, observers cannot escape their own perspective, which could undermine how evidence confirms a theory. The Encyclopedia also says there is no consensus among physicists or philosophers of physics on what quantum theory says about the world."
setup: "In 1961 the physicist Eugene Wigner, in an essay called “Remarks on the mind-body question”, described a friend working in a closed laboratory. The friend measures a quantum system, such as the spin of a particle, which quantum theory describes as a mix, called a superposition, of two outcomes, 0 and 1. The friend sees one result. Wigner waits outside. If he applies quantum theory to the whole lab, friend and all, the rules say the lab is in a mix of two states: “the result is 0 and the friend saw 0” and “the result is 1 and the friend saw 1”. Only when Wigner asks the friend does his description settle on one."
whyWrong: "The friend would say the result was settled the moment it was seen. Wigner’s description says it was settled only when he asked. Both used the same theory. The question is when the mix became one result, and whose description is right. It is the puzzle of [Schrödinger’s cat](schrodingers-cat) with a twist: the thing in the box is a person, who can report what was seen and who uses quantum theory too."
resolution:
  - "Wigner’s own answer in 1961 was that the ordinary quantum rules cannot apply to a conscious being, and that consciousness makes the mix collapse to one result. According to the physicist Leslie Ballentine, as reported by Wikipedia, Wigner had decided by 1987 that consciousness does not cause collapse. He still thought his chain of reasoning was correct, and treated the argument as a reduction to absurdity, showing that the basic rules of quantum theory need revising."
  - "In 2018 Daniela Frauchiger and Renato Renner published an extended version, with two labs and two outside observers who reason about each other’s results. They showed that three assumptions cannot all hold together: that quantum theory can be applied to any system, including other agents who use it; that one agent can take over another agent’s certain conclusions as its own; and that a measurement has a single outcome, from the view of whoever made it. Their theorem does not say which assumption to drop. Wikipedia reports that its meaning is highly debated, and that supporters of several interpretations have challenged its validity. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes this family of results as no-go theorems for approaches that try to keep a whole set of assumptions, not as refutations of the existing approaches."
  - "Each existing approach gives up something, and none is widely accepted. The main options are below."
positions:
  - title: "Consciousness causes collapse"
    body: "This is Wigner’s 1961 view: the quantum rules stop applying when a conscious being becomes aware of a result, so the friend’s result was settled when the friend saw it. Cost: it gives the mind an influence on the physical world that the laws of quantum theory do not capture, the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy calls it speculative, and Wigner himself later gave it up."
  - title: "Quantum theory needs changing or adding to"
    body: "Collapse theories add a rule that makes a mix fall into one outcome by itself, so a lab would not stay in a mix. Bohmian mechanics adds definite positions for particles, so the friend’s result is always definite, whatever Wigner’s description says. Frauchiger and Renner class both as giving up the assumption that quantum theory, as it stands, applies to any system. Cost: collapse theories change the basic law of quantum theory and depart from its usual predictions in some conceivable experiments, and Bohmian mechanics needs a special notion of distant events happening “at the same time”, which sits badly with relativity."
  - title: "Every outcome happens"
    body: "The many-worlds view says nothing collapses. The measurement splits the world into branches, and in each branch the friend sees one result. Frauchiger and Renner class this as giving up the assumption of a single outcome. Cost: the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says it is not clear whether such views can make sense of statistical testing when every outcome happens."
  - title: "Facts are relative to the observer"
    body: "Relational quantum mechanics, proposed by Carlo Rovelli in 1996, says a result is a fact relative to the system that interacted with it, so the friend’s account and Wigner’s can both be correct without matching. QBism says a quantum state expresses an agent’s own degrees of belief, so the two can rightly assign different states. Frauchiger and Renner class QBism and most Copenhagen-style readings as giving up the assumption that one agent can take over another’s certain conclusions. Cost: it weakens the idea of one shared world. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that the relational view may seem to imply a kind of solipsism, the idea that only one’s own point of view is real, and reports Adlam’s worry that observers cannot escape their own perspective, which could undermine how evidence confirms a theory."
finePrint: "This is a simplified summary of a technical debate, and these views are only some of those on offer. The physicist David Deutsch developed the thought experiment further in 1985. In 2020 a team led by Bong reported a laboratory version of a scenario with several friends, with single particles of light standing in for the friends. Critics objected: Vaidman said the friend has to be macroscopic, that is, large, and Maudlin said “Nobody thinks a photon is an observer.” The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that Wigner’s view is sometimes wrongly credited to von Neumann as well, who did not require a conscious observer for collapse."
