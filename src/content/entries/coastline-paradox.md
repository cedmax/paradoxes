problem: "How long is the coast of Britain?"
claimA: "A coastline has one true length, independent of the scale used to measure it."
claimB: "Measurements of the same coastline at different scales give different lengths."
verdict: "A coastline’s quoted length depends on the scale and method used to measure it. Endless growth belongs to an idealised mathematical coast."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that a coastline’s quoted length is independent of the measurement scale."
statusNote: "a coastline measurement needs a scale and a method. Endless growth as the ruler shrinks belongs to an ideal mathematical coast, not a claim about a real shore at every possible scale."
setup: "Measure the coast of Britain with a ruler 100 kilometres long and the result is about 2,800 kilometres. Use a ruler 50 kilometres long and it is about 3,400. The shorter ruler follows more bays and headlands. Which answer is the coast’s length?"
whyWrong: "A straight line has a length that finer measurements approach. A coastline has detail over many scales. A shorter ruler follows bends that a longer one skips, so changing the ruler changes the outline being counted."
resolution:
  - "The question needs a scale and a method. Both measurements can be correct for the rules they use. A quoted coastline length is not independent of those rules."
  - "An ideal mathematical coast can have new detail at every scale, making its measured length grow without limit. A real shore stops being a clean line at sufficiently small scales. The idealisation explains the pattern without making that claim about physical shores."
finePrint: "The idea that length grows forever assumes space can be cut into smaller and smaller pieces without end. A real coast stops being a clean line well before that, so this is a mathematical idealisation. In practice we agree on a ruler size and quote the result. The figures for Britain and for the Portugal and Spain border are the ones usually given, and exact numbers depend on the ruler and the method."
