problem: "How long is the coast of Britain?"
claimA: "Every coastline is a real thing, so it must have one true length."
claimB: "Measurements made with shorter rulers follow more detail and give longer answers."
verdict: "A coastline’s quoted length depends on the scale and method used to measure it. Endless growth belongs to an idealised mathematical coast."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that every outline has one fixed length."
statusNote: "the first claim is wrong for rough outlines. Nobody disputes the explanation."
setup: "Measure the coast of Britain with a ruler 100 kilometres long, and you get about 2,800 kilometres. Use a ruler 50 kilometres long and you get about 3,400 kilometres. The shorter ruler can follow more bays and headlands, so the total grows. A smaller ruler follows more detail. For an ideal mathematical coast with detail at every scale, the measured length can keep growing without limit. A real shore is not such a line at arbitrarily small scales."
whyWrong: "We learn that a line has a length, and we expect a coast to be the same. A straight road is. But a coast is rough over many scales. Zoom in on a headland and you find smaller headlands, and smaller ones again inside those. The simple question leaves out which details the measurement should follow."
resolution:
  - "The length of a rough outline is not one number. It depends on the size of the ruler, and in the idealised case it grows without limit as the ruler shrinks. Lewis Fry Richardson noticed this pattern shortly before 1951. He had been studying whether the length of a shared border affects the chance of war, and found that Portugal gave the border with Spain as 987 kilometres while Spain gave it as 1,214 kilometres. The gap between the two figures is the same effect: the shorter the ruler, the longer the measured border."
  - "In 1967 Benoit Mandelbrot published a paper called “How Long Is the Coast of Britain? Statistical Self-Similarity and Fractional Dimension”. It showed how the roughness can be given a number, which he called a fractional dimension. He coined the word “fractal” later, in 1975, and the number is now usually called a fractal dimension. A smooth line has a dimension of 1. Rougher outlines score higher: about 1.02 for the coast of South Africa and about 1.25 for the west coast of Great Britain. The bigger the number, the faster the measured length grows as the ruler shrinks."
  - "So the question “how long is the coast?” is incomplete until you say how long a ruler to use. The useful question is “how long with a ruler of this size?”, and that has a clear answer."
finePrint: "The idea that length grows forever assumes space can be cut into smaller and smaller pieces without end. A real coast stops being a clean line well before that, so this is a mathematical idealisation. In practice we agree on a ruler size and quote the result. The figures for Britain and for the Portugal and Spain border are the ones usually given, and exact numbers depend on the ruler and the method."
