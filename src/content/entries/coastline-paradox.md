problem: "How long is the coast of Britain?"
claimA: "Even an ideal coastline must have a fixed, finite length."
claimB: "Some ideal coastlines have no finite length."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that every outline must have a finite length that finer measurements approach."
statusNote: "an ideal rough outline need not have a finite limiting length. Real coastline measurements need a scale and method, without assuming physical detail continues for ever."
setup: "Measure the coast of Britain with a ruler 100 kilometres long and the result is about 2,800 kilometres. Use a ruler 50 kilometres long and it is about 3,400. The shorter ruler follows more bays and headlands. Which answer is the coast’s length?"
whyWrong: "Finer measurements of a smooth curve, such as a circle, approach one finite length. An ideal coastline can instead have new detail at every scale, making its measured length grow without a finite limit. Different ruler readings alone are not the paradox: the failed expectation is that they must eventually settle on a finite number."
resolution:
  - "An ideal coastline with detail at every scale can lack a finite limiting length. That disproves the claim that every outline must have one. A smooth circle is different: its finer measurements approach a finite circumference."
  - "A real shore stops being a clean line at sufficiently small scales. The idealisation explains why its measurements grow across many scales without claiming that physical detail continues for ever. In practice, “how long is the coast?” needs a stated ruler size and method."
finePrint: "The idea that length grows forever assumes space can be cut into smaller and smaller pieces without end. A real coast stops being a clean line well before that, so this is a mathematical idealisation. In practice a stated ruler size gives a usable measurement. The figures for Britain and for the Portugal and Spain border are the ones usually given, and exact numbers depend on the ruler and the method."
