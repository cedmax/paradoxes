problem: "How many clouds are in the sky when the edge of the cloud is a haze of droplets with no clear boundary?"
claimA: "There is exactly one cloud in the sky."
claimB: "Many slightly different collections of droplets are equally good candidates for being that cloud, and each of them is one."
verdict: "Each response keeps most of what seems obvious and gives up one piece of it, and philosophers disagree about which piece."
whatBreaks: "Philosophers split over what to give up: that there is only one cloud, that any of the candidates is a cloud, or the ordinary idea of counting."
statusNote: "experts agree the puzzle is real and disagree on which assumption to drop. Nihilism, overpopulation, Lewis’s almost-identity view and supervaluationism all have defenders, and none has won out."
setup: "From the ground a cloud looks like one thing with a clear outline. Up close, the edge is a haze. Some droplets are neither clearly part of the cloud nor clearly outside it. Pick any collection of droplets that stretches a little further, or stops a little sooner, and it looks just as good a candidate for being the cloud as the next one. If each candidate is a cloud, the sky holds millions of clouds. If only one is, nothing explains why that one. The philosopher Peter Unger set out the problem in 1980, and Peter Geach raised a similar one at the same time. David Lewis gave an influential treatment in 1993."
whyWrong: "The problem has the same root as the [sorites paradox](sorites-paradox): a word with no sharp edge. There the question is whether a heap is still a heap after you take a grain away. Here the question is how many things there are, which is harder to dodge. Counting seems to need a sharp answer, and the vague edge seems to forbid one."
resolution:
  - "Philosophers have proposed many ways out, and none is widely accepted. Four of the most discussed are set out below. Each keeps most of what seems obvious and gives up one piece of it."
positions:
  - title: "Nihilism"
    body: "There are no clouds, cats or people in the ordinary sense, only particles arranged cloud-wise. Peter Unger argued this. Cost: it denies that clouds, cats and people exist, which most find too much to accept, and it makes ordinary talk about single things hard to explain."
  - title: "Overpopulation"
    body: "There really are many overlapping clouds, and many overlapping people, and ordinary speech picks one out by habit. Williams (2006) endorses this view. Cost: it makes ordinary talk of “the cloud” or one person’s name hard to explain, and it raises a worry about which of the many overlapping people acts freely."
  - title: "Almost one"
    body: "Many candidate clouds exist, but they overlap almost completely, and in everyday counting things that are almost identical count as one. David Lewis defended this in 1993. Cost: counting strictly still gives many clouds, and almost-identity alone cannot handle every case, such as Quine’s and Lewis’s house and mountain examples."
  - title: "Vagueness"
    body: "It is vague which droplets belong to the cloud, so “there is one cloud” comes out true however the edge is drawn, even though no particular way of drawing it is the right one. Supervaluationism, associated with McGee and McLaughlin, takes this line. Cost: it makes some claims about what exists true with no particular true instance, and it sits uneasily with the standard treatment of the sorites paradox."
finePrint: "The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy lists more solutions than these four, including brutalism, relative identity, constitution and vague parthood. The problem is often told with a cat in place of a cloud."
