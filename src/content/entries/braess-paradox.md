problem: Can building a new road make everyone's journey slower?
claimA: More road can only help drivers.
claimB: When every driver picks their own fastest route, a new road can make all of them slower.
verdict: "A shortcut can leave every driver slower when each chooses their own fastest route. The new choices alter congestion on the shared roads."
whatBreaks: The idea that when each person chooses what is best for them, the group ends up as well off as it could be.
statusNote: the first claim is wrong, and the reason is well understood and can be checked with simple sums.
setup: "There are 4,000 drivers and two routes from Start to End. Each route has one road that always takes 45 minutes and one whose time is its car count divided by 100. Split evenly, each route takes 20 + 45 = 65 minutes. Then a free shortcut connects A to B, as shown below."
whyWrong: "The shortcut lets drivers use both roads whose times depend on traffic. If everyone takes it, both carry 4,000 cars: 40 + 0 + 40 = 80 minutes. Switching alone to an old route would take 40 + 45 = 85 minutes, so nobody gains by leaving the slower arrangement."
resolution:
  - "Each driver’s choice changes the congestion facing the others. The final arrangement is stable against one driver switching, but worse for everyone than the old 65-minute routes."
  - "The new road does not physically remove an option. It changes which routes people choose. Closing it can restore the faster arrangement in this example."
finePrint: "The numbers are a standard textbook example, chosen so the sums are easy. The effect only appears when drivers choose selfishly and the roads slow down as more cars use them. A new road does not always cause it, and often a new road helps. It is a surprising result, not a contradiction, so it is a paradox only in the everyday sense."
