problem: "You will be paid a million for intending tonight to drink a mild poison tomorrow, and you need not actually drink it. Can a rational person form that intention?"
claimA: "A rational person cannot form the intention, because they know that once the money is paid they will have no reason to drink."
claimB: "A rational person can form the intention and win the million."
whatBreaks: "Experts split on how intention and reasons fit together: either an intention must rest on reasons to do the act, so the million is out of reach, or it can be rational to intend, and perhaps to do, something only because of the benefits of intending it."
statusNote: "philosophers split three ways. Kavka argued that you cannot intend to act as you have no reason to act, Gauthier and McClennen argue that drinking as planned is rational, and Mele argues that the intention can be rational without any reason to drink. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy reports “no widespread agreement” that it is rational to drink."
setup: "An eccentric billionaire places a vial of toxin in front of you. Drinking it makes you painfully ill for a day but does no lasting harm. He will pay you one million dollars tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink the toxin tomorrow afternoon. A brain scanner will check your intention at midnight. You do not have to drink it. Once the money is in your account, you are free to change your mind. Outside aids such as hypnosis are ruled out. The philosopher Gregory Kavka set out the case in a short paper in the journal Analysis in 1983."
whyWrong: "It seems easy money. You only need to intend, and you would happily drink a day’s illness for a million. But think ahead. By tomorrow afternoon the money is either in your account or not, and drinking changes nothing except making you ill. So you know now that, thinking clearly, you will not drink. And it is hard to intend to do something you are confident you will not do. Trying harder does not help, since the reasons on offer are reasons to have the intention, not reasons to do the act. Kavka’s point is that a rational person, seeing all this clearly, seems unable to win a prize that only requires an intention."
resolution:
  - "The puzzle grew out of Kavka’s work on deterrence theory and mutual assured destruction, the nuclear strategy of threatening a devastating counter-attack. It is often discussed alongside [Newcomb's paradox](newcombs-paradox), since in both cases a reward depends on what is known about your choice in advance. The mechanism here is different. Newcomb’s puzzle is about which choice is rational once the money is in place. The toxin puzzle is about whether the rationality of an intention can come apart from the rationality of the act it is an intention to do."
  - "There is no agreed answer. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy reports that “there is no widespread agreement” that a plausible view of rationality makes it rational to drink. The main positions are below."
positions:
  - title: "You cannot intend it"
    body: "Kavka argued that intentions are dispositions to act based on reasons to act, so you cannot intend to act as you have no reason to act, at least when you have substantial reasons not to. A rational person who sees the situation clearly cannot form the intention and loses the million. Cost: a rational person is then worse placed than someone who could commit themselves, and Alfred Mele argues that Kavka’s general claim cannot be established from the puzzle alone, since the billionaire’s conditions do part of the work."
  - title: "Drink, because you planned to"
    body: "David Gauthier and Edward McClennen hold that acting on a rational plan is itself rational, so a person who rationally intends to drink should drink, and can therefore sincerely intend it. Their view, called resolute choice, says the best way to deliberate includes sticking to plans even when a fresh look would advise otherwise. Cost: the Stanford Encyclopedia notes that for those who find rational drinking completely counter-intuitive, this is a problematic implication, and Ken Levy argues that Gauthier’s argument fails to rule out that it can be rational to make yourself act irrationally."
  - title: "Intend it, without a reason to drink"
    body: "Alfred Mele argues that a person can rationally intend to do something without any reason to do it, so the intention can be formed and the money won. On whether the drinking itself is rational, he gives no single verdict: it depends on what counts as a reason. Cost: it separates intentions from reasons to act, and it leaves open whether the person who drinks, or the one who does not, has acted rationally."
finePrint: "The details of the case are from Kavka’s paper as quoted by Mele and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, including the brain scanner and the exclusion of outside devices such as hypnosis. Michael Bratman, who holds that rationality sometimes calls for sticking to a plan even against current preferences, has also written on the puzzle, but his view is not set out here. The Stanford Encyclopedia notes that a superstitious fear of being jinxed might help someone form the intention, but that this is not a solution one could consciously plan on using."
