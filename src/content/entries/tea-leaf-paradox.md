problem: "Stir a cup of tea. Why do the leaves gather in the centre, not at the edge?"
claimA: "Stirring flings everything outwards, so the tea leaves should end up at the edge of the cup."
claimB: "After stirring, the tea leaves gather at the centre and bottom of the cup."
verdict: "Friction at the bottom sets up a flow that carries the leaves inwards along the bottom."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that the outward push is the same at every depth in the cup."
statusNote: "the first claim is wrong. The outward push is weaker near the bottom, where friction slows the water, so a circling flow carries the leaves to the centre."
setup: "Wikipedia describes the tea leaf paradox as a phenomenon in fluid dynamics. After a cup of tea is stirred, the leaves move to the centre and bottom of the cup, instead of being forced to the edges as might be expected in a spiral centrifuge. The cup is stirred, the water spins, and the spin seems to push everything outwards."
whyWrong: "The outward push is not the same at every depth. Stirring makes the water spin, which creates a centrifugal force outwards. Near the bottom, though, friction slows the water, so the outward force is weaker there than higher up."
resolution:
  - "Wikipedia explains that the difference in force leads to a secondary flow, a helical circulation. The water goes outwards at the top, down along the outer edge, inwards along the bottom, and up again at the centre. The inward flow along the bottom carries the leaves to the centre."
  - "Wikipedia says the correct explanation was first given by James Thomson in 1857, who linked secondary flow to friction at the bottom. Joseph Boussinesq treated the formation of secondary flows in a ring-shaped channel in 1868, and A. Ya. Milovich studied the movement of near-bottom particles in river bends in 1913. Albert Einstein gave a solution in a 1926 paper, in which he explained the erosion of river banks."
finePrint: "Wikipedia reports that the effect has been used to develop a technique to separate red blood cells from plasma, to understand atmospheric pressure systems, and to separate trub in brewing beer. The article does not describe any dispute about the explanation."
