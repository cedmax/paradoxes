problem: "Stir a cup of tea. Why do the leaves gather in the middle of the bottom, not at the edge?"
claimA: "The spin pushes water outwards at every depth, so the leaves should reach the edge."
claimB: "The leaves gather in the middle of the bottom."
verdict: "Friction slows the water at the bottom of the cup, so there the push towards the middle wins, and a slow circulation sweeps the leaves inwards along the bottom."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that the outward push from the spin wins at every depth. In the thin layer slowed by friction at the bottom, the inward push from the water’s pressure is stronger."
statusNote: "the first claim is wrong. Near the bottom, friction slows the water, so the pressure pushing towards the middle outweighs the outward push of the spin there, and the water along the bottom flows inwards, carrying the leaves."
setup: "Stir a cup of tea with loose leaves in it and let it spin. The leaves do not end up at the rim, as they would in a spinning drum that flings things outwards. Instead they collect in a little pile in the middle of the bottom of the cup."
whyWrong: "Spinning water does push outwards. That is why the surface of stirred tea dips in the middle and rises at the rim. The water piled up at the rim presses back, so the pressure in the water rises from the middle to the edge. In the body of the tea the two pushes balance, and each bit of water keeps going round in a circle. But at the bottom, friction with the cup slows the water in a thin boundary layer, a layer next to a surface where friction matters. Water going round more slowly is pushed outwards less, while the pressure from the rim is the same. So in that layer the inward push wins."
resolution:
  - "The result is a secondary flow, a slow circulation on top of the main spin. Water moves inwards along the bottom, rises in the middle, moves outwards near the top and sinks down the sides. The inward flow along the bottom sweeps the leaves into a pile in the middle."
  - "So the first claim is false and the second is true. James Thomson linked this kind of flow to friction on the bottom in 1857, and Joseph Boussinesq treated secondary flows in a ring-shaped channel in 1868. Albert Einstein opened his short 1926 paper on why rivers meander with the stirred teacup. He used the same kind of flow to explain the erosion of river banks: in a bend, the water along the bed moves towards the inner bank. The thin layer of slowed fluid is the same idea that settles [d’Alembert’s paradox](dalemberts-paradox)."
finePrint: "Einstein’s paper, Die Ursache der Mäanderbildung der Flußläufe und des sogenannten Baerschen Gesetzes, appeared in Die Naturwissenschaften in 1926. The same effect has been used to separate red blood cells from blood plasma and to collect solids in the whirlpool stage of brewing beer, and it helps explain air flow in weather systems."
