problem: "Does being born among the first 60 billion or so humans make an early end for humanity more likely?"
claimA: "If you count yourself as a random pick from all humans who will ever live, an early place in the birth order makes a small total far more likely."
claimB: "Your place in the birth order, on its own, is no evidence about how many humans will ever live."
verdict: "The argument has serious defenders and many critics, and the critics disagree among themselves about what is wrong with it."
whatBreaks: "Experts split on whether you may treat yourself as a random pick from all humans who will ever live, past and future, and so read humanity’s future from your own birth rank."
statusNote: "philosophers and physicists still split over whether your birth rank is evidence about how long humanity will last. Wikipedia reports that no consensus has emerged on a solution, and the critics do not agree on a single flaw."
setup: "Number every human in order of birth. In the philosopher John Leslie’s example, about 60 billion humans have been born, so your number is somewhere near 60 billion. Now take two guesses about the future, equally likely before you think about your number. On the first, 60 billion humans will ever live, so the end is close. On the second, 6,000 billion will live. If the first guess is right, a number near 60 billion is just what you would expect. If the second is right, only one human in 100 has a number that low."
whyWrong: "The argument says you should reason as if you were picked at random from all humans who will ever live. Then your low number is 100 times more likely on the first guess than on the second, so the odds swing from even to 100 to 1, about 99 percent, in favour of an early end. The general form goes like this. You are probably not among the first 5 percent of all humans who will ever live. So, with 95 percent confidence, the total is less than 20 times the number born so far, which is 1.2 trillion. With a steady population of 10 billion and lives of 80 years, the remaining 1,140 billion births would take about 9,120 years. Against this, it seems absurd that a plain fact like your own birth order could say when humanity ends, without any data about wars, disease or technology. Both cannot be right."
resolution:
  - "The astrophysicist Brandon Carter proposed the argument in 1983, and the philosopher John Leslie championed it. J. Richard Gott, in a 1993 paper in Nature, and Holger Bech Nielsen, in 1989, arrived at it independently. Gott’s version uses time instead of births. If humans have existed for about 200,000 years and now is a random moment in the species’ lifetime, then with 95 percent confidence humanity will last between about 5,100 years and 7.8 million years more."
  - "Critics attack the argument on different grounds. Dennis Dieks, in 1992, raised what Nick Bostrom later named the self-indication assumption objection: the more humans will ever exist, the more likely it is that you exist at all, and this can cancel the swing towards an early end. Kopf, Krtouš and Page argued against the doomsday argument on this basis in 1994. Robin Hanson argues that “we have good reasons for thinking we are not randomly selected humans from all who will ever live”. Others, such as Carlton Caves, and Andrew Gelman and Christian Robert, say the argument misuses statistics."
  - "Wikipedia reports that no consensus has emerged. Bostrom, who has written widely on the argument, has called it inconclusive, and he also argues against the self-indication assumption. Which group to count yourself a random member of, the reference class, is in Wikipedia’s words “a crucial point of contention”."
positions:
  - title: "The shift is real"
    body: "Leslie argues that whatever your earlier beliefs about humanity’s future, taking your birth rank into account shifts the odds towards an earlier end, which he calls the probability shift. Gott tested his version against how long things such as the Berlin Wall and Broadway plays lasted. Cost: the answer depends on which group you count yourself a random member of, humans, conscious beings or something else, and there is no agreed way to choose it."
  - title: "Count the extra observers"
    body: "Following Dieks, accept the self-indication assumption: your own existence is more likely if more people ever exist. This can cancel the shift. Matthew Adelstein defends it, arguing that every alternative makes the doomsday argument sound. Cost: Bostrom argues that the same assumption would let pure armchair reasoning settle the scientific question of whether the universe is infinite, and Bostrom and Milan Ćirković argued against Ken Olum’s defence of it."
  - title: "The statistics are misused"
    body: "Caves argues that Gott’s rule gives answers few would bet on: a woman celebrating her 50th birthday would get even odds of living past 100. Gelman and Robert argue that the argument confuses two kinds of statistical interval, and Ben O’Neill argues it is invalid under standard probability theory. Cost: Wikipedia notes that more careful versions, such as Bostrom’s, which require that knowing your birth rank says nothing by itself about the total, are not shown to be implausible by Caves’s example, so these objections do not reach every version."
finePrint: "The argument does not say humanity cannot last for ever, only that an early end is more likely than you would otherwise think. The numbers come from the sources and were recomputed: 60 billion divided by 5 percent is 1.2 trillion; 10 billion people living 80 years means 125 million births a year, and 1,140 billion births at that rate take 9,120 years; Gott’s range is 200,000 years divided by 39 (about 5,100) and multiplied by 39 (7.8 million), since the middle 95 percent of a lifetime leaves between a 39th and 39 times as much still to come. The two-guess example is the one Wikipedia uses. The 60 billion figure is the one Leslie uses to explain the argument, not a current count. The Population Reference Bureau estimated that roughly 117 billion people had ever been born by 2022. The 95 percent prediction depends on treating birth rank as a random sample. The same question, how to reason about where you are among people or moments you cannot tell apart, drives the [Sleeping Beauty problem](sleeping-beauty-problem), where the self-sampling assumption’s answer also depends on the reference class."
