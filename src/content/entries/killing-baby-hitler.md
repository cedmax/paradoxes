problem: "If a time traveller killed Hitler as a baby, what would later send the traveller back to do it?"
claimA: "The past can be changed: a traveller could kill the infant Hitler, and history would then run differently."
claimB: "The past cannot be changed: Hitler grew up and died in 1945, so any traveller sent to kill him as a baby must fail."
verdict: "Philosophers disagree on whether a visit to the past could change it, and no answer is settled."
whatBreaks: "The contested choice: give up the idea that a traveller can change the past, or give up the idea that there is only one past to change."
statusNote: "philosophers split on whether the past could be changed at all, the same split as in the [grandfather paradox](grandfather-paradox). No answer can be tested, because no time machine is known to be possible."
setup: "Killing baby Hitler is a thought experiment: travel back in time and kill Adolf Hitler as an infant, before he can take power. Stories using the idea date back to the Second World War, with examples in the magazines Weird Tales (1941) and Astounding Science Fiction (1942). The question has an ethical side, which this entry does not cover and on which it takes no side. It also has a logical side, sometimes called the “Hitler’s murder paradox”, which is a version of the [grandfather paradox](grandfather-paradox)."
whyWrong: "Suppose the traveller succeeds. Hitler never takes power, so the events that followed never happen. But those events are the traveller’s reason for going back. Without them, nobody has a reason to make the trip, so nobody kills the baby, so Hitler does take power, and the reason returns. The story undoes itself. In the grandfather paradox the trip erases the traveller’s own birth. Here it erases only the motive for the trip. The question underneath is the same: can a visitor to the past change what happened?"
resolution:
  - "There is no agreed answer. One plain fact frames the debate. Hitler killed himself in 1945, so in the history that actually happened, no time traveller killed him as a baby. Those who hold that there is only one past take this to settle what any traveller could do. Others argue that a richer model of time, or a second universe, would let the past be different without contradiction."
  - "Each camp answers this puzzle the same way it answers the [grandfather paradox](grandfather-paradox). The positions below are those in the philosophy of time travel. None of the sources treats the Hitler case as needing a different answer."
positions:
  - title: "Only one past, which cannot be changed"
    body: "Whatever a traveller does in the past was always part of it. The philosopher David Lewis argued in 1976 that a traveller who tries to change it fails “for some commonplace reason”, such as a jammed gun. So a traveller sent to kill the baby fails, Hitler grows up, and the reason for the trip stays in place. Cost: Horwich (1987) argued that the repeated, uncaused coincidences needed would make time travel improbable, and Riggs and Ismael argued that a long run of failures is left unexplained."
  - title: "A second dimension of time"
    body: "Some models add a second time dimension, often called hypertime, in which one version of the past can be replaced by another. Meiland proposed such a model in 1974, and Goddu (2003) and van Inwagen (2010) argue that, in particular hypertime models, the past really is changed. Cost: Smith (1997, 2015) argues that this is not changing the past but “avoiding the past”, leaving the original untouched while travelling to a different version of it."
  - title: "The trip leads to a different universe"
    body: "If the traveller arrives in a parallel universe, killing the baby there removes Hitler from that universe only, and the original history goes on. Deutsch and Lockwood described scenarios of this kind in 1994. The astrophysicist Brian Koberlein argued in 2015 that killing baby Hitler would only create a parallel universe without Hitler in it, and would be inconsequential at best. Cost: the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy asks whether travel to another version of the past is time travel at all, and Stephen Hawking argued that a time traveller should expect to experience a single self-consistent history."
finePrint: "No time machine is known to be possible. This entry is filed as a variant of the grandfather paradox because the same step is at issue in both: the assumption that a visitor to the past can change it. The Wikipedia article on temporal paradoxes also lists it as a variant. The ethical arguments, a 2015 New York Times reader poll and the many works of fiction on the theme are not covered here."
