problem: Should a tolerant society tolerate people who want to end tolerance?
claimA: "The intolerant should be tolerated unless they pose a real danger or incite violence."
claimB: "A tolerant society may claim the right to suppress movements that preach intolerance, by force if necessary."
verdict: "Almost everyone accepts some limit on tolerance. They split on where it starts."
whatBreaks: "Whether tolerance should stop only at real danger and incitement, or earlier, at movements that preach intolerance. Experts split on which."
statusNote: "experts split on where the limit of tolerance starts. Rawls would restrict the intolerant only when security and the institutions of liberty are in danger, and the free-speech view limits speech only where incitement to violence or other illegal activity is explicit. Popper’s note claims the right to suppress movements that preach intolerance, if necessary even by force, while calling suppression “most unwise” as long as argument and public opinion keep them in check."
setup: "Being tolerant means putting up with views or ways of life you object to. So what about those who would not return the favour? If they are allowed to speak and organise freely, and then gain power, they may use that freedom to shut tolerance down. If they are stopped, the tolerant society has stopped someone, and that looks like intolerance."
whyWrong: "The puzzle does not need strange logic. It comes from two aims held at once: a society that puts up with a wide range of views, and a society that lasts. Taken to the letter, the first can undo the second. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that most classical texts on toleration treat it as a matter of give and take, so those who are intolerant need not be tolerated. It warns that the slogan “no toleration of the intolerant” is “not just vacuous but potentially dangerous”, because calling a group intolerant is all too often itself the result of one-sidedness and intolerance. It also says that tolerance can only be a virtue if a line can be drawn between those who reject toleration as a norm and those who refuse to tolerate that rejection, and if the limits can be drawn in a non-arbitrary, justifiable way."
resolution:
  - "The philosopher Karl Popper described the problem in The Open Society and Its Enemies (1945). It appears in an endnote to chapter 7 of the first volume, not in the main text, and the note calls it “the paradox of tolerance”. He wrote that “unlimited tolerance must lead to the disappearance of tolerance”, and that a tolerant society must be prepared to defend itself against the intolerant."
  - "What he said next matters. He wrote that he did not mean that intolerant philosophies should always be suppressed. As long as they can be countered by rational argument and kept in check by public opinion, suppression “would certainly be most unwise”. But, he went on, in the wording of the fourth and fifth editions, “we should claim the right to suppress them if necessary even by force; for it may easily turn out that they are not prepared to meet us on the level of rational argument, but begin by denouncing all argument”. The first edition says only “we should claim the right even to suppress them”, without “if necessary even by force”. The refusal to argue is given there as a reason why suppression may turn out to be needed."
  - "He then went further: “We should therefore claim, in the name of tolerance, the right not to tolerate the intolerant. We should claim that any movement preaching intolerance places itself outside the law, and we should consider incitement to intolerance and persecution as criminal”, in the same way as incitement to murder or kidnapping."
  - "Wikipedia notes that several post-war European democracies have built related ideas into law. Germany’s Basic Law lets its Federal Constitutional Court ban parties that seek to undermine or abolish the free democratic basic order, and similar provisions elsewhere are often called “militant democracy”. The United States, by contrast, protects intolerant speech unless incitement to violence or other illegal activity is made explicit."
positions:
  - title: Claim the right not to tolerate the intolerant
    body: "Popper’s note. Answer intolerant views with argument while that works, since suppressing them then would be “most unwise”, but claim the right to suppress them if necessary, and treat movements that preach intolerance as outside the law. Cost: someone has to decide which views and movements count as intolerant, and that power can be abused to silence legitimate dissent."
  - title: Tolerate the intolerant except in real danger
    body: "John Rawls, in A Theory of Justice (1971), argued that a just society should tolerate the intolerant, and that the freedom of an intolerant sect “should be restricted only when the tolerant sincerely and with reason believe that their own security and that of the institutions of liberty are in danger”. Cost: intolerant movements may grow freely until that point is reached, and the judgement that it has been reached is still a human one."
  - title: Answer bad speech with more speech
    body: "Tolerate intolerant speech, limiting it only where incitement to violence or other illegal activity is made explicit. This is closer to the legal position in the United States, as Wikipedia describes it, while many European countries restrict more. Cost: intolerant movements can win influence and manipulate democratic processes without ever crossing the line into explicit incitement."
finePrint: "These are three positions out of many. Wikipedia also covers further views, including Michael Walzer’s claim that most minority religious groups who benefit from tolerance are themselves intolerant, at least in some respects, and Herbert Marcuse’s argument that tolerance can be a tool of the powerful that lets the status quo persist. Whether a given group or opinion counts as intolerant is itself disputed, and this entry takes no side on any current case."
