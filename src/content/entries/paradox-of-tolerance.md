problem: Should a tolerant society tolerate people who want to end tolerance?
claimA: "A tolerant society should tolerate the intolerant until they pose a real danger to its freedoms."
claimB: "A tolerant society should act against movements that preach intolerance before they reach that point."
verdict: "Most thinkers accept that tolerance has some limit. They split over whether the limit starts at preaching intolerance or only at real danger."
whatBreaks: "The idea that tolerance can be a rule with no exceptions. What is left is where the exception begins."
statusNote: "the views covered here, Popper’s, Rawls’s and the free-speech view, all accept some limit but place it at different points, and none has won general agreement."
setup: "Being tolerant means putting up with views or ways of life you object to. So what about those who would not return the favour? If they are allowed to speak and organise freely, and then gain power, they may use that freedom to shut tolerance down. If they are stopped, the tolerant society has stopped someone, and that looks like intolerance."
whyWrong: "The puzzle does not need strange logic. It comes from two things we want at once. We want a society that puts up with a wide range of views, and we want that society to last. Taken to the letter, the first can undo the second. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that most classical texts on toleration treat it as a matter of give and take, so those who are intolerant need not be tolerated. It then warns that the slogan “no toleration of the intolerant” is “not just vacuous but potentially dangerous”, because calling a group intolerant is all too often itself the result of one-sidedness and intolerance."
resolution:
  - "The philosopher Karl Popper described the problem in The Open Society and Its Enemies (1945). It appears in an endnote to chapter 7 of the first volume, not in the main text, and the note calls it “the paradox of tolerance”. He wrote that “unlimited tolerance must lead to the disappearance of tolerance”, and that a tolerant society must be prepared to defend itself against the intolerant."
  - "What he said next matters. He wrote that he did not mean that intolerant philosophies should always be suppressed. As long as they can be countered by rational argument and kept in check by public opinion, suppression “would certainly be most unwise”. But, he went on, “we should claim the right to suppress them if necessary even by force; for it may easily turn out that they are not prepared to meet us on the level of rational argument, but begin by denouncing all argument”. The refusal to argue is given there as a reason why force may turn out to be needed."
  - "He then went further: “We should claim that any movement preaching intolerance places itself outside the law and we should consider incitement to intolerance and persecution as criminal”, in the same way as incitement to murder or kidnapping."
  - "How far the note reaches is itself argued over, and it is read and used in different ways. Some readers stress its warning that suppression is unwise while argument still works. Others stress the sentences about the right to suppress and placing intolerant movements outside the law, and Wikipedia notes that several European democracies have built similar ideas into laws that allow anti-democratic parties to be banned. The note does not say who decides which movements count, or how."
positions:
  - title: Claim the right not to tolerate the intolerant
    body: "Popper’s note. Answer intolerant views with argument while that works, but claim the right to suppress movements that preach intolerance, by force if necessary. Cost: someone has to decide which movements count, and that power can be misused."
  - title: Tolerate nearly everyone
    body: "John Rawls, in A Theory of Justice (1971), argued that a just society should tolerate the intolerant, and may restrict them only when the tolerant sincerely and with reason believe their own security and their free institutions are in danger. Cost: it can mean waiting until the danger is real, and the judgement about danger is still a human one."
  - title: Answer bad speech with more speech
    body: "Limit speech only for things like explicit incitement to violence. This is closer to the legal position in the United States, as Wikipedia describes it, while many European countries restrict more. Cost: critics say intolerant movements can win influence without breaking those limits."
finePrint: These are three positions out of many. Wikipedia also covers further views, including Michael Walzer's point that many groups who benefit from tolerance are themselves intolerant, and Herbert Marcuse's argument that supposedly neutral tolerance can protect existing power. Whether a given group or opinion counts as intolerant is itself disputed, and this entry takes no side on any current case.
