problem: Should a tolerant society tolerate people who want to end tolerance?
claimA: A tolerant society should tolerate everyone.
claimB: A society that tolerates those who would destroy tolerance may lose it.
verdict: Most thinkers accept some limit, but disagree about where it lies.
whatBreaks: The idea that tolerance can be a rule with no exceptions.
statusNote: experts agree it is a real puzzle but disagree on where the limit sits and who should draw it.
setup: "Being tolerant means putting up with views or ways of life you object to. So what about those who would not return the favour? If they are allowed to speak and organise freely, and then gain power, they may use that freedom to shut tolerance down. If they are stopped, the tolerant society has stopped someone, and that looks like intolerance."
whyWrong: "The puzzle does not need strange logic. It comes from two things we want at once. We want a society that puts up with a wide range of views, and we want that society to last. Taken to the letter, the first can undo the second. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy puts it this way: toleration is a matter of give and take, so those who are intolerant need not be tolerated, but calling a group intolerant is itself often one-sided."
resolution:
  - 'The philosopher Karl Popper named the problem in The Open Society and Its Enemies (1945). It appears in an endnote to chapter 7 of the first volume, not in the main text. He wrote that "unlimited tolerance must lead to the disappearance of tolerance", and that a tolerant society must be prepared to defend itself.'
  - 'What he said next matters. He wrote that he did not mean that intolerant views should always be suppressed. As long as they can be met with rational argument and held in check by public opinion, suppression would be "most unwise". He claimed the right to suppress them, if necessary by force, when they refuse to argue at all and answer arguments with violence. He also said that incitement to intolerance and persecution should be treated as a crime, in the way incitement to murder is.'
  - "So Popper did not say that anyone who holds intolerant opinions may be silenced. His test was about rejecting argument and turning to force, and he left unsettled who applies the test and how."
positions:
  - title: Defend tolerance, when argument fails
    body: "Popper's view. Tolerate intolerant views while they can be answered with argument, but keep the right to act when a movement rejects argument and turns to force. Cost: someone has to decide when that point is reached, and that power can be misused."
  - title: Tolerate nearly everyone
    body: "John Rawls, in A Theory of Justice (1971), argued that a just society should tolerate the intolerant, and may restrict them only when the tolerant sincerely and with reason believe their own security and their free institutions are in danger. Cost: it can mean waiting until the danger is real, and the judgement about danger is still a human one."
  - title: Answer bad speech with more speech
    body: "Limit speech only for things like explicit incitement to violence. This is closer to the legal position in the United States, as Wikipedia describes it, while many European countries restrict more. Cost: critics say intolerant movements can win influence without breaking those limits."
finePrint: These are three positions out of many. Wikipedia also covers further views, including Michael Walzer's point that many groups who benefit from tolerance are themselves intolerant, and Herbert Marcuse's argument that supposedly neutral tolerance can protect existing power. Whether a given group or opinion counts as intolerant is itself disputed, and this entry takes no side on any current case.
