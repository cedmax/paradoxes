---
title: "Lottery paradox"
titleAccent: "paradox"
chapter: belief
status: disputed
simplification: light
order: 2
written: true
problem: "If you believe every ticket will lose, do you believe that no ticket will win?"
claimA: "It is reasonable to believe something that is almost certainly true."
claimB: "It is not reasonable to believe things that you know cannot all be true together."
verdict: "Philosophers disagree on which rule to give up."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that reasonable beliefs always fit together without contradiction."
statusNote: "experts agree it is a real puzzle but disagree on which claim to give up."
setup: "A fair lottery has 1,000 tickets and exactly one winner. Take any single ticket. It is almost certain to lose, so it seems reasonable to believe it will lose. The same goes for every other ticket. Add these beliefs up and you get: ticket 1 will lose, ticket 2 will lose, and so on to ticket 1,000. That means no ticket will win. But you know one ticket will win. You now believe something you know is false."
whyWrong: "The puzzle uses three rules that each sound sensible. First, you may believe what is very likely. Second, you should not hold beliefs that cannot all be true together. Third, if you are right to believe two things, you are right to believe them both at once. Together they lead to a contradiction, so one of them has to go."
resolution:
  - "The paradox was formulated by the philosopher Henry Kyburg in 1959 and first appeared in print in his 1961 book. Kyburg gave up the third rule. On his view you can reasonably believe each ticket will lose, without being forced to believe they all will."
  - "Others keep the third rule and give up the first. Being very likely, they say, is not enough for belief. No agreed answer exists."
positions:
  - title: Beliefs do not have to add up
    body: "Each belief is fine on its own, and you need not join them together. This is Kyburg's view. Cost: you can end up with a set of beliefs that cannot all be true, which many people find unacceptable."
  - title: High odds are not enough
    body: "Being very likely does not make a belief reasonable. You can say it is very probable that ticket 1 will lose without believing it. Cost: it is hard to say when high odds are enough, since we believe many things on far less than certainty."
  - title: Weaken the rules
    body: "Keep all three rules in a more careful form. Logicians have built formal systems for reasoning that can be withdrawn as new information arrives. Cost: the systems are technical, and the rules no longer look simple."
finePrint: "The rules above are simplified from the versions in the literature, and the 1,000 ticket figure is only an example, since the argument works for any large lottery. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that people resist saying they know a ticket will lose, even at very long odds, and that this is part of why the puzzle matters."
