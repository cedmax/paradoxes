title: "Lottery paradox"
titleAccent: "paradox"
chapter: belief
status: disputed
simplification: light
order: 2
written: true
problem: "If you believe every ticket will lose, do you believe that no ticket will win?"
claimA: "It is reasonable to believe each ticket will lose, since each almost certainly will."
claimB: "If it is reasonable to believe each of several things, it is reasonable to believe them all together."
verdict: "Philosophers disagree on which rule to give up."
whatBreaks: "Either the high-odds rule or the combining rule has to go, and experts split on which."
statusNote: "experts agree the three rules clash, and the main camps split between giving up the high-odds rule and giving up the combining rule."
setup: "A fair lottery has 1,000 tickets and exactly one winner. Take any single ticket. It is almost certain to lose, so it seems reasonable to believe it will lose. The same goes for every other ticket. Add these beliefs up and you get: ticket 1 will lose, ticket 2 will lose, and so on to ticket 1,000. That means no ticket will win. But you know one ticket will win. You now believe something you know is false."
whyWrong: "The puzzle uses three rules that each sound sensible. The high-odds rule: you may believe what is very likely. The combining rule: if you are right to believe each of several things, you are right to believe them all together. The no-contradiction rule: never believe something you know is a contradiction. The first two rules do not clash on their own. They clash only once you add that exactly one ticket will win, and the no-contradiction rule. The first rule lets you believe of each ticket that it will lose. The second joins those beliefs, and your belief that one ticket wins, into a single belief that no ticket wins and one does. The third forbids exactly that. One of the three has to go."
resolution:
  - "The paradox was formulated by the philosopher Henry Kyburg in 1959 and first appeared in print in his 1961 book. Kyburg kept the high-odds rule and the no-contradiction rule and gave up the combining rule. On his view you can reasonably believe of each ticket that it will lose without believing that they all will. You end up with beliefs that cannot all be true together, but you never believe the contradiction itself. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes this as tolerating joint inconsistency in order to avoid believing contradictions."
  - "According to the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, many others take the lesson to be that the high-odds rule cannot stand. Some, such as Igor Douven, keep a weaker version: high odds are enough for belief unless some special condition rules it out. An example due to Kevin Korb suggests that such conditions end up ruling out belief in anything short of certainty. No agreed answer exists."
positions:
  - title: "Beliefs need not combine"
    body: "Drop the combining rule. Each belief is fine on its own, and you need not join them together, so you never believe the contradiction itself. This is Kyburg's view. Cost: you can rationally hold beliefs that cannot all be true together, and then it is unclear how a paradox could ever force anyone to change their mind."
  - title: "High odds are not enough"
    body: "Being very likely does not make a belief reasonable. You can say it is very probable that ticket 1 will lose without believing it will. Cost: almost no belief is certain, so this threatens to leave very little that anyone may believe outright rather than merely think likely."
finePrint: "The rules above are simplified from the versions in the literature, and the 1,000 ticket figure is only an example, since the argument works for any large lottery. Wikipedia also lists formal systems that keep weaker versions of the rules, including logics of belief, non-monotonic logic, modal logic, first-order probability and paraconsistent logic. A related puzzle concerns knowledge rather than belief: the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that, even at very long odds, people are reluctant to say that anyone knows their ticket will lose."
