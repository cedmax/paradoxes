---
title: "Lottery paradox"
titleAccent: "paradox"
chapter: belief
status: disputed
simplification: light
order: 2
written: true
problem: "If you believe every ticket will lose, do you believe that no ticket will win?"
claimA: "It is reasonable to believe each ticket will lose, since each almost certainly will."
claimB: "If it is reasonable to believe each of several things, it is reasonable to believe them all together."
verdict: "Philosophers disagree on which rule to give up."
whatBreaks: "The high-odds rule, the combining rule and the no-contradiction rule cannot all stand. Both main camps keep the no-contradiction rule, and experts split on whether to give up the high-odds rule or the combining rule."
statusNote: "experts agree the three rules clash, but disagree on whether to give up the high-odds rule or the combining rule."
setup: "A fair lottery has 1,000 tickets and exactly one winner. Take any single ticket. It is almost certain to lose, so it seems reasonable to believe it will lose. The same goes for every other ticket. Add these beliefs up and you get: ticket 1 will lose, ticket 2 will lose, and so on to ticket 1,000. That means no ticket will win. But you know one ticket will win. You now believe something you know is false."
whyWrong: "The puzzle uses three rules that each sound sensible. The high-odds rule: you may believe what is very likely. The combining rule: if you are right to believe each of several things, you are right to believe them all together. The no-contradiction rule: never believe something you know is a contradiction. The first rule lets you believe of each ticket that it will lose. The second joins those beliefs, and your belief that one ticket wins, into a single belief that no ticket wins and one does. The third forbids exactly that. One of the three has to go."
resolution:
  - "The paradox was formulated by the philosopher Henry Kyburg in 1959 and first appeared in print in his 1961 book. Kyburg kept the high-odds rule and the no-contradiction rule and gave up the combining rule. On his view you can reasonably believe of each ticket that it will lose without believing that they all will. You end up with beliefs that cannot all be true together, but you never believe the contradiction itself. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes this as tolerating joint inconsistency in order to avoid believing contradictions."
  - "Orthodox probabilists keep the combining rule and the no-contradiction rule and give up the high-odds rule instead. For them, the reasonable belief about a very likely event is just that it is very likely, not that it is true. No agreed answer exists."
positions:
  - title: "Beliefs do not have to combine"
    body: "Drop the combining rule. Each belief is fine on its own, and you need not join them together, so you never believe the contradiction itself. This is Kyburg's view. Cost: you can rationally hold beliefs that cannot all be true together, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy asks how a paradox could ever force anyone to change their mind if that is allowed."
  - title: "High odds are not enough"
    body: "Being very likely does not make a belief reasonable. You can say it is very probable that ticket 1 will lose without believing it will. Cost: almost nothing we believe is certain, so this threatens to leave very little that we may believe outright rather than merely think likely."
  - title: "Weaken the rules"
    body: "Keep all three rules in a more careful form. Logicians and computer scientists have built several formal systems for this, including logics of belief, logics that tolerate some contradictions without collapsing (paraconsistent logic), logics where conclusions can be withdrawn as new information arrives (non-monotonic logic), modal logics, and first-order probability. Cost: the rules survive only in weaker, more technical forms, and there are many rival systems to choose between."
finePrint: "The rules above are simplified from the versions in the literature, and the 1,000 ticket figure is only an example, since the argument works for any large lottery. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that people resist saying they know a ticket will lose, even at very long odds, and that this is part of why the puzzle matters."
