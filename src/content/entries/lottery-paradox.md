title: "Lottery paradox"
titleAccent: "paradox"

simplification: light
order: 40
written: true
problem: "If you believe every ticket will lose, do you believe that no ticket will win?"
claimA: "It is reasonable to believe each ticket will lose, since each almost certainly will."
claimB: "If it is reasonable to believe each of several things, it is reasonable to believe them all together."
verdict: "High odds seem to justify each belief, but combining those beliefs gives a conclusion known to be false. Philosophers disagree on whether to give up the high-odds rule or the combining rule."
whatBreaks: "Either the high-odds rule or the combining rule has to go, and experts split on which."
statusNote: "experts agree the three rules clash, and the main camps split between giving up the high-odds rule and giving up the combining rule."
setup: "A fair lottery has 1,000 tickets and exactly one winner. Any particular ticket is almost certain to lose. It seems reasonable to believe it will. The same reasoning applies to every ticket, yet believing all those losses together means believing there will be no winner."
whyWrong: "High odds make each belief look reasonable. Combining reasonable beliefs also looks safe. But the combined belief conflicts with something known: exactly one ticket will win. The puzzle needs that consistency requirement as well as the two tempting rules."
resolution:
  - "One answer permits each belief separately but refuses to combine them into “all tickets will lose”. Another says a very high probability is not enough for outright belief."
  - "The arithmetic is agreed. The dispute is about what it is reasonable to believe, not the chance of any ticket winning."
positions:
  - title: "Beliefs need not combine"
    body: "Kyburg allows each belief separately without requiring the combined belief. Cost: the reasonable beliefs still cannot all be true together, raising questions about when inconsistency should force a change."
  - title: "High odds are not enough"
    body: "Treat each loss as very probable without believing it outright. Cost: much ordinary belief falls short of certainty, so a stricter rule risks excluding too much."
  - title: "Add an exception"
    body: "Allow belief from high odds unless a special condition blocks it, as some accounts propose for lotteries. Cost: finding a principled exception that does not spread to ordinary beliefs is difficult."
finePrint: "The rules above are simplified from the versions in the literature, and the 1,000 ticket figure is only an example, since the argument works for any large lottery. Wikipedia also lists formal systems that keep weaker versions of the rules, including logics of belief, non-monotonic logic, modal logic, first-order probability and paraconsistent logic. A related puzzle concerns knowledge rather than belief: the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that, even at very long odds, people are reluctant to say that anyone knows their ticket will lose."
