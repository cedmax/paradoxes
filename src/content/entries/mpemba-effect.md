problem: "Can hot water freeze sooner than cooler water put in the same freezer?"
claimA: "A hotter liquid has further to cool, so it freezes later than a cooler one."
claimB: "Under some conditions, a hotter liquid freezes sooner than a cooler one."
verdict: "Physicists still disagree on how the effect is defined, whether it is real in water, and why."
whatBreaks: "Whether to give up the idea that hotter always freezes later, or to doubt that the effect is real in water."
statusNote: "physicists remain divided on the effect’s precise definition and on its causes, and studies of water give mixed results."
setup: "The effect is named after Erasto Mpemba, a Tanzanian schoolboy. In 1963, in a cookery class at Magamba Secondary School, he noticed that a hot ice cream mixture froze before a cold one. Later, at Mkwawa Secondary School, he asked the visiting physicist Denis Osborne why, of two containers of water at 35 °C and 100 °C put in a freezer, the one that started at 100 °C froze first. Osborne first thought the student was mistaken, but he tried it and, according to Wikipedia, was amused to confirm Mpemba’s result. They published together in 1969. Wikipedia notes that earlier writers, including Aristotle, Francis Bacon and René Descartes, described similar effects of heat on freezing."
whyWrong: "The first claim looks like plain common sense. Yet it has been reported in several kinds of systems, and the dispute is partly about definition. Wikipedia quotes Monwhea Jeng’s version for water: there exists a set of starting conditions and two temperatures such that, of two otherwise identical bodies of water, the hot one freezes first. Even that does not say whether “frozen” means a surface layer of ice or solid all the way through."
resolution:
  - "Wikipedia reports that the effect has been seen in ice cream and water and later in other colloids, and has also been studied in magnetic alloys, nanomechanical systems and quantum systems. It adds that the effect is not seen in all environments and set-ups, and that some water experiments found no reproducible effect."
positions:
  - title: It is real in water, and supercooling helps explain it
    body: "Modern studies with well-understood freezers have seen the effect where water supercools before freezing, since water that starts out cooler tends to supercool further. David Auerbach saw it in 1995. John Bechhoefer described a way to reproduce it in 2021, and a 2024 study by Argelia Ortega and colleagues found that small hot drops froze faster than cold ones. Wikipedia also lists proposed causes, including changes in hydrogen bonding, dissolved solids and gases, convection and frost. Cost: Wikipedia says the effect is not observed in every set-up, and that Auerbach found considerable random variation in when freezing began."
  - title: Meaningful observations in water are not shown
    body: "Many things must be controlled: how freezing time is defined, the water’s type and dissolved gas, the container, the cooling method and the freezer temperature. Philip Ball made this point in 2006. In 2016, Burridge and Linden, studying the time for water to reach 0 °C, found that large early effects had not been replicated and that small effects could come from thermometer positions. They concluded that “there is no evidence to support meaningful observations of the Mpemba effect.” Cost: other careful studies, such as the 2024 drop experiments, report that hot samples did freeze first, and Wikipedia notes that Burridge and Linden measured a different quantity, the time to reach 0 °C without freezing."
  - title: It is a general feature of cooling systems
    body: "A hotter system can reach equilibrium faster than a colder one after both are cooled to the same low temperature. This has been shown in a single-particle colloid by Avinash Kumar and Bechhoefer in 2020, including a “strong” version where cooling is exponentially faster, and later in quantum systems. Cost: these are different systems from water in a freezer. Wikipedia notes that the definition used in theoretical studies varies, so comparing experiments is hard."
