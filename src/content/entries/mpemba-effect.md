problem: "Can hot water freeze sooner than cooler water put in the same freezer?"
claimA: "Under the same conditions, water that starts out hotter does not freeze first in any meaningful, reproducible way."
claimB: "Under some conditions, water that starts out hotter really does freeze before otherwise identical water that starts out cooler."
verdict: "Scientists still disagree on whether hot water really freezes first in a reproducible way, and what would cause it. A related speed-up in cooling has been shown in other systems."
whatBreaks: "The contested choice is between accepting that hot water can genuinely freeze first under some conditions, and treating the reported cases in water as errors of measurement or chance."
statusNote: "careful experiments on water disagree, and reviews describe the controversy as still open. Researchers also differ on what “freezes first” should mean: ice first appearing, the water reaching 0 °C, or the water freezing solid."
setup: "In 1963 Erasto Mpemba, a pupil at Magamba Secondary School in Tanganyika, now part of Tanzania, was making ice cream in a cookery class. He put his mixture in the freezer while it was still hot, and it froze before the cold mixtures of other pupils. Later, at Mkwawa Secondary School in Iringa, he asked a visiting physicist, Denis Osborne, why water that started at 100 °C would freeze before water that started at 35 °C. Osborne doubted it at first, but experiments he arranged found the same, and the two published their results in the journal Physics Education in 1969. The idea is much older. Aristotle wrote that water that has been warmed beforehand freezes more quickly. Francis Bacon, in his Novum Organum of 1620, wrote that slightly warm water freezes more easily than very cold water. René Descartes, in Les Météores of 1637, wrote that water kept hot over a fire for a long time freezes faster than other water."
whyWrong: "Common sense says hot water has further to go, so it should take longer. Hot water does not have to cool faster for it to freeze first, though. Several effects have been proposed. Hot water evaporates more. It holds less dissolved gas, which may change the currents that carry heat through it. A hot container may melt through a layer of frost that would otherwise insulate it from the freezer. And water can stay liquid below 0 °C, called supercooling, before ice suddenly forms: water that starts out cooler tends to supercool further. The trouble is that the outcome depends on many details, and results in water have been hard to repeat. The physicist Monwhea Jeng pointed out that even the question needs care: does “frozen” mean the first ice crystals, or the whole body of water turned to ice?"
resolution:
  - "Studies of water give mixed results. A 2026 review by Gianluca Teza, John Bechhoefer, Antonio Lasanta, Oren Raz and Marija Vucelja says that both the controversy and the difficulty of getting reproducible results for water have lasted to this day."
  - "Outside water, a broader version of the effect is better established. Physicists now use the name for any system in which a hotter start reaches a cold final state faster than a cooler start. Avinash Kumar and John Bechhoefer reported this in 2020 for a single tiny particle held in water, including a “strong” form in which cooling was exponentially faster, and related effects have since been studied in magnetic alloys and quantum systems. These results do not settle what happens in a freezer full of water."
positions:
  - title: "Hot water can really freeze first"
    body: "Mpemba and Osborne reported that water starting near 90 °C began to freeze much sooner than water starting near 25 °C. David Auerbach found in 1995 that in some cases water that started hotter began freezing first, linked to how far each sample supercooled. Jonathan Katz replied to Burridge and Linden that the effect concerns freezing, not cooling to 0 °C, and proposed that it depends on minerals dissolved in hard water. The 2026 review by Teza and colleagues says there is strong evidence for at least some kinds of the effect, notably those linked to evaporation and frost. Cost: the results are hard to reproduce, depend on many details of the set-up, and freezing times vary a great deal from one run to the next."
  - title: "Meaningful observations in water are not shown"
    body: "Henry Burridge and Paul Linden cooled water under carefully controlled conditions in 2016 and measured the time for it to reach 0 °C. They found no sign that hot water got there first, showed that placing a thermometer just 1 cm higher or lower could create an apparent effect, and judged Mpemba and Osborne’s original data to be erroneous. They concluded that “there is no evidence to support meaningful observations of the Mpemba effect.” Cost: as Katz pointed out, they measured cooling to 0 °C rather than freezing, so they did not test what Mpemba reported, and reviews still cite reports of hot water freezing first through evaporation and frost."
finePrint: "Joseph Black also reported in 1775 that boiled water froze before water that had not been boiled. The same year as Mpemba and Osborne, George Kell independently reported the effect. Mpemba and Osborne used 70 ml of water in 100 ml beakers in the ice box of a domestic fridge. A 2026 study by Anna Janni, Santiago Botero Ampudia and E. Dan Dahlberg recorded instances of hotter water reaching 0 °C first or beginning to freeze first, 46 and 58 in 176 measurements under the two meanings. It found that the results depended on convective air currents and on variations in supercooling. This entry takes no view on whether the effect is real in everyday water."
