title: "Barber paradox"
titleAccent: "paradox"
chapter: self-reference
status: defused
simplification: light
order: 2
written: true
sourcesVerified: true
problem: "A barber shaves everyone who doesn't shave themselves, and nobody else. Who shaves the barber?"
claimA: "A barber can follow any rule he likes about who he shaves."
claimB: "The barber is one of the people his rule is about."
verdict: "The first claim is wrong. The contradiction never goes away: it proves that no barber can follow this rule."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that any rule about who to shave is one a barber could follow."
statusNote: "the contradiction is real and it stays, but it proves something instead of leaving a puzzle open: no barber can follow this rule. Nobody disputes this, and giving up the idea that any rule can be followed costs nothing."
setup: "In a village there is one barber. His rule is that he shaves every man in the village who does not shave himself, and nobody else. It sounds like a tidy arrangement. Then someone asks the obvious question, does the barber shave himself?"
whyWrong: "The puzzle is told as if the rule is one the barber can follow, and the only question is what he does about his own beard. The rule was stated in one sentence, so it seems a barber could keep it. Stating a rule does not guarantee that."
resolution:
  - "Check both answers. If he shaves himself, he is one of the men who shave themselves, and his rule says he shaves only men who do not. If he does not shave himself, he is one of the men who do not, and his rule says he must shave them. Either way, the rule is broken."
  - "So the right conclusion is not a mystery about what the barber does. It is that no barber can keep that rule. A barber who keeps it cannot exist, in the same way that a married bachelor cannot exist."
  - "The contradiction does not disappear. What changes is what it shows. A paradox stays open when the assumption it knocks down is one that cannot be given up without a big cost. Here the only assumption knocked down is that a barber can follow any rule he likes, and nothing depends on it, so the contradiction simply proves that no barber can follow this one."
finePrint: "The barber is often used as a popular illustration of Russell's paradox, which asks about the set of all sets that do not contain themselves. Russell himself kept the two apart. He said that in the barber form “the contradiction is not very difficult to solve”: the usual answer is simply that no such barber can exist. The set version could not be dismissed that way: it forced mathematicians to change the rules for building sets."
