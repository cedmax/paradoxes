title: "Barber paradox"
titleAccent: "paradox"
chapter: self-reference
status: defused
simplification: light
order: 2
written: true
sourcesVerified: true
problem: "A barber shaves everyone who doesn't shave themselves, and nobody else. Who shaves the barber?"
claimA: "A barber can follow any rule he likes about who he shaves."
claimB: "The barber is one of the people his rule is about."
verdict: "The first claim is wrong. The contradiction never goes away: it proves that no barber can follow this rule."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that any rule about who to shave is one a barber could follow."
statusNote: "the contradiction is real and it stays, but it proves something instead of leaving a puzzle open: no such barber can exist. Nobody disputes this, and giving up the barber costs nothing."
setup: "In a village there is one barber. His rule is that he shaves every man in the village who does not shave himself, and nobody else. It sounds like a tidy arrangement. Then someone asks the obvious question, does the barber shave himself?"
whyWrong: "The puzzle is told as if the barber is a given, a person we can ask questions about. We described him in one sentence and so we assume he exists. Descriptions do not guarantee that."
resolution:
  - "Check both answers. If he shaves himself, he is one of the men who shave themselves, and his rule says he shaves only men who do not. If he does not shave himself, he is one of the men who do not, and his rule says he must shave them. Either way, the rule is broken."
  - "So the right conclusion is not a mystery about what the barber does. It is that there is no such barber. A village with that rule and that barber cannot exist, in the same way that a married bachelor cannot exist."
  - "The contradiction does not disappear. What changes is what it shows. A paradox stays open when the assumption it knocks down is one that cannot be given up without a big cost. Here the only assumption knocked down is that this barber exists, and nothing depends on him, so the contradiction simply proves that he cannot."
finePrint: "The barber is often used as a popular illustration of Russell's paradox, which asks about the set of all sets that do not contain themselves. Russell himself kept the two apart. He said the barber form is not very difficult to solve, because the answer is simply that there is no such barber. The set version could not be dismissed that way: it forced mathematicians to change the rules for building sets."
