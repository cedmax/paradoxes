title: "Unexpected hanging paradox"
titleAccent: "paradox"
chapter: belief
status: unreconciled
simplification: light
order: 5
written: true
problem: "A prisoner is told he will be hanged on a day he cannot predict. Can he rule out every day?"
claimA: "The prisoner can prove that a surprise hanging is impossible."
claimB: "A hanging on, say, Wednesday would be a complete surprise to him."
verdict: "Philosophers do not agree on what exactly goes wrong."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that what you know today lets you reason reliably about what you will know later."
statusNote: "the problem is real, but there is no agreed diagnosis and the proposed fixes each cost something."
setup: "A judge tells a prisoner that he will be hanged at noon on one weekday of the coming week, and that the day will be a surprise: he will not know it the evening before. The prisoner reasons. It cannot be Friday, because if he is still alive on Thursday evening he will know it is Friday. So Friday is out. Then it cannot be Thursday, since with Friday gone, he would know on Wednesday evening. He goes back through every day and rules out them all. He feels safe. On Wednesday morning the executioner arrives, and it is a complete surprise."
whyWrong: "The prisoner's argument looks airtight, but the judge turns out to be right. Either the argument has a hidden flaw or the announcement, which seemed fine, was somehow impossible. Philosophers cannot agree which, and that is what makes it a paradox rather than a trick."
resolution:
  - "Wikipedia says there is no consensus on the nature of the paradox, so no agreed resolution exists. It reached the wider public in Martin Gardner's March 1963 column in Scientific American, and appeared in print earlier, in a 1948 article by D. J. O'Connor. Michael Scriven's 1951 version is closest to the one we know."
  - "The best known diagnosis comes from W. V. Quine in 1953. He argued that the prisoner's reasoning only shows he cannot know the announcement is true in advance, not that the judge is wrong. Others say the announcement refers to itself, or note that the backward argument assumes the prisoner will know the judge's words are true on each evening, which is not guaranteed. Sources list several other proposals, including giving up some principles about knowledge."
positions:
  - title: The prisoner cannot be sure the announcement holds
    body: "The prisoner assumes he will know the judge is telling the truth. If he does not, the surprise is still possible. This is close to Quine's view and to an analysis by Chow. Cost: the story seems to say the judge is truthful, so some think this changes the puzzle."
  - title: The announcement refers to itself
    body: "The judge's words are partly about their own truth, which makes them unreliable, as with the liar paradox. Shaw took this view in 1968. Cost: self-reference is not always a fault, and a logical proof by Fitch showed the statement can be written down in a formal system."
  - title: Give up a principle about knowledge
    body: "Reject that knowing something means you know that you know it, or that you know what follows from what you know. Cost: each is a rule that normally looks safe, and nobody has shown that dropping one is enough on its own."
finePrint: "The surprise exam is the same puzzle with a teacher and a test, and this entry tells it as a hanging, the best known form. The three positions are a short summary of a literature with many more. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy lists rejecting KK (the principle that knowing means knowing you know), closure and bivalence as proposed ways out. Wikipedia and the Stanford entry give slightly different histories of the earliest appearances, so the dates here use only what both support."
