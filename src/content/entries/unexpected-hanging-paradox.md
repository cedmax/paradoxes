description: "A prisoner is promised a hanging on a day they cannot predict. Can they rule out every day? The same puzzle can be told with a surprise exam."

chapter: belief

simplification: light
order: 70
problem: "A prisoner is told they will be hanged on a day they cannot predict. Can they rule out every day?"
claimA: "The judge's announcement makes sense and can come true."
claimB: "The prisoner's argument proves that it cannot come true."
whatBreaks: "Either the judge's announcement, which talks about what the prisoner can know, or the prisoner's assumption that they will still know it is true on each evening."
statusNote: "the diagnoses are that the prisoner cannot count on the announcement holding, that the announcement defeats itself, or that knowing something does not mean knowing that you know it."
setup: "A judge tells a prisoner that they will be hanged at noon on one weekday of the coming week, and that the day will be a surprise: they will not know it the evening before. The prisoner reasons. It cannot be Friday, because if they are still alive on Thursday evening they will know it is Friday. So Friday is out. Then it cannot be Thursday, since with Friday gone, they would know on Wednesday evening. They go back through every day and rule out them all. They feel safe. On Wednesday morning the executioner arrives, and it is a complete surprise."
whyWrong: "A Wednesday execution seems to fulfil the announcement, while the backward argument seems to rule out every day. The dispute is whether the announcement is coherent and which assumptions about knowledge the prisoner may use."
resolution:
  - "Some accounts challenge what the prisoner can know about the announcement on each evening. Others argue that the announcement defeats itself, or that familiar rules about knowledge fail here."
  - "The surprise is not by itself a proof that every version of the judge’s words is coherent. The result depends on what those words mean and what the prisoner is entitled to know. No diagnosis is agreed."
positions:
  - title: "The prisoner cannot be sure the announcement holds"
    body: "The backward argument assumes the prisoner will still know the judge’s announcement is true. Cost: ordinary announcements normally do convey knowledge, so this failure needs explaining."
  - title: "The announcement defeats itself"
    body: "Some formulations make the promise about surprise self-referential or inconsistent. Cost: other ways of expressing surprise block the backward argument without making the announcement inconsistent."
  - title: "Change a rule about knowledge"
    body: "Question whether knowing means knowing that one knows, or knowing every consequence. Cost: these rules normally seem safe, and rejecting them changes the account of knowledge."
finePrint: "The surprise exam is the same puzzle with a teacher and a test. Formal versions differ in how they express surprise and knowledge. Some responses question whether knowing something means knowing that one knows it, or whether one knows all the consequences of what one knows. Other formulations block the backward argument differently. The literature has many more proposals than the three summarised here."
