title: "Unexpected hanging paradox"
titleAccent: "paradox"
chapter: belief
status: disputed
simplification: light
order: 5
written: true
problem: "A prisoner is told he will be hanged on a day he cannot predict. Can he rule out every day?"
claimA: "The judge's announcement makes sense and can come true."
claimB: "The prisoner's argument proves that it cannot come true."
verdict: "Philosophers do not agree on what exactly goes wrong."
whatBreaks: "Either the judge's announcement, which talks about what the prisoner can know, or the prisoner's assumption that he will still know it is true on each evening."
statusNote: "experts agree the puzzle is real, but disagree on whether the fault lies in the prisoner's assumptions about what he will know or in the announcement itself."
setup: "A judge tells a prisoner that he will be hanged at noon on one weekday of the coming week, and that the day will be a surprise: he will not know it the evening before. The prisoner reasons. It cannot be Friday, because if he is still alive on Thursday evening he will know it is Friday. So Friday is out. Then it cannot be Thursday, since with Friday gone, he would know on Wednesday evening. He goes back through every day and rules out them all. He feels safe. On Wednesday morning the executioner arrives, and it is a complete surprise."
whyWrong: "The prisoner's argument looks airtight, but the judge turns out to be right. Either the argument has a hidden flaw or the announcement, which seemed fine, was somehow impossible. Philosophers cannot agree which, and that is what makes it a paradox rather than a trick."
resolution:
  - "Wikipedia says there is no consensus on the nature of the paradox, so no agreed resolution exists. Both sources listed here mention a 1948 article by D. J. O'Connor, who thought the announcement defeats itself. Wikipedia adds that Michael Scriven's 1951 version is closest to the one we know, and that Martin Gardner brought it to a wide audience in his March 1963 column in Scientific American."
  - "W. V. Quine argued in 1953 that the prisoner's reasoning only shows he cannot know in advance that the announcement is true, not that the judge is wrong. Others say the announcement refers to itself, or note that the backward argument assumes the prisoner will know the judge's words are true on each evening, which is not guaranteed. Sources list several other proposals, including giving up some principles about knowledge."
positions:
  - title: "The prisoner cannot be sure the announcement holds"
    body: "The prisoner assumes he will know the judge is telling the truth. If he does not, the surprise is still possible. This is close to Quine's view and to an analysis by Chow. Cost: as the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes, common sense says the announcement does inform the prisoner, just as a judge's other statements of intent would."
  - title: "The announcement refers to itself"
    body: "The judge's words are partly about their own truth, which makes them unreliable, as with the liar paradox. R. Shaw took this view in 1958. In 1964 Frederic Fitch showed the announcement can be written down in formal logic, and argued that, although self-reference is not always a fault, here it is, because the statement contradicts itself. Cost: the word “surprise” is vague, and the outcome depends on how it is written down. Some ways of stating the announcement block the prisoner's argument without any appeal to self-reference."
  - title: "Give up a principle about knowledge"
    body: "Reject that knowing something means you know that you know it, or that you know what follows from what you know. Cost: each is a rule that normally looks safe, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy treats rejecting such rules as a sign of how deep, or how desperate, the problem is."
finePrint: "The surprise exam is the same puzzle with a teacher and a test, and this entry tells it as a hanging, the best known form. The three positions are a short summary of a literature with many more. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy lists rejecting KK (the principle that knowing means knowing you know), closure and bivalence as proposed ways out. The two sources give different histories. Of the dates here, only O'Connor (1948) and Quine (1953) appear in both. Scriven (1951), Shaw (1958), Gardner (1963) and Fitch (1964) come from Wikipedia alone, and Wikipedia flags its Gardner claim as needing a citation."
