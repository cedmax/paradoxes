problem: "How can people be truly moved by characters they know do not exist?"
claimA: "People feel real emotions for fictional characters and events that they know are made up."
claimB: "A person can feel a real emotion for something only if they believe it truly exists."
verdict: "The rule that emotion needs a belief in the object’s existence does not hold. People feel real emotion about the past, the possible and the imagined."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that a real emotion needs a belief that its object exists."
statusNote: "the second claim is wrong, on the account most philosophers now give, because real emotions can be felt about things that are not believed to exist. Some philosophers still resolve it by denying the first claim instead."
setup: "A reader cries when a character in a novel dies, though the reader knows the character never lived. The puzzle was put forward in a paper by Radford and Weston in 1975. It rests on three claims that each seem true. First, people feel emotions about characters and events that they know are fictional. Second, to be moved, people must believe that what they are moved by truly exists. Third, a person who takes a character to be fictional does not at the same time believe that the character is real. The three cannot all be true. Radford and Weston concluded that responses to fiction are irrational."
whyWrong: "The second claim sounds like common sense. Fear of a snake seems to need a belief that the snake is there. That makes the rule look like part of what an emotion is. But the rule is too strong. People feel emotion about events that happened long ago, about things that might happen, and about fears they know are irrational. None of these involves a belief that the object is in front of them."
resolution:
  - "Most current accounts treat the second claim as the one that fails. It is now widely rejected, for the reasons above, and on several theories of emotion the paradox disappears. On thought theories, which Peter Lamarque, Noël Carroll and Robert Yanal have defended, a vivid thought about something is enough to produce a real emotion, with no belief that it exists."
  - "So the first claim is true and the second is false. Reading fiction can produce real feelings."
finePrint: "Other philosophers resolve it differently. Walton (1978) denied the first claim: readers feel something like emotions, which he called quasi-emotions, and do not truly fear a monster on the page. Alan Paskow denied the third by treating fictional characters as real in a way. Neuroscience studies have found weaker responses to things believed to be fictional, which suggests the difference is one of degree, not of kind."
