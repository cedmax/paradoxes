titleAccent: dilemma

written: true

problem: If each of two people does best by betraying the other, why do both end up worse off than if they had trusted each other?
claimA: Whatever the other person does, you are better off betraying them.
claimB: If both of you betray, you are both worse off than if neither had.
verdict: The logic is agreed. Whether it is rational to cooperate anyway is still argued.
whatBreaks: The assumption that when everyone chases their own best result, everyone gets a good result.
statusNote: the arithmetic is agreed, but experts disagree on whether the rational choice is to betray or to cooperate.
setup: "Two prisoners are held in separate cells and cannot talk. Each can stay silent or testify against the other. If both stay silent, each gets 1 year. If one testifies and the other stays silent, the one who testified goes free and the other gets 3 years. If both testify, each gets 2 years. You do not know what the other will do. If they stay silent, testifying gets you 0 years instead of 1. If they testify, testifying gets you 2 years instead of 3. So testifying is better for you either way."
whyWrong: "Nothing in the reasoning is a trick. Betraying really is the better choice for each person taken alone, and yet two people who both follow it end up with 2 years each instead of 1. The two claims are both true, and the oddness comes from that. It is not a contradiction. It is a conflict between what is best for each person and what is best for the pair. That is why careful writers call it a dilemma or a puzzle, not a paradox in the strict sense."
resolution:
  - "The puzzle was devised in 1950 by Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher at the RAND Corporation. Albert Tucker later gave it the prison story. Game theorists agree on the one-shot result: betraying is the dominant choice, meaning it is better whatever the other person does, and mutual betrayal is the stable outcome."
  - "Repetition changes things. If the same two people play many times, with no known last round, cooperation can pay, because betrayal can be punished next time. In Robert Axelrod's tournaments in the 1980s, a simple strategy called tit for tat, which cooperates first and then copies the other player's last move, did very well against many rivals. But if both players know exactly how many rounds there are, a backward argument says they should still betray every time. They reason that the last round is a one-shot game, so they betray there, which makes the round before it the last that matters, and so on back to the start. Real people often cooperate anyway."
  - "The live question is whether, in a single game, cooperating can ever be rational. Some thinkers say it can, for example when the other player is much like you and will probably choose as you do. Others say your choice cannot change what the other person does, so betraying stays right."
positions:
  - title: Betray, even in one game
    body: "Your choice cannot change what the other player has already decided, and betraying is better against either choice. Cost: two people who reason this way reliably do worse than two who do not."
  - title: Cooperate if you are alike
    body: "If the other player thinks as you do, your choice is strong evidence of theirs, so cooperate. This is close to the reasoning for taking one box in Newcomb's paradox. Cost: it treats your choice as a sign of something it does not cause."
  - title: The one-shot game is rare
    body: "Most real situations repeat, or involve reputation, so the sensible question is what works over time. Cost: it does not answer the one-shot case, which is the one the puzzle asks about."
finePrint: The prison sentences above are one common set of numbers. Any numbers work if temptation beats the reward for mutual cooperation, which beats the punishment for mutual betrayal, which beats being the one betrayed. The label "prisoner's dilemma" is often credited to Tucker, but the Stanford Encyclopedia says it first appeared in print in a 1957 book by Luce and Raiffa. The Stanford entry also says there remain people committed to each view on whether cooperating is rational.
