title: Prisoner's dilemma
titleAccent: dilemma
chapter: choice-identity
status: disputed
simplification: light
order: 3
written: true
sourcesVerified: true
problem: If each of two people does best by betraying the other, why do both end up worse off than if they had trusted each other?
claimA: "Rational players testify, because it is better for each whatever the other does."
claimB: "Rational players stay silent, because two rational players who reason alike both do better."
verdict: "The arithmetic is agreed, but whether rational players should testify or stay silent is still argued."
whatBreaks: "The idea that a choice which is better for you whatever the other does is always the rational one, even against someone who reasons exactly as you do."
statusNote: "game theorists agree on the arithmetic, but for players who reason alike the Stanford Encyclopedia says there remain people committed to each view."
setup: "Two prisoners are held in separate cells and cannot talk. Each can stay silent or testify against the other. If both stay silent, each gets 1 year. If one testifies and the other stays silent, the one who testified goes free and the other gets 3 years. If both testify, each gets 2 years. You do not know what the other will do. If they stay silent, testifying gets you 0 years instead of 1. If they testify, testifying gets you 2 years instead of 3. So testifying is better for you either way."
whyWrong: "Nothing in the arithmetic is a trick. Testifying really is better for each person taken alone, whatever the other does (game theorists say testifying “dominates”), and yet two people who both testify end up with 2 years each instead of 1. Now suppose both players are rational and reason in exactly the same way. Then they will make the same choice, so the real options look like both silent or both testifying, and both silent is better. One line of reasoning says testify, the other says stay silent, and both start from what a rational player should do. The arithmetic contains no contradiction. The conflict is over what rationality recommends."
resolution:
  - "The puzzle was devised in 1950 by Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher at the RAND Corporation. Albert Tucker later gave it the prison story. Game theorists agree on the one-shot result: betraying is the dominant choice, meaning it is better whatever the other person does, and mutual betrayal is the stable outcome."
  - "Repetition changes things. If the same two people play many times, with no known last round, cooperation can pay, because betrayal can be punished next time. In Robert Axelrod's tournaments around 1980, described in his 1984 book The Evolution of Cooperation, a simple strategy called tit for tat, which cooperates first and then copies the other player's last move, did very well against many rivals. But if both players know exactly how many rounds there are, a backward argument says they should still betray every time. They reason that the last round is a one-shot game, so they betray there, which makes the round before it the last that matters, and so on back to the start. Real people often cooperate anyway."
  - "The live question is whether, in a single game, staying silent can ever be rational. Some thinkers say it can, for example when the other player is much like you and will probably choose as you do. Others say your choice cannot change what the other person does, so testifying stays right."
positions:
  - title: Testify, even in one game
    body: "Keep the first claim. Your choice cannot change what the other player decides, and testifying is better against either choice. Cost: two people who reason this way reliably do worse than two who do not."
  - title: Stay silent if you reason alike
    body: "Keep the second claim. If the other player thinks as you do, your choice is strong evidence of theirs, so stay silent. This is close to the reasoning for taking one box in Newcomb's paradox. Cost: it treats your choice as a sign of something it does not cause."
  - title: The one-shot game is rare
    body: "Most real situations repeat, or involve reputation, so the sensible question is what works over time. Cost: it does not answer the one-shot case, which is the one the puzzle asks about."
finePrint: The prison sentences above are one common set of numbers. Any numbers work if temptation beats the reward for mutual cooperation, which beats the punishment for mutual betrayal, which beats being the one betrayed. The label "prisoner's dilemma" is often credited to Tucker, but the Stanford Encyclopedia says it first appeared in print in a 1957 book by Luce and Raiffa. The Stanford entry also says that, for players who reason alike, there remain people committed to each view on whether cooperating is rational.
