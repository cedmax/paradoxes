problem: "Pinocchio says “My nose grows now”, and his nose grows only when he lies. What is the sentence?"
claimA: "If the sentence is true, his nose does not grow, so the sentence is false."
claimB: "If the sentence is false, his nose grows, so the sentence is true."
verdict: "Experts disagree on whether this is a real Liar, or no paradox at all."
whatBreaks: "Whether the sentence is a true Liar, or whether the way the nose rule and the tense work means there is no paradox."
statusNote: "the discussion in Wikipedia is between Peter Eldridge-Smith, who sees a Liar without a truth predicate, and critics such as William Vallicella, who see no paradox."
setup: "In the 1883 novel The Adventures of Pinocchio by Carlo Collodi, the puppet’s nose grows when he tells a lie. Wikipedia says the Pinocchio paradox was proposed in February 2001 by Veronique Eldridge-Smith, who was 11 years old. Her father, Peter Eldridge-Smith, who works on logic, had explained the Liar paradox to her and her older brother, and asked them to invent their own versions. In a few minutes she suggested: “Pinocchio says, ‘My nose will be growing.’” He wrote an article on it for the journal Analysis. The version usually discussed is in the present tense, “My nose grows now”, with the rule that his nose grows if and only if he is saying something false."
whyWrong: "If “My nose grows now” is true, his nose does not grow, since it only grows when he lies. But then what he said is false. If it is false, his nose does grow, which makes the sentence true. As Eldridge-Smith puts it, Pinocchio’s nose is growing if and only if it is not growing. Wikipedia says the paradox is a special case of the Liar tradition because it has no semantic predicate, a word such as “true” or “false”, in the sentence."
resolution:
  - "Wikipedia reports that Eldridge-Smith argues that the paradox is a counterexample to solutions of the Liar that exclude semantic predicates from the language, because “is growing” is not a semantic predicate. He also says it raises a purely logical problem for any solution that arranges languages in a hierarchy. Such solutions follow Tarski, and Eldridge-Smith says they cannot explain the case."
positions:
  - title: It is a genuine Liar without a truth predicate (Eldridge-Smith)
    body: "The sentence is a version of the Liar even though it says nothing about truth. A world in which Pinocchio’s nose grows if and only if he says something untrue is logically possible, but one in which he says “My nose is growing” cannot be, and a hierarchy of languages cannot explain why. Cost: it rules out the solutions that exclude truth words from the language, or that arrange languages in a hierarchy, which Wikipedia reports he says cannot explain the case. He adds that, if the nose growing and not growing were a true contradiction, such a world would be metaphysically impossible, not merely semantically impossible."
  - title: There is no paradox (Vallicella)
    body: "In the future tense, the sentence can never be treated as a lie, since a false prediction is not a lie. In the present tense, he is either lying or not, and in either case the result is wholly unproblematic. Cost: Wikipedia reports a criticism that, unlike a person’s blood pressure, Pinocchio’s nose responds to the truth of his own statements. A further reply says the sentence can only be a guess about an earlier lie, not something he could have worked out, so it still does not settle the matter."
finePrint: "Wikipedia also mentions the story of Buridan’s bridge, in which Socrates may cross only if the first thing he says is true and answers “You are going to throw me into the water”. That is a related puzzle in which the rule sets the answer against itself. The entry for the [Liar paradox](liar-paradox) covers the general ways out. The sources here do not cover how each of them handles this sentence."
