problem: "Pinocchio’s nose grows exactly when he says something false. What happens when he says “My nose is growing”?"
claimA: "The clash only arises through a rule about truth, so the Pinocchio story is a genuine Liar paradox, even though it has no word like “true” or “false”."
claimB: "The Pinocchio story is simply an impossible story, like that of a barber who shaves exactly the people who do not shave themselves, so it raises no new problem about truth."
verdict: "Logicians disagree on whether this is a real Liar paradox that needs no word like “true”, or just a story that cannot happen."
whatBreaks: "Whether the clash comes from a rule about truth, which would make it a version of the Liar, or from a story that describes something impossible, as with the barber."
statusNote: "logicians writing in the journal Analysis disagree. Peter Eldridge-Smith argues that the sentence is a Liar with no truth word in it, and Jc Beall replies that the story is merely impossible, like the barber’s."
setup: "In Carlo Collodi’s 1883 novel The Adventures of Pinocchio, the puppet’s nose grows when he tells a lie. The logician Peter Eldridge-Smith explained the Liar paradox to his elder children and asked them to invent versions of their own. In February 2001 his daughter Veronique, then 11, came up with: “Pinocchio says ‘My nose will be growing’.” Father and daughter published it as “The Pinocchio paradox” in the journal Analysis in 2010. The future tense leaves open how soon after a lie the nose grows, so the version discussed in Analysis, by Eldridge-Smith and by Jc Beall, is in the present tense: Pinocchio says “My nose is growing”, and his nose grows if and only if what he is saying is false."
whyWrong: "If “My nose is growing” is true, his nose does not grow, since it only grows when he says something false. But then what he said is false. If it is false, his nose grows, which makes what he said true. So his nose is growing if and only if it is not growing. This has the shape of the [Liar paradox](liar-paradox), but the sentence only talks about a nose. Words like “true” and “false” are called semantic predicates, and many proposed fixes for the Liar work by limiting how those words can be used. “Grows” is not such a word."
resolution:
  - "The disagreement is about what kind of problem this is. If it is a version of the Liar, it brings the Liar’s unsolved trouble with it, and some of the Liar’s proposed fixes do not reach it. If it is only an impossible story, it is no more troubling than a story about a barber who cannot exist. That is why this entry is Disputed while the Liar is Unreconciled: the open question here comes one step earlier, whether the Pinocchio case is a Liar at all."
  - "Eldridge-Smith argues that it is. He says it is a counterexample to fixes for the Liar that exclude semantic predicates, because “is growing” is not one. It also defeats, he argues, fixes in the style of Alfred Tarski, which arrange languages in levels so that a sentence can only talk about the truth of sentences on a lower level: a world where Pinocchio’s nose grows exactly when he says something untrue is logically possible, but not one where he also says “My nose is growing”, and the levels cannot explain why."
  - "In 2011 he aimed it at dialetheists, logicians who accept that some contradictions are true, but only ones about truth and language, such as the Liar. A nose that both grows and does not grow is a contradiction about the world, not about language, so on his view it is “one dialetheia too many”, a dialetheia being a true contradiction. Beall, a dialetheist, replied that same year that the case is like the barber: the contradiction holds only according to the story, and nothing shows it carries over to the real world. Eldridge-Smith answered in 2012 that the two cases differ, because the barber story contradicts itself on its own, while the Pinocchio story needs a principle about truth to produce a contradiction."
positions:
  - title: It is a Liar without a truth word (Eldridge-Smith)
    body: "The contradiction comes from linking the nose to the falsity of what Pinocchio says, which is a principle about truth. So the case is a version of the Liar, one that slips past fixes which limit words like “true”. Cost: it treats a contradiction inside a story as a problem for truth itself, and Beall argues that nothing has shown how what is true according to a story carries over to the real world."
  - title: It is an impossible story (Beall)
    body: "Like the barber, the Pinocchio of this story simply cannot exist. According to the story his nose grows and does not grow, and that is, in Beall’s words, “an enjoyable story without noteworthy metaphysical consequences”. Cost: Eldridge-Smith replies that, unlike the barber story, this one needs a principle about truth to produce a contradiction, so it looks like a Liar, and he asks why some Liars would be impossible stories when, on Beall’s view, others produce true contradictions."
finePrint: "The future-tense version raises its own questions about when the nose would grow. The 2010 paper notes that if Pinocchio says “My nose will grow” and everything else he says is true, his nose will grow if and only if it does not. Some deny there is any paradox. William Vallicella, who said he had not read the Analysis papers, argued on his blog Maverick Philosopher that a failed prediction is not a lie, and that in the present tense the result is unproblematic either way. One reply reads “My nose grows now” as an educated guess implying that Pinocchio’s previous sentence was a lie, so that whether the nose grows depends on what else he has said. Eldridge-Smith also compares the case to the puzzle of Buridan’s bridge, in which Socrates says “You are going to throw me into the water”, and Plato, who must decide, can neither throw him in nor let him cross. The [barber](barber-paradox) is on this site as its own entry. The fixes for the Liar in general are covered in the [Liar paradox](liar-paradox) entry."
