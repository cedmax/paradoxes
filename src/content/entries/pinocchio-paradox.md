problem: "Pinocchio’s nose grows exactly when he says something false. What happens when he says “My nose is growing”?"
claimA: "Pinocchio’s contradictory nose is a problem about truth itself, like the liar paradox."
claimB: "Pinocchio’s contradictory nose is only an impossible story, with no new problem about truth itself."
verdict: "Logicians disagree on whether this is a real Liar paradox that needs no word like “true”, or just a story that cannot happen."
whatBreaks: "The disagreement is over whether the story reveals a problem about truth or merely describes something impossible."
statusNote: "the authors disagree over what the clash shows. Eldridge-Smith treats it as a liar paradox without a truth word in the sentence. Beall treats it as an impossible story like the [barber paradox](barber-paradox)."
setup: "In this version of the story, Pinocchio’s nose grows exactly when what he says is false. He says “My nose is growing”. If the nose grows, his statement is true, so the rule says it should not grow. If it does not grow, his statement is false, so it should grow."
whyWrong: "Neither outcome fits the rule. The sentence never says “true” or “false”, yet the nose seems caught in the same trap as the [liar paradox](liar-paradox). Is that a new difficulty for theories of truth, or just a fictional rule that cannot work?"
resolution:
  - "Both sides recognise the clash. They disagree on what it shows. Calling it a liar brings the liar’s unsolved problems into the physical story. Calling it impossible treats it like the [barber](barber-paradox), whose defining rule cannot be satisfied."
positions:
  - title: "A liar without a truth word"
    body: "Eldridge-Smith argues that the nose’s rule connects the sentence to its own falsity. A ban on truth words in sentences does not catch it. Cost: Beall objects that a contradiction in a story need not reveal a problem in the real world."
  - title: "An impossible story"
    body: "Beall argues that this Pinocchio cannot exist under the stated rules, just as the barber cannot. Cost: Eldridge-Smith replies that the clash needs a principle about truth, and asks why it should be dismissed when other liars are taken seriously."
finePrint: "The rule here concerns saying something false, not knowingly lying. That distinction matters: a failed prediction need not be a lie. Veronique Eldridge-Smith proposed a future-tense version in 2001; she and Peter Eldridge-Smith published it in 2010. The present tense avoids some questions about when the nose would grow. The subsequent debate also concerns theories that accept some contradictions as true, and theories that divide truth into levels. This entry covers the disagreement over whether the story is a liar, rather than deciding among those wider theories."
