problem: "Why does backward reasoning tell a large chain never to fight a new rival?"
claimA: "A chain facing many entrants should fight early rivals to protect a reputation that deters later rivals."
claimB: "A chain facing those entrants should never fight: backward reasoning shows it will always earn more by sharing the market after entry."
verdict: "The simple game predicts no fighting, yet reputation and bounded reasoning can make fighting a plausible strategy."
whatBreaks: "Whether a backward-induction recommendation or the long-term deterrent value of fighting should guide the chain."
statusNote: "the mathematical result holds in Selten’s complete-information game, while Selten and later researchers questioned whether it captures sensible behaviour."
setup: "A large chain meets a possible rival in one town after another. Once a rival enters, the chain can share the market or start a costly price war. A war may frighten later rivals."
whyWrong: "At the last town, fighting loses money and has nobody left to deter. Backward reasoning then rules out fighting at the second-last town and repeats the step all the way to the first."
resolution:
  - "Selten’s formal game predicts accommodation at every stage, yet Selten himself argued that sensible players might not follow the recommendation. The puzzle is about whether that reasoning is a good guide to behaviour."
  - "Kreps and Wilson, and Milgrom and Roberts, showed that uncertainty about a chain’s willingness to fight can make reputation-building rational in richer games. Those models change assumptions rather than disproving Selten’s result."
positions:
  - title: "Follow backward induction"
    body: "In the original game, the chain always earns more by accommodating after any entry, so threats to fight are not credible. Cost: it predicts behaviour Selten considered implausible."
  - title: "Value deterrence and reputation"
    body: "Selten defended limited rationality; later reputation models show why early fighting can deter entrants when information is incomplete. Cost: those explanations depart from the original game’s assumptions."
finePrint: "Selten’s 1978 paper identified tension between game-theoretic advice and plausible human behaviour. The [unexpected hanging paradox](unexpected-hanging-paradox) also uses reasoning backwards, but adds a question about knowledge and surprise. The games are not identical."
