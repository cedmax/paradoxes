problem: "Why does backward reasoning tell a large chain never to fight a new rival?"
claimA: "A profit-seeking chain should fight early rivals to scare later ones away."
claimB: "In a fixed-payoff game where everyone knows the incentives, backward reasoning predicts that the chain will accommodate every rival."
verdict: "A threat to fight later is not credible in the simple model if fighting would cost more once a rival enters."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that a costly future threat will be carried out simply because it could deter rivals now."
statusNote: "the backward result follows from the simple game’s assumptions; reputation, uncertainty or different payoffs can change the result."
setup: "A chain faces possible new shops in a series of towns. It can fight each entrant with a price war or let them share the market. Fighting costs it money now but might frighten later entrants."
whyWrong: "The last entrant knows there are no later rivals to frighten, so the chain gains nothing in the model by fighting. The same reasoning then moves back to the second-last and every earlier town."
resolution:
  - "If each entrant knows the chain’s payoffs and is sure the chain will choose its best response later, an early price-war threat has no force."
  - "Real chains can value reputations, be uncertain about costs or have different incentives. Those variations can make tough behaviour rational. The link to the [unexpected hanging](unexpected-hanging-paradox) is backward reasoning, not the same paradox."
finePrint: "Reinhard Selten’s chain-store game uses fixed, commonly known payoffs. The result is specific to that game and does not prove that real chains never deter entry."
