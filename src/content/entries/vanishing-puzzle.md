problem: "Turning part of a picture changes how many people it shows. Where does the missing one go?"
claimA: "Every figure in the picture is a whole, full-sized figure in both positions, so the count cannot change."
claimB: "One position shows 13 boys and the other shows 12."
verdict: "The figures are not the same size in both positions. Turning the disc shares the pieces of one figure out among the others, so there are fewer, slightly larger figures."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that each figure stays whole and the same size when the pieces move."
statusNote: "the idea that every figure stays whole and the same size is wrong. The pieces of one figure are spread among the rest, so each of the remaining figures is a little larger and none is lost."
setup: "A vanishing puzzle is a picture cut into pieces that can be moved. In one position it shows a certain number of figures. Move the pieces and it shows one fewer. In the 1890s the American puzzle maker Sam Loyd made rotating versions: a round card pinned to a backing card so it can turn. In Get Off the Earth, thirteen warriors stand round the rim of a turning disc; turn it and one of them is gone. In The Disappearing Bicyclist, turning the disc so its arrow points to A shows 13 boys, and pointing it to B shows 12. An earlier advertising card, The Magic Egg Puzzle, sold in New York in 1880, could be cut into four strips and rearranged to show 8, 9 or 10 eggs."
whyWrong: "The eye counts heads and bodies, and assumes each one it counts is a complete figure of the usual size. But every figure is drawn across the cut, and the parts on the turning disc are not lined up with the parts on the backing in the same way in both positions. In one position, the parts make 13 figures. Turn the disc one step and each figure is joined to the parts its neighbour had, so the same arms, legs and heads make up only 12 figures."
resolution:
  - "Nothing vanishes. The total amount of drawing, all the arms, legs, heads and flags, is the same in both positions. With 13 figures, each one is missing a small slice. With 12, each one is a little bigger, because it has gained a slice from its neighbour. The difference is spread so evenly that no single figure looks wrong. Martin Gardner called this “the principle of concealed distribution”."
  - "This is a different mechanism from the [missing square puzzle](missing-square-puzzle). There, a hidden sliver of area is the answer: the outline is not the shape it seems. Here, the area is the same and nothing is hidden at the edges. What changes is how the drawing is divided up into figures. Gardner kept the two kinds apart in his book Mathematics, Magic and Mystery (1956), with Get Off the Earth among the vanishing figures and the [chessboard paradox](chessboard-paradox) and Curry’s triangle among the vanishing areas."
finePrint: "Loyd offered prizes of $5 to $100 for the best explanation of one of these puzzles. The winners’ names were published, but their explanations were not."
