problem: "A lamp is switched on and off faster and faster for two minutes. Is it on or off at the end?"
claimA: "A lamp must be either on or off when the two minutes are up."
claimB: "If every on is followed by an off and vice versa, there is no end state."
verdict: "The pattern says nothing about the end, so there is no contradiction."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that the switching story, told up to the end, also decides what happens at the end."
statusNote: "the second claim is wrong. The switching only covers times before two minutes, so it rules nothing out at two minutes."
setup: "A lamp starts off. Someone switches it on. After one minute they switch it off. After another half minute they switch it on. After a quarter of a minute, off again. Each wait is half the one before, so the waits shrink without end. They add up to exactly two minutes, so every switch happens before the two minutes are up. Now look at the lamp at two minutes. James F. Thomson posed this in 1954. His reasoning was that it cannot be on, because every time it was turned on it was at once turned off again, and it cannot be off, because it was first turned on and every time it was turned off it was at once turned on again. But a lamp has to be one or the other."
whyWrong: "The story only tells us what happens before the two-minute mark. Each switch has a next switch, so there is no last switch to leave the lamp in a final state. It is natural to think the pattern must decide the answer, but nothing in it does."
resolution:
  - 'Paul Benacerraf pointed out in 1962 that Thomson''s description only says what the lamp does at times before two minutes. It says nothing about the lamp at two minutes. You can add "it is on" or "it is off" to the story, and neither makes it contradict itself. So Thomson''s argument that the lamp is impossible does not work.'
  - "That is also why the story does not settle the final state. The pattern on, off, on, off, forever has no limit that would pick a winner, and nothing in the description says what happens at the two-minute mark itself. You could say the lamp is half on, but Thomson himself pointed out that this means nothing for a lamp."
  - "The physical side is different. Some physical set-ups that switch more and more quickly, such as certain circuits built around a bouncing ball, have been shown to end up clearly on or clearly off, depending on how they are built. So the final state depends on the machine, not on the pattern alone."
finePrint: 'This entry is marked Explained because the trap is agreed to be an incomplete description and not a contradiction. John Earman and John Norton, writing on physical versions of the lamp, conclude with Benacerraf that it is "not a matter of paradox but of an incomplete description". Physical models using a bouncing ball can be built to end with the lamp on or with it off, so the ending depends on the set-up. Whether an endless sequence of tasks can be carried out in a finite time at all is a separate question, not covered here.'
