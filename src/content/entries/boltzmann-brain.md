problem: "If a universe produces far more brief brains with false memories than people with real pasts, should an observer expect to be one of those brains?"
claimA: "In some cosmological models, brief brains with false memories far outnumber evolved observers, so any observer should expect to be one."
claimB: "Observers in an orderly universe can trust their memories and their view of cosmic history."
verdict: "Some cosmological models, taken at face value, predict that most observers are chance fluctuations with false memories. Many cosmologists regard such a model as problematic. They disagree over which assumption or part of the model has to change, and each main proposed fix faces a serious objection and none is widely accepted."
whatBreaks: "Whether to change the universe’s long-term behaviour, the production or counting of observers, or the assumption that an observer should be typical."
statusNote: "models dominated by these observers undermine trust in the evidence used to support them. The main proposed fixes remain contested."
setup: "Imagine a brain briefly forming by chance in otherwise empty space. It has memories of a life that never happened, then disappears. Some models of a universe lasting for ever predict so many such brains that they vastly outnumber observers who evolved on planets."
whyWrong: "If an observer should expect to be typical, it should expect to be one of those brains. Its memories and observations would then be unreliable, including the evidence used to support the model. The theory seems to undermine the grounds for believing it."
resolution:
  - "This is a problem for certain cosmological models, not evidence that random brains have been observed. Many cosmologists treat a prediction of overwhelmingly many such observers as a sign that something in the model or its assumptions needs changing."
  - "The difficulty is choosing what to change. The answers below each face serious objections, and there is no widely accepted fix."
positions:
  - title: "The universe changes first"
    body: "Let empty space decay, or dark energy change, before random brains dominate. Cost: this requires particular long-term physics and timing that have not been established."
  - title: "Empty space produces no brains"
    body: "Some quantum accounts say empty expanding space does not undergo the kind of events needed. Cost: the argument depends on a contested interpretation of quantum theory."
  - title: "Count observers differently"
    body: "Choose a rule for comparing observer numbers in an infinite universe. Cost: there is no agreed counting rule, and different rules give different answers."
  - title: "Do not expect to be typical"
    body: "Reject the assumption that an observer is a random sample of all observers. Cost: critics argue that this weakens the use of observations to distinguish cosmological theories."
finePrint: "The historical idea began with fluctuations away from thermal equilibrium, proposed to explain the universe’s ordered past. The brain version is modern. The argument depends on assumptions about fluctuations, lifetime, observers and their typicality; it does not apply to every model of the universe. Carroll calls models that undermine their own evidence cognitively unstable. The timing required by decay proposals and the “measure problem” of comparing infinite populations are technical matters omitted here. The connection to [Loschmidt’s paradox](loschmidts-paradox) is the attempt to explain a low-entropy past by chance."
