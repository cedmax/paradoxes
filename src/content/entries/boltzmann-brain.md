problem: "If a universe that lasts for ever produces far more lone brains by chance than evolved observers, why trust that you are not one of them?"
claimA: "In a universe that lasts for ever with a fixed amount of dark energy, random fluctuations produce far more lone brains with false memories than evolved observers, so any observer should expect to be one of those brains."
claimB: "Observers who find themselves in an orderly universe can trust that their memories, and their view of a long cosmic history, are real."
verdict: "Some cosmological models, taken at face value, predict that most observers are chance fluctuations with false memories. Many cosmologists regard such a model as problematic. They disagree over which assumption or part of the model has to change, and each main proposed fix faces a serious objection and none is widely accepted."
whatBreaks: "Something in the package that produces the clash: a universe that lasts for ever with fixed dark energy, the idea that empty space keeps fluctuating, the way observers are counted, or the rule that an observer should expect to be typical. Each fix drops one of these, and each pays a cost."
statusNote: "many cosmologists regard a model that predicts mostly Boltzmann brains as problematic. The dispute is about what to conclude from models that predict such brains, and how to make those models compatible with reliable observation. Each main fix, whether letting the universe decay, denying that empty space fluctuates, counting observers another way or dropping the assumption that observers are typical, faces a serious objection, and none is widely accepted."
setup: "In the 1890s Ludwig Boltzmann offered an answer to a question left open by [Loschmidt’s paradox](loschmidts-paradox): why was entropy, a measure of disorder, so low in the past? In Craig Callender’s summary, Boltzmann treated the observable universe as a natural statistical fluctuation away from equilibrium in a vastly larger universe. Equilibrium is the state of maximum disorder, where nothing much happens. Given enough time, even an equilibrium universe will now and then fluctuate, by chance, into a more ordered state, and observers could only find themselves in such a patch. In an 1895 letter to Nature, Boltzmann credited the idea to “my old assistant, Dr. Schuetz”. In 1931 Arthur Eddington pointed out a flaw. A large fluctuation is far less likely than a small one, and the reasoning needs only observers to exist, not a whole ordered universe around them. So the most likely fluctuation that contains an observer is the smallest one that will do."
whyWrong: "The modern version comes from cosmology. If the dark energy that makes the universe expand faster is a fixed property of space, called a cosmological constant, the universe ends up as nearly empty space expanding for ever. Sean Carroll notes that this state has a temperature, so it can produce random fluctuations, and it lasts for ever. Over endless time, even absurdly unlikely fluctuations happen again and again. In 2004 Andreas Albrecht and Lorenzo Sorbo put the consequence plainly: “The most likely fluctuation consistent with everything you know is simply your brain”, which then dissolves back into chaos. They noted this is “sometimes called the ‘Boltzmann’s Brain’ paradox”. Counted over all time, such brains, complete with false memories of a past that never happened, would vastly outnumber observers who evolved on planets. If an observer should expect to be typical, it should expect to be one of them. Its memories and its view of the sky would then be fake, including the evidence that led it to the theory in the first place."
resolution:
  - "Nobody in the debate claims that such brains exist. The argument is used against cosmological models: a model that predicts mostly Boltzmann brains is taken to be faulty. In 2002 Lisa Dyson, Matthew Kleban and Leonard Susskind argued, in terms of recurrences (the contents of the universe returning by chance to earlier arrangements) rather than brains, that in a universe with a true cosmological constant a universe like ours would be extraordinarily unlikely. Their conclusion was that “perhaps the only reasonable conclusion is that we do not live in a world with a true cosmological constant.”"
  - "In 2017 Sean Carroll argued that observations do not rule such observers out. The trouble, in his words, is that theories which predict them are “cognitively unstable”: they “cannot simultaneously be true and justifiably believed”, because believing them would undercut the evidence for them. He recommends setting such theories aside even before looking at the data."
  - "That tells physicists something has to give, but not what. Several fixes have been proposed. Each costs something big, and none is widely accepted."
positions:
  - title: "The universe does not last for ever as it is"
    body: "If empty space decays into some other state before brains can form, or if dark energy fades away instead of being constant, the endless supply of fluctuations is cut off. Dyson, Kleban and Susskind leaned this way in 2002. Cost: the decay has to come soon by cosmic standards. Don Page calculated that the universe would then be more likely than not to decay within about 19 billion years, and proposed ways of making that happen rely on physics not seen, such as a top quark heavier than the one measured."
  - title: "Empty space does not really fluctuate"
    body: "Boddy, Carroll and Pollack argued in 2015 that in the quantum state of empty, expanding space nothing really happens over time, so it cannot produce brains. Cost: Carroll himself calls the argument controversial, and it depends on a particular way of understanding measurement in quantum theory, which is itself unsettled."
  - title: "Count observers another way"
    body: "In an infinite universe there are infinitely many of every kind of observer, so comparing their numbers needs a rule for counting, called a measure. Some measures, proposed by Linde and colleagues among others, give few Boltzmann brains. Cost: there is no agreed measure, the answer changes with how the infinite counts are cut off, and a measure picked partly to avoid the brains rests on a problem that is still open."
  - title: "Do not assume observers are typical"
    body: "James Hartle and Mark Srednicki argued in 2007 that there is no evidence humans are a random pick from all observers, and that a theory should not be thrown out just because humans would be unusual in it. Cost: Don Page replied that without some such assumption, observations “would count for nothing” in choosing between theories that predict many observers, and “much of cosmology would cease to be an observational science.”"
finePrint: "This entry is related to [Loschmidt’s paradox](loschmidts-paradox), and it takes one of that puzzle’s loose ends further. Loschmidt’s paradox asks why entropy rises toward the future when the laws work both ways. One answer is that the universe began in a very special, low-entropy state. Boltzmann’s fluctuation idea tried to explain that state by chance, and the Boltzmann brain argument shows why chance gives the wrong kind of observer. The status differs from the anchor’s: there, expert camps each give up a different claim, while here there is broad agreement that something is wrong but no fix that most accept, and each faces a serious objection. Boltzmann himself did not talk about brains; the brain version is modern. The physics of fluctuations in expanding space, and of counting observers in an infinite universe, is heavily simplified here."
