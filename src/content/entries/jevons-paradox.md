problem: "Can making a resource cheaper to use per job cause people to use more of it overall?"
claimA: "If a machine gets more efficient at using a resource, total use of that resource must fall."
claimB: "After steam engines became more efficient, England’s coal use soared."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that the amount of work done stays the same when it gets cheaper. It goes up, and sometimes by more than the saving."
statusNote: "when lower costs raise demand by more than the saving per job, total use of the resource rises. Economists agree this can happen but argue about how often, and in a single market the rebound is usually below 100 percent."
setup: "In his 1865 book The Coal Question, the economist William Stanley Jevons noted that England’s coal use soared after James Watt’s steam engine improved on Thomas Newcomen’s design. Watt’s engine got more work from each lump of coal. Jevons argued that it is a confusion to suppose that economical use of fuel means less fuel is used."
whyWrong: "The first claim treats the amount of work to be done as fixed. If the work is fixed, a more efficient machine needs less fuel. But better efficiency also lowers the cost of each job, and people buy more of what gets cheaper. The extra jobs need fuel too."
resolution:
  - "The effect is called a rebound. If the cost falls by half and people respond by doing more than twice as much, total fuel use rises. The size of the rebound depends on how strongly demand responds to price. The paradox occurs when the rebound is more than 100 percent of the efficiency gain."
  - "So the first claim is false in some conditions and the second is true. It needs three things: a technology that makes use more efficient, a lower price as a result, and a big rise in demand when the price falls. Farming is a case where it does not hold. Productivity gains lowered food prices without a large rise in food demand, and farm work fell from 40 percent of Americans in 1900 to under 2 percent in 2024."
finePrint: "In the 1980s Daniel Khazzoom and Leonard Brookes revived the idea for society’s total energy use, and Harry Saunders named it the Khazzoom-Brookes postulate in 1992. In a single market the rebound is usually below 100 percent, so efficiency usually does reduce use there. At the level of a whole economy, cheaper energy can speed growth and raise total use. Gillingham and colleagues (2013) argue the rebound effect is overplayed, and the debate is open. Common policy responses pair efficiency with measures that keep the cost of use from falling."
