problem: "A coin game has an infinite average prize. Why would nobody pay much to play it?"
claimA: "Since the average prize is endless, you should be willing to pay any price to play."
claimB: "No sensible person would pay more than a modest sum to play."
verdict: "The game has an infinite average payout but seems worth only a modest entry fee. Proposed answers change the value rule or the ideal game, and none is accepted as the whole answer."
whatBreaks: "The rule that a bet is worth its average payout. Each fix either changes the rule, changes the game, or drops it, and each pays a cost."
statusNote: "each fix has a known cost. Counting what money is worth to you can be beaten by a game with bigger prizes, a limited bank leaves the idealised game untouched, ignoring tiny chances raises the question of why any very improbable outcome should be ignored, and replacing the average means giving up expected value as the rule for decisions. None is accepted as the whole answer, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s survey ends without settling the question."
setup: "A casino tosses a fair coin until the first head. A head on toss one pays 2 pounds; on toss two, 4; on toss three, 8. The prize keeps doubling. How much is it worth paying to play?"
whyWrong: "A one-half chance of 2 pounds contributes 1 pound to the average. A one-quarter chance of 4 contributes another 1. Every possible stopping round adds 1, so the average is infinite. Yet seven games in eight pay 8 pounds or less. Paying everything to play once seems unreasonable. Ian Hacking wrote in 1980 that “few of us would pay even $25 to enter such a game”. The 2004 Stanford Encyclopedia survey quotes this as an example of the modest prices commentators accept."
resolution:
  - "A larger amount of money need not be proportionally more valuable to a person. Accounting for that can give a finite value to this game. But faster-growing prizes challenge any value scale with no upper limit."
  - "A real casino also has a limited bank. Roughly, a million-pound bank supports about 20 rounds of doubling and an average prize of about 20 pounds. A billion-pound bank gives about 30 pounds; a billion-billion-pound bank, about 60. The exact figures depend on how the last round is handled. This makes the real game finite, but does not settle how to judge the ideal unlimited game. Other answers change how small chances or averages guide decisions."
positions:
  - title: "Money has diminishing value"
    body: "Value each extra pound less as wealth grows. Cost: faster-growing prizes restore the difficulty for value scales with no upper limit, while a ceiling needs justification."
  - title: "Limit the casino’s bank"
    body: "Use the finite prizes a real casino could pay. Cost: this changes the ideal game instead of settling its decision rule."
  - title: "Ignore tiny chances"
    body: "Disregard sufficiently unlikely outcomes. Cost: a principled threshold is hard to defend; very unlikely events still happen."
  - title: "Use another decision rule"
    body: "Look beyond the average over all possible outcomes, for example to a player’s results over time. Cost: this gives up or changes the familiar expected-value rule, and its justification is disputed."
finePrint: "Nicolaus Bernoulli posed an early version in 1713. Cramer and Daniel Bernoulli developed replies based on how money is valued. The coin game has an infinite mathematical expectation, not a guaranteed large payout. Limited-bank calculations depend on how the maximum prize is handled. A mere belief that an unlimited promise might be honoured can restore an infinite expectation in some formulations. Further puzzles about infinite expectations remain."
