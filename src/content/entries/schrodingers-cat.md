title: "Schrödinger's cat"
titleAccent: "cat"

status: unreconciled
simplification: light
noMaths: true
order: 7
written: true
problem: "If quantum rules say an atom can be in two states at once, what does that mean for a cat whose life depends on it?"
claimA: "Quantum theory says an atom that may or may not have decayed is in a mix of both until it is measured, and it works for anything made of atoms."
claimB: "A cat is either alive or dead, never some mix of the two."
verdict: "Every reading of quantum theory pays a big price, and there is no consensus on which is right."
whatBreaks: "How the quantum rules for tiny things connect to the one definite world we see."
statusNote: "the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says there is no consensus on what quantum theory tells us about the world. The Copenhagen reading is often called the common view, but the encyclopedia notes that different authors mean different things by it, and it carries a big cost like every other reading. So no single fix is widely accepted as the answer."
setup: "In 1935 Erwin Schrödinger described a thought experiment. A cat is sealed in a steel box with a tiny amount of radioactive material, a Geiger counter, and a flask of poison. If an atom decays during the hour, the counter trips a device that breaks the flask, and the cat dies. The chance of a decay in that hour is one half. Quantum theory describes the atom with a mathematical state that includes both possibilities, decayed and not decayed, until it is measured."
whyWrong: "Schrödinger did not mean it as a claim that cats are half dead. He built the example to show how odd one way of reading quantum theory seems, if you take it literally. The atom's state is linked to the cat's, so the cat's state would be a mix of alive and dead too. He called such cases quite ridiculous. The point of the story is the question it asks: at what point does a mix of possibilities become one definite result?"
resolution:
  - "This question is called the measurement problem, and there is no agreed answer. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says there is no consensus on what the success of quantum theory tells us about the world. Its summary of the main approaches is that they all fit the evidence we have so far. This is why the entry is Unreconciled: each answer either leaves something vague, adds something hard to accept, or changes the theory itself."
positions:
  - title: "Measurements must be described classically"
    body: "This is the Copenhagen view of Niels Bohr. He held that quantum theory is complete, but that any measuring set-up must be described in everyday, classical terms. On his view there is no riddle: the cat would be dead or alive long before anyone opens the box. Cost: it is vague about where the line between the quantum and the classical lies, and the theory itself does not draw it."
  - title: "Nothing ever collapses"
    body: "The many-worlds view says all outcomes happen, in separate branches that no longer affect each other. Cost: it needs a vast number of equally real branches, and it struggles to explain what probability means when every outcome happens."
  - title: "Quantum theory is incomplete"
    body: "Bohmian mechanics adds detail the standard theory leaves out: particles always have definite positions, guided by the quantum state, so the cat is always either alive or dead. Cost: these hidden positions are linked instantly across any distance, which needs a special notion of “at the same time” that sits badly with relativity."
  - title: "Superpositions really collapse"
    body: "Collapse theories change the rules so that a mix falls into one outcome by itself once a system gets big enough. Cost: it changes the basic law of quantum theory by adding new physics. That makes it testable in principle, but no experiment has yet shown it."
finePrint: "This is a simplified summary of a long debate, and these views are only some of those on offer. No experiment has put anything near the size of a cat into such a mix. Physicists have made such states with light, single ions, and tiny objects. In 2023 a group at ETH Zurich put a sapphire crystal of 16 micrograms, a few hundred million billion atoms (Scientific American gives about 10^17; a count from its weight gives about 5 × 10^17), into such a mix, the biggest reported at the time. Do not read the story as a settled claim that the cat is both alive and dead. That is only one reading, and some of the views above reject it."
