title: "Schrödinger's cat"
titleAccent: "cat"
chapter: time-physics
status: disputed
simplification: light
noMaths: true
order: 7
written: true
problem: "If quantum rules say an atom can be in two states at once, what does that mean for a cat whose life depends on it?"
claimA: "Quantum theory says an atom that may or may not have decayed is in a mix of both until it is measured, and it works for anything made of atoms."
claimB: "A cat is either alive or dead, never some mix of the two."
verdict: "Physicists still disagree about what the theory says is real."
whatBreaks: "How the quantum rules for tiny things connect to the one definite world we see."
statusNote: "it is a real puzzle. The main interpretations all fit the evidence so far, and experts disagree on which to accept."
setup: "In 1935 Erwin Schrödinger described a thought experiment. A cat is sealed in a steel box with a tiny amount of radioactive material, a Geiger counter, and a flask of poison. If an atom decays during the hour, the counter trips a device that breaks the flask, and the cat dies. The chance of a decay in that hour is one half. Quantum theory describes the atom with a mathematical state that includes both possibilities, decayed and not decayed, until it is measured."
whyWrong: "Schrödinger did not mean it as a claim that cats are half dead. He built the example to show how odd one way of reading quantum theory seems, if you take it literally. The atom's state is linked to the cat's, so the cat's state would be a mix of alive and dead too. He called such cases quite ridiculous. The point of the story is the question it asks: at what point does a mix of possibilities become one definite result?"
resolution:
  - "This question is called the measurement problem, and there is no agreed answer. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says there is no consensus on what the success of quantum theory tells us about the world. Its summary of the main approaches is that they all fit the evidence we have so far."
positions:
  - title: "Quantum theory is incomplete"
    body: "The Copenhagen view, associated with Niels Bohr, treats measurements as part of the physics and describes results in everyday terms. Bohr saw no riddle: the cat would be alive or dead before anyone looks. Other incomplete-picture views add hidden details, as in Bohmian mechanics. Cost: it leaves open what is really going on with the atom before measurement."
  - title: "Superpositions really collapse"
    body: "Collapse theories change the rules so that a mix falls into one outcome by itself once a system gets big enough. Cost: it adds new physics, and it predicts small differences from standard quantum theory that experiments could in principle detect."
  - title: "Nothing ever collapses"
    body: "The many-worlds view says all outcomes happen, in separate branches that no longer affect each other. Cost: it needs a huge number of real branches, which some find hard to accept."
finePrint: "This is a simplified summary of a long debate, and these views are only some of those on offer. No experiment has put anything near the size of a cat into such a mix. Physicists have made such states with light, single ions, and tiny mechanical devices of the order of ten trillion atoms. Do not read the story as a settled claim that the cat is both alive and dead. That is only one reading, and some of the views above reject it."
