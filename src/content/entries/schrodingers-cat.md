problem: "If quantum rules say an atom can be in two states at once, what does that mean for a cat whose life depends on it?"
claimA: "Quantum theory applies to anything made of atoms, so in principle a cat can be in a mix of alive and dead."
claimB: "A cat must be either alive or dead."
verdict: "The cat tests how a quantum combination of alternatives becomes one observed result. The main interpretations make different changes, and there is no consensus on which is right."
whatBreaks: "How to combine a complete quantum description of the box with one definite outcome. The interpretations change different assumptions."
statusNote: "each main answer carries a big cost. Bohr’s view is vague about where the quantum world ends, many-worlds needs countless equally real branches and, critics say, struggles to make sense of statistical testing, Bohmian mechanics sits badly with relativity, and collapse theories add new physics that no experiment has yet shown. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says there is no consensus on what quantum theory says about the world."
setup: "A cat is sealed in a box. A radioactive atom has a one-half chance of decaying within an hour. If it decays, a detector releases poison and the cat dies. Quantum theory links the atom’s alternatives to the cat: atom not decayed and cat alive, together with atom decayed and cat dead."
whyWrong: "A superposition is a quantum combination, not merely ignorance about which result is already settled. Applying it to the whole box seems to conflict with the definite cat that an observer finds. Schrödinger used the story to challenge how the theory was being interpreted, not to claim that a cat is literally half dead."
resolution:
  - "This is the measurement problem: how the quantum description relates to one observed result. Opening the box does not by itself explain where or how that result arose."
  - "Interaction with the surroundings can hide observable interference between alternatives. That process, called decoherence, does not by itself choose among the interpretations below. The [Wigner’s friend](wigners-friend) puzzle adds an observer inside the box."
positions:
  - title: "Describe measurements classically"
    body: "Bohr requires measuring arrangements to be described in ordinary, classical terms. Cost: the quantum rules themselves do not draw an agreed boundary between that description and the quantum one."
  - title: "Every outcome happens"
    body: "Different branches contain different definite outcomes. Cost: explaining probability and statistical evidence is difficult when every outcome occurs."
  - title: "Add definite properties"
    body: "Bohmian mechanics gives particles definite positions guided by the quantum state. Cost: its distant links are difficult to fit with relativity."
  - title: "Change the quantum rules"
    body: "Collapse theories make a superposition select one outcome through new physical rules. Cost: they change the theory, and no experiment has established that such collapse occurs."
finePrint: "Schrödinger proposed the thought experiment in 1935. The description of the whole box assumes ordinary quantum evolution without collapse. Bohr’s complementarity differs from some later uses of the label Copenhagen interpretation. The listed positions are broad summaries, and they do not exhaust the options. Experiments on much smaller systems than a cat test superpositions, but the thought experiment is not a settled claim that a cat experiences being alive and dead."
