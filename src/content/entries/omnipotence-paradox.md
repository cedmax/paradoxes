problem: "Can an all-powerful being make a stone so heavy that it cannot lift it?"
claimA: "Being all-powerful means being able to do anything, even the logically impossible."
claimB: "Nothing can make a stone it cannot lift and then lift that stone."
verdict: "The first claim is wrong. All-powerful does not mean able to do the logically impossible."
whatBreaks: "The idea that all-powerful means able to do absolutely anything, even what contradicts itself."
statusNote: "all-powerful does not mean able to do the logically impossible, so a stone its maker cannot lift is no real task, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy treats the puzzle as answered."
setup: "Suppose there is a being with unlimited power. Ask it to make a stone so heavy that it cannot lift it. If it can make such a stone, there is something it cannot lift, so its power has a limit. If it cannot make the stone, there is something it cannot make, so its power has a limit. Either way, it seems not to be all-powerful."
whyWrong: "We usually take all-powerful to mean able to do anything. The stone puzzle turns that against itself, because the task asks the being to be both able to lift anything and unable to lift one particular thing. It looks like a fair question about power, and no answer feels free."
resolution:
  - "The standard reply is that all-powerful cannot mean able to do the logically impossible. Thomas Aquinas already held that God could not make things contrary to the principles of logic, geometry and arithmetic. C. S. Lewis compared a stone too heavy for God to lift to a square circle. George Mavrodes called such tasks pseudo-tasks: they sound like tasks, but describing them contradicts itself, so failing to do them is no limit."
  - "The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy reaches the same place by asking what kind of being it is. If the being is all-powerful by its very nature and cannot stop being so, then a stone it cannot move is impossible, and an all-powerful being need not bring about the impossible. If the being is all-powerful but not necessarily so, it could make the stone and move it while all-powerful, and afterwards give up being all-powerful, after which the stone is too heavy for it. Either way, the stone shows no limit on what an all-powerful being can do."
  - "So the claim that fails is that all-powerful means able to do anything at all, including the self-contradictory. Once that goes, the stone is not a counterexample."
finePrint: "Wikipedia presents a range of replies, while the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy treats the puzzle as answered. One reply rejects the second claim instead. Harry Frankfurt, following Descartes, argued that if God can do one logically impossible thing, such as making the stone, there is no reason he could not do a second and lift it. Wikipedia also notes that if a being can give up its power, this raises the question of whether it was ever truly all-powerful, or just capable of great power. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy states the puzzle with a being called Jane rather than God."
