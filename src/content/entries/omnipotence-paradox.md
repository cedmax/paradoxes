title: "Omnipotence paradox"
titleAccent: paradox
chapter: self-reference
status: disputed
simplification: light
order: 5
written: true
noMaths: true
problem: "Can an all-powerful being make a stone so heavy that it cannot lift it?"
claimA: "An all-powerful being can do anything, including make a stone it cannot lift."
claimB: "An all-powerful being can lift anything, so there is no stone it cannot lift."
verdict: "Philosophers agree it is a puzzle and offer several different ways out."
whatBreaks: "The simple idea that all-powerful means able to do absolutely anything."
statusNote: "experts agree it is a real puzzle but disagree on which claim to give up."
setup: "Suppose there is a being with unlimited power. Ask it to make a stone so heavy that it cannot lift it. If it can make such a stone, there is something it cannot lift, so its power has a limit. If it cannot make the stone, there is something it cannot make, so its power has a limit. Either way, it seems not to be all-powerful."
whyWrong: "We usually take all-powerful to mean able to do anything. The stone puzzle turns that against itself, because the task asks the being to be both able to lift anything and unable to lift one particular thing. It looks like a fair question about power, and no answer feels free."
resolution:
  - "The most common reply is that all-powerful cannot mean able to do the logically impossible. Thomas Aquinas already held that a power to do anything does not include things that contradict themselves, and C. S. Lewis compared a stone too heavy for God to lift to a square circle. George Mavrodes called such tasks pseudo-tasks: they sound like tasks, but describing them contradicts itself, so failing to do them is no limit."
  - "Others disagree. Harry Frankfurt, following Descartes, argued that an all-powerful being could do even the impossible: make the stone and also lift it. A third kind of answer depends on whether the being is all-powerful by its very nature or only for a while. Matthew Whittle suggested a being could make itself no longer all-powerful by creating the stone, so it was all-powerful until then."
  - "None of these has won general agreement, which is why the entry is marked Disputed. The fight is over what all-powerful should mean, not over any hidden error in the logic."
positions:
  - title: Power does not cover contradictions
    body: "All-powerful means able to do anything that can be done, and a stone it cannot lift is not something that can be. Cost: you have to say what counts as doable, and critics think that quietly shrinks the idea."
  - title: It can do even the impossible
    body: "An all-powerful being could make the stone and still lift it. This is Frankfurt's reply, following Descartes. Cost: it gives up the ordinary rules of logic for this one case."
  - title: It depends on the kind of power
    body: "If the being is all-powerful only for a time, it could make the stone and lose that power afterwards. Cost: it describes a being that can stop being all-powerful, which many would not call all-powerful."
finePrint: "The stone version is one of several ways of putting the puzzle, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy states it with a being called Jane rather than God. It names two main replies, depending on whether the being is all-powerful by its nature or only by accident. The positions above are simplified, and the entry does not cover every proposed account of all-powerful."
