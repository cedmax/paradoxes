problem: "Can an all-powerful being make a stone so heavy that it cannot lift it?"
claimA: "An all-powerful being has no limits."
claimB: "Some tasks are impossible for anyone, because they contradict themselves."
verdict: "On the standard definition, being all-powerful does not include doing the logically impossible."
whatBreaks: "The idea that all-powerful means able to do absolutely anything, even what contradicts itself."
statusNote: "all-powerful does not mean able to do the logically impossible, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy gives two routes that need no such power. For a being that is all-powerful by its very nature, a stone it cannot lift is impossible, so making one is no real task. A being that is all-powerful but could stop being so can make the stone, lift it, and then give up its power."
setup: "Suppose there is a being with unlimited power. Ask it to make a stone so heavy that it cannot lift it. If it can make such a stone, there is something it cannot lift, so its power has a limit. If it cannot make the stone, there is something it cannot make, so its power has a limit. Either way, it seems not to be all-powerful."
whyWrong: "All-powerful is usually taken to mean able to do anything. The stone puzzle turns that against itself, because the task asks the being to be both able to lift anything and unable to lift one particular thing. It looks like a fair question about power, and no answer feels free."
resolution:
  - "The standard reply is that all-powerful cannot mean able to do the logically impossible. Thomas Aquinas already held that God could not make things contrary to the principles of logic, geometry and arithmetic. C. S. Lewis compared a stone too heavy for God to lift to a square circle. George Mavrodes called such tasks pseudo-tasks: they sound like tasks, but describing them contradicts itself, so failing to do them is no limit."
  - "The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy gives two solutions, depending on what kind of being it is. If the being is all-powerful by its very nature and cannot stop being so, then a stone it cannot move is impossible, and an all-powerful being is not required to bring about the impossible. That is the standard reply above. If the being is all-powerful but not necessarily so, the encyclopedia takes a different route. The being can make the stone and move it while all-powerful, then give up being all-powerful, after which it cannot move the stone. Both conditions then hold, but at different times, so nothing self-contradictory is asked of it."
  - "On the first route, the claim that fails is that all-powerful means able to do anything at all, including the self-contradictory. The second route escapes by spreading the two conditions over time instead, but it does not need that claim either. Either way, the stone is not a counterexample."
finePrint: "Wikipedia presents a range of replies, while the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy treats the puzzle as answered. One reply rejects the second claim instead. Harry Frankfurt, following Descartes, argued that if God can do one logically impossible thing, such as making the stone, there is no reason God could not do a second and lift it. Wikipedia also notes that if a being can give up its power, this raises the question of whether it was ever truly all-powerful, or just capable of great power. J. L. Mackie, in 1955, distinguished first-order omnipotence (unlimited power to act) from second-order omnipotence (unlimited power to decide what powers things shall have), so a being with both could limit its own power. Wikipedia also notes that arguments based on the paradox are sometimes used against belief in God. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy states the puzzle with a being called Jane rather than God."
