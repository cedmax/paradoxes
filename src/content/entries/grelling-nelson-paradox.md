problem: "Is the word “heterological” a heterological word?"
claimA: "Every describing word either describes itself or does not."
claimB: "Whichever way “heterological” goes, it contradicts itself."
verdict: "Patching the definition just moves the problem to a neighbouring word."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that every word can be sorted by whether it applies to itself."
statusNote: "the sources checked name no accepted fix, and the obvious patches only move the problem."
setup: "Some describing words describe themselves. “English” is an English word. “Pentasyllabic” has five syllables. Call these autological. Other describing words do not describe themselves. “Long” is a short word. “Monosyllabic” has five syllables. Call these heterological, meaning words that do not describe themselves. Now ask: is “heterological” heterological? If it is, then it describes itself, so it is not. If it is not, then it does not describe itself, which is exactly what heterological means, so it is."
whyWrong: "The sorting looks clean. Take any describing word, check whether it fits itself, and put it in one pile or the other. Nothing about the word “heterological” looks odd. It is defined like any other, so it should land in one pile."
resolution:
  - "There is no agreed fix in the sources I checked. The paradox was put forward by Kurt Grelling and Leonard Nelson in 1908, and it has the same shape as Russell's paradox: swap describing words for collections, and the question becomes whether the collection of things that do not contain themselves contains itself."
  - "One quick fix is to say that “heterological” may not be applied to itself. But then you can invent words such as “not autological” that bring the same trap back. Closing every such gap would mean changing the language more deeply."
  - "This is why the entry is marked Unreconciled rather than Resolved. In set theory the fix is to stop building collections from any description. Here there is no equally settled rule for which descriptions of words are allowed."
finePrint: "Wikipedia says the paradox is sometimes wrongly credited to Hermann Weyl, who only mentioned it. The sources I read do not state whether experts have agreed a standard solution, and they do not compare the possible fixes, so the status here rests on that gap and not on a survey. It is a close cousin of the liar paradox, since both come from a statement turning back on itself."
