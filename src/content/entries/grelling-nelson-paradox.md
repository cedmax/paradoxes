problem: "Is the word “heterological” a heterological word?"
claimA: "Every describing word either describes itself or does not."
claimB: "Whichever way “heterological” goes, it contradicts itself."
verdict: "Patching the definition just moves the problem to a neighbouring word."
whatBreaks: "Either the first claim, that every describing word either describes itself or does not, or the rule that a contradiction cannot be true has to go. Which one goes is what the responses disagree on, and each choice costs something big."
statusNote: "it is a version of the liar paradox about words, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says “no commonly agreed upon solution to the paradoxes of self-reference exists”."
setup: "Some describing words describe themselves. “English” is an English word. “Pentasyllabic” has five syllables. Call these autological. Other describing words do not describe themselves. “Long” is a short word. “Monosyllabic” has five syllables. Call these heterological, meaning words that do not describe themselves. Now ask: is “heterological” heterological? If it is, then it describes itself, so it is not. If it is not, then it does not describe itself, which is exactly what heterological means, so it is."
whyWrong: "The sorting looks clean. Take any describing word, check whether it fits itself, and put it in one pile or the other. Nothing about the word “heterological” looks odd. It is defined like any other, so it should land in one pile."
resolution:
  - "There is no agreed fix. The paradox was put forward by Kurt Grelling and Leonard Nelson in 1908. It has the same shape as Russell's paradox: swap describing words for collections, and the question becomes whether the collection of things that do not contain themselves contains itself. It also belongs with the liar paradox, since it turns on a word being true or not true of itself."
  - "One quick fix is to say that “heterological” may not be applied to itself. But a word that means the same, such as “non-self-describing”, raises the same question, and banning that only leads to another. Wikipedia notes that getting rid of the paradox takes much more change to the language than tidying up the two definitions."
  - "This is why the entry is marked Unreconciled rather than Defused, like Russell's paradox. In set theory the fix is to stop building collections from any description. For describing words, as for the other paradoxes of self-reference, the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says “no commonly agreed upon solution” exists. Each of the main proposals, set out below, has a price."
positions:
  - title: "Words come in levels"
    body: "A word can only describe words from a lower level of language, never words at its own level, so “heterological” cannot be asked about itself. This is Tarski's approach to truth. Cost: ordinary language has no such levels, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says building such a hierarchy is “today by most considered an overly drastic and heavy-handed approach”, since harmless self-reference disappears along with the paradoxes."
  - title: "Some cases are neither"
    body: "“Heterological” is neither true nor false of itself, a gap, as in Kripke's theory of truth. Cost: define a word for “not true of itself, or neither”, and the same trap comes back."
  - title: "Some cases are both"
    body: "“Heterological” is both true and false of itself, and we accept that. Cost: it gives up the rule that nothing is both, and needs a special logic to stop the contradiction spreading."
finePrint: "Wikipedia says the paradox is sometimes wrongly credited to Hermann Weyl, who only mentioned it. The positions above are the main responses to the liar family of paradoxes. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy lists Grelling's paradox among the semantic paradoxes, and says each type of solution it discusses “can be applied to any of the paradoxes of self-reference”. The positions are shown in plain terms, and there are others."
