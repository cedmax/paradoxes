problem: "How can you search for something if you do not already know what it is?"
claimA: "People do inquire into things and find out what they did not know before."
claimB: "Inquiry is pointless or impossible, because you either know what you are looking for, so there is nothing to find out, or you do not, so you could not tell it when you found it."
verdict: "The argument uses “knowing what you are looking for” in two senses, and no single sense makes both of its steps true."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that you either know what you are looking for or you do not. It matters whether that means knowing the question or knowing the answer."
statusNote: "the second claim is wrong. The argument switches between two meanings of “knowing what you are looking for”, and no one meaning makes both steps true. A deeper question about how inquiry gets started remains open."
setup: "In Plato’s dialogue Meno, Meno challenges Socrates. If you know something, he says, you have no need to look into it. If you do not know it, you cannot look into it, because you would not know what you were looking for. So inquiry is either needless or impossible. The puzzle is sometimes called the learner’s paradox."
whyWrong: "Both steps sound reasonable, and the either-or at the start sounds like plain logic. It is plain logic only if “you know what you are looking for” means one thing in both halves. It does not. Knowing what you are looking for can mean knowing the question you want answered, or knowing the answer to it."
resolution:
  - "If it means knowing the answer, the first step is true: someone who already has the answer has nothing to look for. But the second step is false, because someone who does not have the answer can still know the question, such as what the side is of a square twice the size of this one. If it means knowing the question, the second step is true, but the first is false, because knowing the question does not give you the answer. No single meaning makes both steps true, so nothing follows from them."
  - "Plato’s own reply was different. Socrates said the soul is immortal and learned everything before birth, so learning is recollection. To show it, he questions a slave from Meno’s entourage who has no training in geometry, and leads him to find that a square built on the diagonal of a given square has twice its area. A common objection is that Socrates is teaching, since he asks leading questions and brings up the diagonal himself."
finePrint: "The equivocation shows the argument fails as stated, but it does not settle a deeper question about how any inquiry gets started, and that question is still discussed. The dialogue also asks why knowledge is better than true belief, which Socrates answers by saying true beliefs tend to wander off unless they are tied down by working out the reason, and scholars disagree about what that means. This entry follows the equivocation reading set out by S. Marc Cohen, which is one reading among several."
