problem: "An endless line of reapers, each killing a man only if no earlier reaper has, cannot exist. What does that show?"
claimA: "Something can have infinitely many causes, with no first cause among them."
claimB: "If endless chains of causes are possible, then reapers that are each possible on their own can be put together into the endless line."
verdict: "Everyone agrees the line of reapers cannot exist. Experts disagree about whether that shows endless chains of causes are impossible, or only that this one arrangement contradicts itself."
whatBreaks: "Which to give up: the possibility of something having infinitely many causes, or the patchwork step from “endless chains of causes are possible and each reaper is possible” to “the whole line is possible”."
statusNote: "everyone accepts that the set-up cannot exist, because its conditions contradict each other. Causal finitists such as Alexander Pruss and Robert Koons conclude that nothing can have infinitely many causes. Defenders of the unsatisfiable pair diagnosis, such as Joseph Schmid and Alex Malpass, conclude that only this arrangement is ruled out."
setup: "A man is alive at 12pm. The first reaper is set to act at 1pm, the second at 12:30pm, the third at 12:15pm, and so on, each time halving the wait. So infinitely many reapers act between 12pm and 1pm, and there is no first one. Each reaper kills the man if he is still alive at its time, and otherwise does nothing. He cannot survive, because then every reaper would find him alive and strike. But no reaper can be the one who kills him, because there is always an earlier reaper who would have found him alive and killed him first. So the set-up cannot happen. Nicholas Shackel showed in 2005 that this is a matter of logic alone: no line with no first member can have each member act exactly when no earlier member has acted. Puzzles of this shape are called Benardete paradoxes, after J. A. Benardete, whose 1964 book Infinity: An Essay in Metaphysics told of gods who each intend to raise a wall in front of a traveller if he reaches their spot."
whyWrong: "Each ingredient looks safe on its own. One reaper with a conditional job is plainly possible. Possible pieces can usually be put together into a possible whole, an idea philosophers call a patchwork or recombination principle, which Koons draws from David Lewis. Causal finitists such as Koons use it to argue that, unless something like a limit on chains of causes stood in the way, the reapers could be lined up. And a chain of causes with no first member, as in a past with no beginning, is not obviously impossible. Together these say the line of reapers could exist, yet it contradicts itself, so the two claims cannot both be true. Something has to give, and the two main camps give up different things."
resolution:
  - "The disagreement is not about the logic. Graham Oppy, reviewing the debate, notes that no one disputes Shackel’s proof that the set-up is impossible. The dispute is over what follows from it. One camp says it reveals a limit on how causes can be arranged, the other says it reveals only that this particular arrangement is inconsistent."
positions:
  - title: "Nothing has infinitely many causes (causal finitism)"
    body: "Each reaper is possible and pieces of the world can be patched together, so the blame falls on the endless chain of causes. Robert Koons used a version of the paradox in 2014 to argue that the past cannot be infinite, and later argued that the same reasoning supports causal finitism. Alexander Pruss, in Infinity, Causation, and Paradox (2018), argues that the story gives compelling grounds for causal finitism, the view that nothing can have infinitely many causes. Causal finitism allows a past with no beginning as long as no chain of causes runs back without end. Cost: it is a substantial claim about what the world can be like, adopted to block the paradoxes, and Joseph Schmid and Alex Malpass argue that it carries heavy commitments that the rival diagnosis avoids. Others dispute that its commitments are heavy."
  - title: "Only this arrangement is impossible (unsatisfiable pair diagnosis)"
    body: "Shackel’s analysis shows that every Benardete paradox rests on a pair of conditions: the line has no first member, and each member acts if and only if no earlier member has. No line can satisfy both. On this view, defended by Joseph Schmid and Alex Malpass in 2025, that is the whole explanation, and no claim about causes or the past is needed. Allowing endless chains of causes does not make the line possible, so the patchwork step fails: possible pieces need not add up to a possible whole when the whole contradicts itself. Schmid argued in 2024 that patchwork principles cannot support the premise the arguments for a finite past rely on. Cost: causal finitists object that something would then have to stop possible pieces from being combined, which looks like a “mysterious force”, and that patchwork principles which seem plausible elsewhere must fail here. Schmid and Malpass argue that both objections can be answered."
finePrint: "This entry is marked Disputed, while its anchor, [Thomson’s lamp](thomsons-lamp), is marked Explained. The lamp story only leaves its end state undescribed and does not contradict itself. The reaper story does contradict itself, and the open question is what that shows. The unsatisfiable pair diagnosis is itself compatible with causal finitism, so a causal finitist can accept Shackel’s analysis too. The camps differ over whether causal finitism is also needed. Oppy denies that the reapers make a good case for causal finitism, while saying that he does not endorse the unsatisfiable pair diagnosis either. Other replies exist. One says time comes in separate steps, so an hour cannot hold infinitely many reapers, but this does nothing against Benardete’s gods, who raise walls at half a mile, a quarter of a mile, an eighth, and so on. Another rejects any actually infinite collection of things. Koons and Pruss also use their conclusion in arguments that the universe has a first cause. This entry does not take up those arguments."
