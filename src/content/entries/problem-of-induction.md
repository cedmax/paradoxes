problem: "What justifies expecting the future to resemble the past?"
claimA: "We are justified in expecting the unobserved to resemble the observed, as when we expect the sun to rise tomorrow."
claimB: "No argument can justify that expectation: a logical proof is impossible, and an argument from experience assumes what it needs to prove."
whatBreaks: "The idea that expecting the future to resemble the past can be backed by reason alone, without taking something on trust."
statusNote: "the replies say induction can defend itself by its past success, that being justified just means following good inductive standards, that induction is the best method to try, or that we must simply assume nature is uniform."
setup: "Every morning of your life, the sun has risen. You expect it to rise tomorrow. That is an inference from what has been seen to what has not. The philosopher David Hume asked in 1739, in A Treatise of Human Nature, and again in 1748 in An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, what the inference rests on. His answer was a dilemma. A logical proof is out, because it is no contradiction to say the sun will not rise. An argument from experience is out too, because it would have to use the principle that the future will be like the past, and that principle is the very thing in question."
whyWrong: "Nothing looks wrong with expecting the sun to rise, and nothing looks wrong with Hume’s two steps. That is the difficulty. Everyday and scientific reasoning rests on inferences from the observed to the unobserved, called induction. Hume concluded that reason cannot justify them, and that we make them because of custom or habit, which he treated as a natural instinct. If that is so, the confidence of science rests on something that argument cannot support."
resolution:
  - "Philosophers have offered many replies over almost three centuries, and none has won out. Each of the four below is serious, and each has a cost."
positions:
  - title: "Use induction to defend itself"
    body: "Induction has worked in the past, so it can be trusted to work. The circle is harmless, this reply says, because a rule is being followed rather than a claim assumed. Papineau and Cleve defend versions of it. Cost: a counter-inductive rule, which expects the opposite of what has happened, can be defended the same way, and the argument cannot persuade a sceptic."
  - title: "Ordinary standards"
    body: "To be justified just is to follow the standards of good inductive reasoning, so asking whether induction as a whole is justified misunderstands the word. Strawson argued this in 1952. Cost: following the standards does not show that the conclusion is likely to be true, as BonJour argued in 1998."
  - title: "Best method to try"
    body: "No one can promise induction will succeed, but if any method will find the regularities in the long run, induction will, so it is the best one to use. Reichenbach argued this in 1938. Cost: many other methods reach the same long-run result, the aim looks too narrowly about the long run, and the argument depends on how costly it is to try."
  - title: "Accept it as a starting assumption"
    body: "The uniformity of nature is an assumption that all science needs and none can prove. Russell, Burks and Wright take versions of this view. Cost: the assumption has no justification of its own, which Salmon compared to making scientific method a matter of faith."
finePrint: "Hume himself did not conclude that we should stop reasoning inductively. He thought the habit is part of human nature. A separate puzzle, Goodman’s new riddle of induction, asks which patterns count as regular in the first place, and is covered in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy alongside this one."
