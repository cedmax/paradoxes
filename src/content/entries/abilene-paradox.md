problem: How can a whole group agree to do something that not one of them wants?
claimA: If a group does something, at least some of its members must want it.
claimB: Every member can privately prefer another plan, and the group still goes ahead.
verdict: Each person wrongly assumes the others want it, and stays quiet.
whatBreaks: The idea that a group decision shows what its members want.
statusNote: the first claim is wrong, and the mechanism is clear. It is a well-known description, not a formal proof.
setup: 'The management writer Jerry B. Harvey told this story in a 1974 article, "The Abilene Paradox: The Management of Agreement". A family is playing dominoes on a porch in Coleman, Texas, on a hot afternoon. The father-in-law suggests driving to Abilene for dinner. The wife says it sounds great. The husband has doubts because the drive is long and hot, but assumes the others want to go, so he agrees. The mother-in-law says of course she wants to go. The drive is hot, dusty and long, the food is bad, and they get home four hours later, worn out.'
whyWrong: "Back home, someone says it was a great trip. Then the truth comes out. The mother-in-law would rather have stayed in. The husband only went to please the others. The wife went to keep him happy. The father-in-law only suggested it because he thought the rest might be bored. Nobody wanted to go. Each person guessed what the others wanted, guessed wrong, and said yes. Each one thought they were the only one with doubts, so nobody spoke."
resolution:
  - "The puzzle comes from one false step. We assume that when everyone agrees out loud, they agree in private. Here what people said and what they wanted were different, and each person's guess about the others was wrong. The group did not have a shared wish. It had a shared mistake."
  - "Harvey's point was about managing agreement. Groups can fail not by fighting, but by not finding out what members really think. The way out follows from the cause. Someone has to say what they actually want before the group commits, and others have to be able to hear it."
  - "Wikipedia notes that this differs from groupthink. In groupthink people distort their own views. In the Abilene paradox, people cannot see what the others actually prefer."
finePrint: This is a description of a pattern in groups, not a mathematical result, and the story is an anecdote used as a thought experiment. The evidence for how common it is comes mostly from case studies and small surveys, which the sources below describe only briefly. Calling it a paradox is a figure of speech. Nothing in it contradicts itself, once you notice that stated and private preferences can differ.
