problem: How can a whole group agree to do something that not one of them wants?
claimA: If a group does something, at least some of its members must want it.
claimB: Every member can privately prefer another plan, and the group still goes ahead.
verdict: Each person wrongly assumes the others want it, and stays quiet.
whatBreaks: The idea that a group decision shows what its members want.
statusNote: the first claim is wrong, and the mechanism is clear. It is a well-known description, not a formal proof.
setup: "A family agrees to drive a long way for dinner on a hot day. Each person privately wants to stay home, but thinks the others want the trip. Each says yes to avoid disappointing them."
whyWrong: "After a miserable journey, they admit what they wanted. Even the person who suggested it thought the others might be bored. Nobody wanted the plan. Each mistake about the others encouraged the next person to agree."
resolution:
  - "Public agreement does not guarantee private agreement. Here the group acts on mistaken guesses about its members, rather than a shared wish."
  - "Finding out what people actually prefer can prevent the pattern. That requires room to express doubts before a decision is made."
finePrint: This is a description of a pattern in groups, not a mathematical result, and the story is an anecdote used as a thought experiment. The evidence for how common it is comes mostly from case studies and small surveys, which the sources below describe only briefly. Calling it a paradox is a figure of speech. Nothing in it contradicts itself, once you notice that stated and private preferences can differ.
