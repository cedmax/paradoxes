problem: "Every emerald seen so far is green. Why does that not equally support that emeralds will be grue?"
claimA: "Every emerald seen so far has been green, which supports the prediction that future emeralds will be green."
claimB: "Every emerald seen so far has also been grue, which supports the prediction that future emeralds will be grue, and so blue."
verdict: "Experts disagree about what makes “green” fit for predictions and “grue” not."
whatBreaks: "The question of which words can carry a prediction from past cases to future ones, and on what ground."
statusNote: "Wikipedia reports several rival answers, from Goodman’s own idea of entrenchment to natural kinds, and none is presented as settled."
setup: "Nelson Goodman introduced the new riddle in his book Fact, Fiction, and Forecast. He defined “grue” relative to a fixed time t, say 1 January 2036. An object is grue if it is observed before t and is green, or if it is not observed before t and is blue. Every emerald seen before t is green, and so is also grue. On the evidence alone, we could say all emeralds are green, or that all emeralds are grue. The first predicts a green emerald after t. The second predicts a blue one. Goodman’s point was that the evidence for each is exactly the same."
whyWrong: "Goodman treated Hume’s old problem of induction as one that dissolves into a question about how evidence confirms generalisations. The new riddle asks which generalisations are “lawlike”, meaning they can be confirmed and used to predict. The generalisation that all copper conducts electricity is lawlike. The generalisation that all men in a certain room are third sons is only accidental. Wikipedia says that for Goodman the question becomes which predicates are projectible, that is, can be used in lawlike generalisations that serve as predictions."
resolution:
  - "Wikipedia notes that grue and bleen are not predicates we use in everyday life or science, but they apply in the same way as green and blue up to time t. From before t, it is indeterminate which pair will carry predictions forward."
  - "Goodman’s own answer, as Wikipedia reports it, is that predicates are projectible because they are entrenched, which depends on how often they have been successfully projected in the past. Green has a record of successful use and grue has none."
positions:
  - title: Entrenchment (Goodman)
    body: "A predicate carries predictions when it has been used in many successful predictions in the past, as green has and grue has not. Where two hypotheses fit the evidence equally, the one using the better entrenched predicates wins. Cost: the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says this describes how we in fact choose predicates, not a justification of the choice, and that it makes projectibility a matter of the language we use and have used."
  - title: Qualitative against positional predicates (Carnap, Swinburne)
    body: "Predicates that refer to a particular time or place, such as grue, are positional and cannot carry predictions. Predicates like green are purely qualitative and can. Carnap put this forward as a tentative answer, and Richard Swinburne distinguished predicates that need no knowledge of when something is observed from those that do. Cost: the Stanford Encyclopedia says this makes it relative to a language. If grue and bleen are the basic words, green and blue are the positional ones, and the two languages are symmetrical, so no semantic or syntactic test draws the line. Wikipedia reports that Goodman called the reply of appealing to grue’s disjunctive definition question begging."
  - title: Natural kinds (Quine)
    body: "Only predicates that pick out natural kinds can carry predictions, and kinds go with similarity: two green emeralds are more alike than two grue ones. Cost: Quine himself calls the scientific standing of a general notion of similarity or kind dubious, and Wikipedia reports that it remains unclear how to define either in terms of the other."
finePrint: "Wikipedia’s article also discusses further responses. This entry gives three groups of the main ones and does not weigh them against each other. The 2036 date in the setup is only the example used on Wikipedia. The riddle is a relative of [the problem of induction](problem-of-induction), which asks what justifies predicting the future from the past at all."
