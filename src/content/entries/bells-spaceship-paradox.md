problem: "Two spaceships accelerate together with a thread between them. Does the thread break?"
claimA: "Both ships have the same speed at all times, so the whole set-up is squeezed evenly and the thread stays whole."
claimB: "The thread breaks."
verdict: "The gap between the ships does not shrink, but the thread would, so it is pulled apart."
whatBreaks: "The idea that the gap between the ships shrinks by the same length contraction as everything else."
statusNote: "the first claim is wrong. Wikipedia reports that calculations in both frames show the thread breaks, so the evenly squeezed picture fails."
setup: "A delicate thread hangs between two spaceships, at rest at first in a frame called S. They start accelerating in the same direction at the same time and equally, as measured in S, so they have the same velocity at all times as seen from S. Everything in the assembly is subject to the same Lorentz contraction, the shrinking of moving objects in the direction of motion in special relativity. So it seems that the thread should not break. E. Dewan and M. Beran described the set-up in 1959, and it became better known after John Stewart Bell discussed it further in 1976."
whyWrong: "Wikipedia says this argument is incorrect, as shown by Dewan and Beran and later Bell. The distance between the ships does not undergo Lorentz contraction relative to the distance at the start, because in S it is defined to stay the same by the equal and simultaneous acceleration. The thread, however, is a physical object held together by electrostatic forces, and it keeps the same rest length."
resolution:
  - "In frame S, the ships stay a fixed distance apart, but the thread, which is moving, would have to be Lorentz contracted. It cannot shrink while the ships hold the same distance, so a stress builds up until the thread reaches its elastic limit and breaks."
  - "From the frame moving along with the first ship at each moment, the story differs but the result is the same. Relativity of simultaneity means the ships’ accelerations are not simultaneous in that frame, so the rest length between them increases. The thread keeps its rest length, so it is stretched and breaks."
  - "Dewan and Beran concluded that whenever a body is made to move so that all its parts have the same acceleration relative to an inertial frame, the body must in general suffer relativistic stresses. They also answered an objection that a gap between two free ships should behave like the length of a rod. A rod has a fixed rest length that stays the same, so it contracts. The gap between the ships is not rigid in the same way."
  - "The [ladder paradox](ladder-paradox) is another puzzle about length contraction in special relativity."
finePrint: "The article describes only the main argument and the original 1959 reasoning. It also discusses further developments, which are not covered here. A frame in which the ships are momentarily at rest is called a comoving frame, and the claims above about it are those reported by Wikipedia."
