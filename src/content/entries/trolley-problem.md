problem: "Why is diverting a runaway trolley to kill one and save five acceptable to most people, but pushing a stranger in its way not?"
claimA: "If diverting the trolley is right, pushing someone to stop it is right too."
claimB: "Diverting it is right, but pushing someone is wrong."
whatBreaks: "The contested choice is between judging the two cases by their results alone, and finding a morally relevant difference that most people feel but cannot easily state."
statusNote: "the camps are counting only results, the doctrine of double effect, and denying that switching is permissible. In a 2009 survey, 68 percent of professional philosophers would switch."
setup: "A runaway trolley will kill five people on the track ahead unless it is diverted onto a side track, where it will kill one. Most people say it is acceptable to divert it. In a second case, the only way to stop the trolley is to push a large stranger off a bridge in front of it. He will die, and the five will be saved. Resistance to doing this seems strong. Philippa Foot introduced the core idea in 1967, and Judith Jarvis Thomson named it the trolley problem in 1976."
whyWrong: "The numbers are the same in both cases, so if only the results mattered the answers would be the same. People still answer differently, and many surveys show about 90 percent choosing to kill one to save five in the switch case. The puzzle is whether the gap points to a real moral difference or to a feeling that does not deserve trust."
resolution:
  - "Philosophers have defended three main answers. None of them is settled."
positions:
  - title: "Only results count"
    body: "Classical utilitarians hold that diverting the trolley is obligatory, because it is better than doing nothing. If only results count, the push would be justified too. Cost: most people strongly resist pushing the stranger, and critics such as Roger Scruton say this approach reduces moral problems to arithmetic alone."
  - title: "Means and side effects differ"
    body: "In the push, the man’s death is the means of stopping the trolley. In the switch case, the one person’s death is a foreseen side effect of diverting it. The doctrine of double effect says that harm one intends is worse than harm one merely foresees. Cost: it is hard to tell a means from a side effect in other cases, descriptions of what was intended may be shaped by moral judgments, and the page notes that these considerations alone do not show that switching is justified."
  - title: "Neither is permissible"
    body: "Thomson (2008) and Anscombe (1982) argued that switching is not permissible either, and Thomson put the contrary intuition down to inadequate reflection. Cost: it asks people to give up a judgment that many hold with confidence, that switching is permissible."
finePrint: "Earlier versions appeared in a 1905 questionnaire by Frank Chapman Sharp, in Karl Engisch’s 1930 thesis and in Hans Welzel’s 1951 work. Critics argue that the scenario is too extreme to teach much about real morality, and that it measures only part of utilitarian thinking."
