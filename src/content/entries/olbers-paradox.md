problem: "If the universe is full of stars, why is the night sky dark?"
claimA: "In an endless universe full of stars, every line of sight should end on a star, so the whole sky should blaze."
claimB: "The night sky is dark."
verdict: "The universe has a finite age, and it is expanding."
whatBreaks: "The picture of an endless, unchanging universe that has always been there."
statusNote: "one claim is wrong. The universe is not static and eternal, and cosmologists agree on this."
setup: "Look into a forest and you see trees in every direction, because the forest is thick enough that every line of sight hits a trunk. Now picture the universe as an endless forest of stars, spread evenly, unchanging, and always there. Every line of sight from Earth would end on the surface of a star. Far stars look fainter, but there would be more of them, and the effects cancel. The sky should be as bright as the surface of a star. It is not."
whyWrong: "The picture assumes a universe that is endless, unchanging and infinitely old. The real universe is not like that. The paradox is named after the German astronomer Heinrich Olbers, who wrote about it in 1823, but it was posed before him, and Kepler raised the problem in 1610."
resolution:
  - "The first reason is the age of the universe. Light travels at a finite speed, and the universe is not infinitely old: it is roughly 14 billion years old. Light from stars beyond a certain distance has not had time to reach us yet. So only a limited number of stars can be seen, and most lines of sight end in empty dark. Edgar Allan Poe suggested something very like this in 1848, and Lord Kelvin gave a fuller argument in 1901."
  - "The second reason is that space is expanding. Light from distant sources is stretched on its way to us, which makes it redder and weaker. The intense light left over from the Big Bang has been stretched to about 1100 times its original wavelength, so it now arrives as microwaves, which our eyes cannot see."
  - "Both effects matter. Together they make the sky vastly darker than the picture of an eternal, static universe predicts."
finePrint: "Sources differ in emphasis on which factor is bigger. The Usenet Physics FAQ says calculations suggest the finite age is the larger effect. Wikipedia notes that expansion alone would also be enough to darken the sky even in a universe of infinite age. The paradox does not depend on that comparison, because either way the old picture is wrong. Some of this entry is simplified, including the precise sums behind the numbers."
