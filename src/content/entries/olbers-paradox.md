problem: "If the universe is full of stars, why is the night sky dark?"
claimA: "The universe has always existed, unchanging and full of stars, so every line of sight ends on a star."
claimB: "The night sky is dark."
verdict: "The first claim is wrong. The universe has a finite age, it is expanding, and stars do not shine for ever. The finite age and the limited lifetimes of stars matter most, and expansion is a smaller factor."
whatBreaks: "The first claim: the picture of an unchanging universe, full of stars, that has always been there."
statusNote: "the first claim is wrong. The universe is not unchanging and eternal, and cosmologists agree on this, though sources weigh the reasons differently."
setup: "Look into a forest and you see trees in every direction, because the forest is thick enough that every line of sight hits a trunk. Now picture the universe as an endless forest of stars, spread evenly, unchanging, and always there. Every line of sight from Earth would end on the surface of a star. Far stars look fainter, but there would be more of them, and the effects cancel. The sky should be as bright as the surface of a star. It is not."
whyWrong: "The picture assumes a universe that is endless, unchanging and infinitely old. The real universe is not like that. The paradox is named after the German astronomer Heinrich Olbers, who wrote about it in 1823, but it was posed before him, and Kepler raised the problem in 1610."
resolution:
  - "The first reason is the age of the universe. Light travels at a finite speed, and the universe is not infinitely old: it is about 13.8 billion years old. Light from stars beyond a certain distance has not had time to reach us yet. So only a limited number of stars can be seen, and most lines of sight end on no visible star. Edgar Allan Poe suggested something very like this in 1848, and Lord Kelvin gave a fuller argument in 1901."
  - "The second reason is that space is expanding. Light from distant sources is stretched on its way to us, which makes it redder and weaker. The intense light left over from the Big Bang has been stretched to about 1100 times its original wavelength, so it now arrives as microwaves, which our eyes cannot see."
  - "A further point is that stars do not shine for ever. A star can give out only as much energy as its nuclear fuel holds, so each one shines for a limited time. Arpino and Scardigli, citing detailed calculations by Wesson, Valle and Stabell, say this is the main part of the answer: how long galaxies shine sets the brightness of the sky “to order of magnitude”, while expansion changes it only by a factor of two."
  - "These effects all matter. Together they make the sky vastly darker than the picture of an eternal, static universe predicts."
finePrint: "The sources listed here broadly agree on the weighting. The Usenet Physics FAQ says numerical arguments suggest the finite age is the larger effect, and cites Wesson's work. Arpino and Scardigli, citing Wesson and colleagues, say the limited lifetime of galaxies sets the brightness of the sky to order of magnitude, while expansion changes it only by a factor of two. Wikipedia adds a separate point: the redshift from expansion would by itself explain the darkness of the night sky even if the universe were infinitely old. That is a claim about what would happen in an eternal universe, not a claim that expansion is the main reason in the real one. They all agree that the first claim is wrong. Some of this entry is simplified, including the precise sums behind the numbers."
