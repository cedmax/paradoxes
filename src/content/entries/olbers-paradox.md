problem: "If the universe is full of stars, why is the night sky dark?"
claimA: "The universe has always existed, unchanging and full of stars, so every line of sight ends on a star."
claimB: "The night sky is dark."
verdict: "The universe has not always been there unchanged. It has a finite age, it is expanding, and stars do not shine for ever. The finite age and the limited lifetimes of stars matter most."
whatBreaks: "The first claim: the picture of an unchanging universe, full of stars, that has always been there."
statusNote: "the first claim is wrong. The universe is not unchanging and eternal, and cosmologists agree on this, though sources weigh the reasons differently."
setup: "Look into a forest and you see trees in every direction, because the forest is thick enough that every line of sight hits a trunk. Now picture the universe as an endless forest of stars, spread evenly, unchanging, and always there. Every line of sight from Earth would end on the surface of a star. Far stars look fainter, but there would be more of them, and the effects cancel. The sky should be as bright as the surface of a star. It is not."
whyWrong: "The bright-sky prediction assumes an unchanging universe that has always contained shining stars. That is the hidden premise, rather than a consequence of having many distant stars alone."
resolution:
  - "The universe has a finite age, so light has had limited time to reach Earth. Stars and galaxies also shine for limited periods. Most lines of sight therefore do not end on visible stellar surfaces as the eternal picture assumes."
  - "Expansion stretches light to longer wavelengths and reduces its observed energy. It contributes to the answer, but finite age and limited stellar lifetimes are central. The sources differ in how they describe the weighting of these effects."
finePrint: "The sources listed here broadly agree on the weighting. The Usenet Physics FAQ says numerical arguments suggest the finite age is the larger effect, and cites Wesson's work. Arpino and Scardigli, citing Wesson and colleagues, say the limited lifetime of galaxies sets the brightness of the sky to order of magnitude, while expansion changes it only by a factor of two. Wikipedia adds a separate point: the redshift from expansion would by itself explain the darkness of the night sky even if the universe were infinitely old. That is a claim about what would happen in an eternal universe, not a claim that expansion is the main reason in the real one. They all agree that the first claim is wrong. Some of this entry is simplified, including the precise sums behind the numbers."
