problem: "If two particles are linked, can measuring one change the other instantly, however far apart they are?"
claimA: "Nothing done to one particle can instantly change a distant one, so quantum mechanics must leave something about the particles out."
claimB: "Quantum mechanics gives a complete description of the particles, and measuring one gives a definite result correlated with the other’s."
verdict: "Experiments appear to rule out the simplest way of keeping both ordinary locality and definite hidden properties, and physicists disagree about which assumption to give up instead."
whatBreaks: "The contested choice is between completeness of quantum mechanics, the idea that nothing acts instantly at a distance, and other assumptions the experiments also depend on."
statusNote: "experts agree the puzzle is real and disagree on which assumption to drop. Experiments appear to confirm the violations of Bell’s inequalities, but the confirmation is described as not fully conclusive, and several interpretations remain viable."
setup: "In 1935 Albert Einstein, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen published a paper asking whether quantum mechanics gives a complete description of reality. They imagined two systems that have interacted and then moved far apart, with their positions and momenta strictly linked. Measuring one would tell you what you would find on the other. Quantum mechanics says a particle cannot have a definite position and a definite momentum together. Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen argued that, if nothing can act instantly over a distance, the distant particle must already have had definite values, so quantum mechanics must be incomplete."
whyWrong: "Their argument seemed to leave only two options: nothing travels faster than light, and quantum theory is missing something, or quantum mechanics is complete and something acts at a distance. Niels Bohr replied that a measurement on one particle changes the conditions that define what can be predicted about the other. In 1964 John Bell used a version of the set-up to derive inequalities that must hold if the distant particles carry definite properties and nothing acts at a distance, along with other assumptions. Quantum mechanics predicts they can be violated."
resolution:
  - "Experiments of this type appear to confirm that the inequalities are violated, though a few loopholes mean the confirmation is, in the words of the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, not fully conclusive. Physicists still disagree about what that shows, and the main positions are set out below."
positions:
  - title: "Keep locality, give up completeness"
    body: "Einstein’s own preference: nothing acts instantly at a distance, so quantum mechanics is missing definite properties of the particles. Cost: Bell derived inequalities that any such local theory must obey, quantum mechanics predicts violations, and experiments appear to confirm them."
  - title: "Give up locality"
    body: "A theory such as de Broglie–Bohm theory keeps definite properties and allows influences between distant events. Cost: it accepts influences between distant events, which Bohr rejected in the strongest terms in other writings."
  - title: "Give up another assumption"
    body: "Bell’s result also depends on other assumptions, including that experimenters’ choices of what to measure are independent of the particles, and that the particles are separate things. Everettian and relational approaches have been tentatively suggested as ways to be both local and complete. Cost: dropping measurement independence means experimenters’ choices are not independent of what they measure, an escape the source says is generally downplayed as a loophole."
finePrint: "Einstein’s later versions of the argument dropped the reality criterion and framed the issue as a choice between completeness and separability."
