problem: "Can a horn hold a finite amount of paint and still have a surface you could never finish painting?"
claimA: "The horn holds only a limited amount of paint, so a limited amount of paint should be enough."
claimB: "Its surface is endless, so no limited amount of paint can cover it."
verdict: "The second claim is wrong for idealised paint that can be thinned without limit. Endless area can be covered with finite paint if the layer keeps getting thinner."
whatBreaks: "The idea that covering a surface always needs paint in proportion to its area."
statusNote: "for idealised, mathematical paint that can be thinned without limit, the second claim is wrong. A limited amount of it can cover the endless surface, because the layer does not have to stay the same thickness. Wikipedia calls the puzzle “only an apparent paradox caused by an incomplete definition of ‘paint’, or by using contradictory definitions of paint for the actions of filling and painting”."
setup: "Take the curve that starts wide and narrows as it goes, so that at distance x along it the width is in proportion to 1 divided by x. Spin it round to make a horn. It is open at the wide end and stretches off forever, getting thinner but never closing. The shape is called Gabriel's horn, after the angel said to blow the horn on Judgment Day. Two facts about the horn are true. Where the mouth is 2 units wide, the space inside it is exactly pi cubic units, about 3.14. And its surface area has no limit. The part of the horn from x equals 1 to x equals a, a length of a minus 1, has a surface of at least 2 times pi times the natural logarithm of a, and that keeps growing as a gets bigger."
whyWrong: "It sounds as if you could fill the horn with a finite tin of paint, but would need endless paint to coat the same surface. That cannot be right, since the paint touches the wall when you fill the horn. The slip is in the unspoken rule that paint covering a wall must have some set thickness."
resolution:
  - "Take paint in the mathematical sense, which can be thinned without limit. Fill the horn with it and the paint does touch every part of the inner wall. So a finite amount of paint does cover an endless surface. What it cannot do is cover it with a coat of the same thickness all the way along. A coat of fixed thickness over an endless area would need endless paint. The paint in the filled horn is thick near the mouth and gets thinner and thinner down the tube, fast enough that the total stays finite. Wikipedia adds one more assumption: the mathematical paint must also flow at infinite speed, or it would take forever to run down the endless tube."
  - "The horn was studied by Evangelista Torricelli, in a paper written in 1643. It later fed a dispute about infinity in mathematics between Thomas Hobbes and John Wallis. Wallis embraced the new ideas of infinity, while Hobbes held that “infinite” in mathematics can only mean “indefinite”."
  - "What it shows is that area and volume are different things. A shape can have endless area and a limited volume. It does not show that real paint can do the impossible."
finePrint: "The horn is an idealised shape. Wikipedia notes that with real, physical paint the puzzle also vanishes, in a different way. Physical paint has a thickness, so a coat on the outside of the horn would need endless paint. It cannot fill the inside either, because its molecules leave gaps and the horn eventually becomes too narrow for them to flow down. With physical paint neither job can be done, so there is no contradiction. The figures above are for the part of the horn from x equals 1 outward."
