problem: "A man takes a year to write up each day of his life. If he lived for ever, would any day go unwritten?"
claimA: "Even with endless time, a growing backlog leaves some days unwritten."
claimB: "With endless time, every day gets written about."
whatBreaks: "The idea that a backlog which grows for ever must leave some days unwritten for ever."
statusNote: "the first claim is wrong. The backlog grows without end, but any day you pick, say the nth, is written up in the nth year. That is the point Bertrand Russell made in 1903."
setup: "In Laurence Sterne’s comic novel Tristram Shandy, published from 1759 to 1767, the narrator complains that he is a whole year older than when he started, yet has only got as far as his first day of life. So he has 364 more days to write about than when he began, and the more he writes, the more he will have to write. Bertrand Russell, in The Principles of Mathematics (1903), retold this as Tristram having taken two years to write the history of his first two days, and asked what would happen if he lived for ever and kept up the same pace."
whyWrong: "The backlog is real. After 10 years he has written up 10 days but lived about 3,650. After 100 years he has written up 100 days but lived about 36,500. The gap only widens, and at no moment is the book finished. It seems to follow that some part of his life must be left out for good. But that step does not follow."
resolution:
  - "Russell’s answer was that if Tristram lived for ever and never tired of the task, then “no part of his biography would have remained unwritten”. The argument is short. Day 1 is written up in year 1, day 2 in year 2, and in general day n in year n. Any day you can name is the nth day for some n, so it gets its year. No day is skipped."
  - "Both things are true at once. At every moment the book is unfinished, and the unwritten part keeps growing. Yet there is no day that stays unwritten for ever. A backlog that grows without end is not the same as a day that is never reached."
  - "This is the same fact behind [Galileo’s paradox](galileos-paradox). The days of his life and the years of his writing can be matched one to one, the nth day with the nth year, even though the days written about in any stretch of time are only a small part of the days lived in it. Russell used the example to show that with endless collections a part can be matched with the whole. He set it out side by side with [Achilles and the tortoise](achilles-and-the-tortoise), which he treated as the other face of the same question about whole and part."
finePrint: "The story needs an everlasting writer and an endless future, so it is an idealisation, not a plan anyone could carry out. Russell called it “strictly correlative to the Achilles”. Russell’s version is a loose retelling: in the novel, Tristram says a year of writing has covered his first day. The philosopher Karl Popper argued in 1950 that Tristram could never finish, for a different reason: the writing is itself part of his life, and his latest act of writing can never be included in what he has already written. The day counts here use 365 days a year and ignore leap years."
