problem: "Can it be reasonable to prefer a sure million in one choice and a long shot in another, when the two choices are the same underneath?"
claimA: "A rational chooser’s preference between two gambles should not change when the same outcome is added to both."
claimB: "Most people prefer the sure million in one choice and the 10% chance of five million in the other, and Maurice Allais argued that both preferences are reasonable."
verdict: "Experts disagree on whether the theory of rational choice or the choosers should give way."
whatBreaks: "The independence axiom of expected utility theory, or the view that the usual choices are mistakes."
statusNote: "Wikipedia reports that Allais took the independence axiom to be invalid, while others built alternative theories of choice, and one experimenter found results that violated several of those alternatives."
setup: "Maurice Allais designed this choice problem in 1953. People face two choices between gambles. In the first, gamble 1A pays $1 million for certain, while 1B pays $5 million with 10% chance, $1 million with 89% chance and nothing with 1% chance. In the second, gamble 2A pays $1 million with 11% chance and nothing otherwise, while 2B pays $5 million with 10% chance and nothing otherwise. Wikipedia reports that several studies found most people choose 1A over 1B, and 2B over 2A."
whyWrong: "Expected utility theory says a person should pick either 1A and 2A, or 1B and 2B. In both choices, the two gambles give the same result 89% of the time, which is $1 million in the first choice and nothing in the second. Under the theory, a shared outcome should not affect which gamble is better. If that 89% is ignored, both choices become the same: an 11% chance of $1 million against a 10% chance of $5 million and a 1% chance of nothing. Wikipedia says there is no way of assigning values to money that makes the usual pair of choices fit the theory."
resolution:
  - "Wikipedia says that when Allais presented the paradox at a 1952 conference in Paris, the audience of economists gave answers inconsistent with the theory but dismissed the result as a simple irregularity. He published it in Econometrica in 1953. It gained wide attention after Kahneman and Tversky cited it in 1979 as the best known counterexample to expected utility theory, and Allais received a Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences in 1988."
positions:
  - title: The independence axiom is not a requirement of rationality (Allais)
    body: "Parts of a gamble cannot be judged independently of one another. The 1% chance of nothing in 1B carries a sense of disappointment, knowing a sure $1 million was on offer, which depends on the rest of the gamble. Choosing 1A and 2B is not irrational, according to Allais. Cost: expected utility theory loses a principle it relies on, and Wikipedia says that this means the theory is not robust enough to capture such “bounded rationality” choices."
  - title: Replace expected utility theory with a richer theory
    body: "Wikipedia lists prospect theory, weighted utility, rank-dependent expected utility and regret theory as responses, built to allow a wider range of behaviour. Cost: Wikipedia reports that Michael Birnbaum’s experiments showed the results violated the theories of Quiggin, Kahneman, Tversky and others, but could be explained by his configural weight theory, which gives up a property called coalescing."
finePrint: "The most common explanation is the certainty effect, a preference for a sure thing. Wikipedia reports a more recent study that reached a different result, called the zero effect, an aversion to any lottery that could pay nothing. In that study the zero effect was statistically significant and the certainty effect was not. The amounts and probabilities above are those given by Wikipedia, and the paradox has been tested with small and hypothetical payoffs as well as health outcomes."
