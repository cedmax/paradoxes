problem: "Most people take a sure million over a gamble with a bigger prize, yet prefer the bigger prize when both options are long shots. Is that consistent?"
claimA: "A rational person’s choices obey the independence rule: a chance of an outcome that two options share should not affect which one is chosen."
claimB: "A rational person can prefer a sure $1 million to a gamble with a 10 percent chance of $5 million, an 89 percent chance of $1 million and a 1 percent chance of nothing, and yet prefer a 10 percent chance of $5 million to an 11 percent chance of $1 million."
verdict: "The usual pair of choices breaks a basic rule of expected utility theory. Whether that shows the choices are mistaken or the rule is too strict is still argued."
whatBreaks: "Either the independence rule of expected utility theory, or the view that the usual pair of choices can be rational, has to go. Experts split on which."
statusNote: "how to read the usual choices is contested. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes Savage and Raiffa defending expected utility theory by calling such preferences irrational, Buchak arguing they are rationally permissible, and others redescribing the outcomes so the choices fit the theory, and it does not call the question settled."
setup: "There are two choices. In the first, Gamble 1A pays $1 million for certain. Gamble 1B pays $5 million with a 10 percent chance, $1 million with an 89 percent chance, and nothing with a 1 percent chance. In the second, Gamble 2A pays $1 million with an 11 percent chance and nothing otherwise. Gamble 2B pays $5 million with a 10 percent chance and nothing otherwise. Most people choose 1A in the first choice and 2B in the second. The economist Maurice Allais presented the problem to a conference in Paris in 1952 and published it in the journal Econometrica in 1953."
whyWrong: "Each choice looks sensible on its own. In the first, a sure million is hard to give up for a tiny risk of ending with nothing. In the second, both options are long shots, so the larger prize seems worth one extra percent of risk. The trouble shows when the two choices are compared. Gamble 1B is 1A with one change: an 11 percent slice of “$1 million” is swapped for a 10 percent chance of $5 million and a 1 percent chance of nothing. Gamble 2B is 2A with exactly the same swap. The only difference between the two choices is an 89 percent chance that is shared within each pair: $1 million in the first choice, nothing in the second. Expected utility theory, the standard theory of rational choice, includes a rule called independence, which says a shared chance of the same outcome should not change which option you prefer. By that rule, choosing 1A commits you to 2A, and choosing 2B commits you to 1B."
resolution:
  - "This is not about expected money. Gamble 1B has a higher average payout than 1A, $1.39 million against $1 million, so preferring 1A can be ordinary caution. Gamble 2B averages $0.5 million against $0.11 million for 2A. Expected utility theory allows caution, because it lets each prize be worth whatever it is worth to you. What it does not allow is this pair of choices together: whatever values you give to $5 million, $1 million and nothing, preferring 1A over 1B and preferring 2A over 2B come down to the same comparison, so one cannot be right and the other wrong."
  - "The most common description of what people are doing is the certainty effect: a sure outcome gets extra weight compared with one that is merely very likely. Wikipedia notes that a newer study found a different pattern, called the zero effect, to be significant and the certainty effect not. These describe what people do. Whether they are right to do it is a separate question."
  - "The puzzle is related to the [Ellsberg paradox](ellsberg-paradox). Both show common choices that break the same kind of rule. In Ellsberg’s case the odds are unknown. Here every probability is stated, so the trouble cannot come from ambiguity, only from how people weigh risk."
positions:
  - title: "It is a mistake"
    body: "Independence is a requirement of rational choice, so the usual pair of choices is an error, however natural it feels. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy cites Leonard Savage and Howard Raiffa as defending expected utility theory in this way. Cost: it calls irrational a pair of choices that most people make and that the Stanford Encyclopedia describes as seeming reasonable."
  - title: "Attitudes to risk are allowed"
    body: "A rational person may care about the spread of outcomes, not just their average value, and independence is too strict. Allais himself argued that choosing 1A and 2B is not irrational. Lara Buchak argues that such preferences are rationally permissible, and offers a theory with an extra setting for a person’s attitude to risk. Cost: it gives up expected utility theory as the standard of rational choice and needs a rival theory, and several rival theories are on offer."
  - title: "The outcomes are not what they seem"
    body: "Describe the outcomes more fully and the choices fit the standard theory. Loomes and Sugden add the disappointment of ending with nothing after passing up a sure million. Weirich holds that a sure $1 million is worth more than $1 million won in a gamble. Cost: John Broome objects that any preferences at all could be justified by redescribing outcomes this way, unless there is a principled limit on what counts as a real difference."
finePrint: "The numbers above are the standard presentation in Wikipedia. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy gives the same structure with 100 lottery tickets and prizes of $100 million and $500 million. The averages given here are recomputed: 10 percent of $5 million plus 89 percent of $1 million is $1.39 million, 11 percent of $1 million is $0.11 million, and 10 percent of $5 million is $0.5 million. The independence rule is stated loosely here. The paradox has motivated many alternative theories of choice, including prospect theory and rank-dependent expected utility."
