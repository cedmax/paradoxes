problem: "Most people take a sure million over a gamble with a bigger prize, yet prefer the bigger prize when both options are long shots. Is that consistent?"
claimA: "If two gambles share a chance of the same outcome, changing that shared outcome should not reverse a rational preference."
claimB: "Changing an outcome shared by both gambles can rationally reverse which one you prefer."
verdict: "The usual pair of choices breaks a basic rule of expected utility theory. Whether that shows the choices are mistaken or the rule is too strict is still argued."
whatBreaks: "Whether to keep the rule about shared outcomes or accept the usual switch in preference as rational."
statusNote: "the switch violates a rule of expected utility theory. Experts disagree about whether that makes the choices wrong or the rule too strict."
setup: "Choose between a sure $1 million and a gamble that might pay $5 million but has a small chance of paying nothing. Many people take the sure million. Now change an 89 percent chance shared by both options: instead of paying $1 million, it pays nothing. Both options become long shots, and many people switch to the bigger prize. The table gives the exact chances."
whyWrong: "The part of the gamble that differs between the options is unchanged. Only an outcome shared by both has changed. A standard rule of rational choice says that shared part should not determine which option is preferred. Yet losing certainty changes many people’s minds."
resolution:
  - "The rule belongs to expected utility theory, which allows people to be cautious and to value money differently. It still says this pair of preferences cannot both hold. The issue is not simply whether people should chase the highest average payout."
  - "Unlike the [Ellsberg paradox](ellsberg-paradox), all the odds are known. The disagreement is over what a rational attitude to risk may include."
positions:
  - title: "The choices are mistaken"
    body: "Keep the rule: the common switch in preference is inconsistent. Cost: it calls a pair of reasonable-seeming choices irrational."
  - title: "The rule is too strict"
    body: "Allow attitudes to risk that give certainty special importance. Cost: a different theory of rational choice is needed, and rival theories make different demands."
  - title: "The outcomes mean more"
    body: "Include regret or the special value of a sure prize when describing what the person receives. Cost: without a principled limit, redescribing outcomes could excuse any preference."
finePrint: "The standard options are: 1A, a sure $1 million; 1B, a 10 percent chance of $5 million, an 89 percent chance of $1 million and a 1 percent chance of nothing; 2A, an 11 percent chance of $1 million; 2B, a 10 percent chance of $5 million. Both second-pair options otherwise pay nothing. The usual choices are 1A and 2B. Expected utility theory’s independence rule makes preferring 1A to 1B equivalent to preferring 2A to 2B, whatever values the person gives the prizes. This entry omits the algebra and summarises the rival theories. Allais published the paradox in 1953."
