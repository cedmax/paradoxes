problem: "Every emerald seen so far is green. Why does that support “all emeralds are green” and not “all emeralds are grue”?"
claimA: "The emeralds seen so far are good evidence that emeralds examined later will be green, and not that they will be blue."
claimB: "The emeralds seen so far fit “all emeralds are grue” exactly as well, and nothing in the evidence or in logic favours green over grue."
verdict: "The puzzle asks what justifies treating some patterns as fit for prediction and not others. Each main answer, from settled habits of language to natural kinds, faces serious objections, and none is agreed."
whatBreaks: "The idea that a general claim is backed by its observed examples, whatever words it uses. Each main attempt to say which words are fit for prediction faces serious objections."
statusNote: "each main answer faces a serious objection. Goodman’s own answer describes which words people habitually use rather than justifying the choice, the test of time-free words fails because green and grue are defined in terms of each other just as easily, and natural kinds have proved hard to define. The sources present the riddle as open."
setup: "Pick a future moment, call it t. Now invent a new colour word. Something is “grue” if it was examined before t and is green, or if it was not examined before t and is blue. Every emerald anyone has looked at so far was examined before t and was green, so every one of them was also grue. The same evidence therefore backs two general claims: “all emeralds are green” and “all emeralds are grue”. For any emerald first examined after t, these two claims disagree. One says it will be green, the other that it will be blue."
whyWrong: "Ordinary reasoning from examples, called induction, seems to work like this: if every A seen so far has been B, that supports “all As are B”. The rule makes no mention of which words are used for A and B. Grue shows that the rule, applied evenly, backs incompatible predictions from the same evidence. Everyone expects green emeralds, not blue ones. The puzzle is to say what makes “green” fit for predicting and “grue” unfit, without simply assuming the answer."
resolution:
  - "The philosopher Nelson Goodman raised the problem in a 1946 paper, “A Query on Confirmation”, and gave it its best-known form in his book Fact, Fiction, and Forecast, first published in 1954 in London and 1955 in the United States. He called it the new riddle of induction. It is different from Hume’s older [problem of induction](problem-of-induction). Hume asked whether reasoning from the seen to the unseen can be justified at all. Goodman asked which of the many patterns in the same evidence should be carried forward."
  - "The obvious reply is that “grue” is a strange, artificial word, because its definition mentions a time and joins two colours with “or”. Goodman answered that this depends on the starting point. Define “bleen” as blue if examined before t and green otherwise. Then, for someone who starts with grue and bleen, “green” is the artificial word: green means grue if examined before t and bleen otherwise. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that the two vocabularies are mirror images in all their formal properties, so the form of a word alone cannot pick the right one."
  - "The riddle links to the [raven paradox](raven-paradox). Both attack the simple idea that a general claim is supported by each of its examples. With ravens, the trouble is which objects count as examples. With grue, it is which words. W. V. Quine connected the two puzzles in 1970. The main answers are set out below, and none has won general acceptance."
positions:
  - title: "Words earn their place by use"
    body: "Goodman’s own answer. A word is fit for prediction if it is entrenched, meaning it has been used in many successful predictions before. “Green” has a long track record and “grue” has none, so “green” wins. Cost: this describes how people actually choose their words rather than showing that the choice is right, and it makes what counts as good evidence depend on the language people happen to have used."
  - title: "Time-free words only"
    body: "Rudolf Carnap, in 1947, tentatively proposed that purely qualitative words can be projected and words that point to a time or place cannot. Richard Swinburne argued in 1968 that when a qualitative word and a time-dependent word give conflicting predictions, the qualitative one should be followed. Cost: green can be defined from grue and bleen just as grue is defined from green and blue, so whether a word mentions a time depends on which words are taken as basic."
  - title: "Natural kinds only"
    body: "Quine proposed in 1970 that only natural kinds, groups whose members are genuinely similar, are fit for prediction, and suggested that evolution gave people a sense of which things are similar. Cost: similarity has proved hard to define without letting in gerrymandered groups, an objection Goodman had pressed against definitions of this kind, and the Stanford Encyclopedia says the natural-kinds idea “has not borne much fruit” in this debate."
  - title: "No general rule"
    body: "Some philosophers, including Sober, Norton and Okasha, draw the lesson that there is no single rule for which patterns continue. Each inference from examples rests on its own factual assumptions about the case at hand. Cost: those assumptions need support from earlier inferences, which rest on assumptions of their own, so the problem becomes an endless regress of justifications."
finePrint: "The time t can be any future moment, and the argument does not depend on emeralds or colours. Any evidence gathered so far fits endlessly many grue-like rules that agree about the past and disagree about the future. Saul Kripke later used a similar construction, a function called “quus”, in a puzzle about meaning rather than evidence. The four answers above are a plain summary of a very large literature."
