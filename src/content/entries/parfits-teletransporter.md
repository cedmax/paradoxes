title: Parfit's teletransporter
titleAccent: teletransporter
chapter: choice-identity
status: disputed
simplification: light
order: 4
written: true
sourcesVerified: false
problem: A machine destroys you and builds an exact copy on Mars. Have you travelled, or died?
claimA: The person on Mars has your body, memories and character, so it is you.
claimB: Your original body was destroyed, so the person on Mars is someone else, a copy.
verdict: Parfit said the question has no deeper answer. Many philosophers disagree.
whatBreaks: The belief that there is always a plain fact about whether a person survives.
statusNote: "philosophers disagree on whether the person on Mars is you. Views based on psychological continuity say yes, views that tie you to your living body say no, and Parfit held that there is no further fact to settle it and that identity is not what matters."
setup: "You step into a booth. It scans every atom of your body and destroys it, then sends the details to Mars, where a machine builds a body that matches yours exactly. The person who steps out remembers your life, has your character, and believes they are you. Did you survive the trip? The philosopher Derek Parfit used this case in his 1984 book Reasons and Persons, published by Oxford University Press."
whyWrong: "Both claims feel obvious. The first looks at what is carried across: memories, personality, plans. The second looks at what is lost: this particular body, which was taken apart. In ordinary life these go together, so we never had to choose. The machine pulls them apart. Parfit added a twist, which he called the Branch-Line case. The scanner leaves you alive on Earth but damages your heart, so you will die within days, while your replica lives on Mars. In this case Parfit treats the one on Earth as himself: as J. David Velleman retells it, the dying Parfit talks by videophone with his Replica on Mars. Parfit still argued that the replica’s link to your past is about as good as ordinary survival, and he used the case to support his view that identity is not what matters."
resolution:
  - "Parfit argued that there is no further fact beyond the facts about the body, the memories and how they connect. If so, asking whether the Martian is really you has no hidden answer to find. What matters, he said, is psychological continuity and connectedness, which he called Relation R. On that view the teletransporter is about as good as ordinary survival, even if it is not strictly the same person."
  - "Not everyone accepts this. Some philosophers hold that you are a living animal or body, so the machine ends you. Others think there is a further fact, such as a soul or a self, that a copy could not carry. Philosophers still disagree, and each answer costs something."
positions:
  - title: Psychological continuity is what matters
    body: "You survive as long as someone carries on your memories, character and plans, however it is built. Cost: if a person splits into two equally good successors, as in thought experiments where each half of a brain goes into a different body, the view cannot say both are you, so Parfit concluded that identity is not what matters."
  - title: You are your living body
    body: "You are a particular organism, so a copy made elsewhere is a new person and the machine kills you. Cost: it says an exact copy with all your memories is a stranger, which many find hard to accept."
  - title: There is a further fact
    body: "Something over and above body and mind, such as a soul or a self, makes you the same person. Cost: it posits something nobody can observe, and Parfit argued we have no good reason to believe in it."
finePrint: This entry covers the central case and Parfit's main conclusion. His wider argument has many steps, and the view that identity is not what matters is one of the most contested parts. Parfit did not invent the idea of a duplicate. Wikipedia notes earlier discussions of similar cases, including by Stanislaw Lem in the 1950s and 1960s.
