title: Parfit's teletransporter
titleAccent: teletransporter
chapter: choice-identity
status: disputed
simplification: light
order: 4
written: true
sourcesVerified: true
problem: "A machine scans and destroys your body, then builds a body exactly like yours on Mars from the scan. Have you travelled, or died?"
claimA: "The person on Mars has a body exactly like yours, with all your memories and character, so it is you."
claimB: Your original body was destroyed, so the person on Mars is someone else, a copy.
verdict: Parfit said the question has no deeper answer. Many philosophers disagree.
whatBreaks: "Whether psychological continuity is enough for survival. Either it is, and the trip is as good as surviving, or it is not, and the trip ends you. Experts split on which."
statusNote: "philosophers disagree on whether the person on Mars is you. Many views based on psychological continuity say yes, views that tie you to your living body say no, and Parfit held that there is no further fact to settle it and that identity is not what matters."
setup: "You step into a booth. It scans every atom of your body and destroys it, then sends the details to Mars, where a machine builds a body that matches yours exactly. The person who steps out remembers your life, has your character, and believes they are you. Did you survive the trip? The philosopher Derek Parfit used this case in his 1984 book Reasons and Persons, published by Oxford University Press."
whyWrong: "Both claims feel obvious. The first looks at what is carried across: memories, personality, plans. The second looks at what is lost: this particular body, which was taken apart. In ordinary life these go together, so nobody has had to choose. The machine pulls them apart. Parfit added a twist, which he called the Branch-Line case. The scanner leaves you alive on Earth but damages your heart, so you will die within days, while your replica lives on Mars. In this case Parfit treats the one on Earth as himself: as J. David Velleman retells it, the dying Parfit talks by videophone with his Replica on Mars. Parfit still argued that the replica’s link to your past is about as good as ordinary survival, and he used the case to support his view that identity is not what matters."
resolution:
  - "Parfit argued that there is no further fact beyond the facts about the body, the memories and how they connect. If so, asking whether the Martian is really you has no hidden answer to find. What matters, he said, is psychological continuity and connectedness, which he called Relation R. On that view the teletransporter is about as good as ordinary survival, even if it is not strictly the same person."
  - "Not everyone accepts this. Some philosophers hold that you are a living animal or body, so the machine ends you. Others think there is a further fact, such as a soul or a self, that a copy could not carry. Philosophers still disagree, and each answer costs something."
positions:
  - title: You are your psychological continuity
    body: "You are whoever carries on your memories, character and plans through the right kind of continuity, so the person on Mars can be you. Theorists of this kind disagree on whether copying a brain state into a new brain is the right kind: Shoemaker says yes and Unger says no. Cost: you could be continuous with two future people at once, for example if each half of your brain went into a different body. One proposed fix, the non-branching view, says you would then die, though you would survive if only one half were kept, which the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy calls “not just hard to believe, but also mysterious”."
  - title: Identity is not what matters
    body: "Parfit’s view. Whether the person on Mars is strictly you may have no deeper answer, and it does not matter. What matters is psychological continuity and connectedness, which the trip preserves, so it is about as good as ordinary survival. Cost: it gives up the ordinary belief that each person has a special concern for their own future that they do not have for anyone else’s, and philosophers such as Sosa and Merricks argue that identity does matter."
  - title: You are your living body
    body: "You are a particular organism, so a copy made elsewhere is a new person and the machine kills you. Cost: it says an exact copy with all your memories is a stranger, which many find hard to accept."
  - title: There is a further fact
    body: "Something over and above body and mind, such as a soul or a self, makes you the same person. Cost: it posits something nobody can observe, and Parfit argued there is no good reason to believe in it."
finePrint: This entry covers the central case and Parfit's main conclusion. His wider argument has many steps. Parfit did not invent the idea of a duplicate. Wikipedia notes earlier discussions of similar cases, including by Stanislaw Lem in the 1950s and 1960s.
