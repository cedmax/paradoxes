problem: "A machine scans and destroys your body, then builds a body exactly like yours on Mars from the scan. Have you travelled, or died?"
claimA: "The person on Mars has a body exactly like yours, with all your memories and character, so it is you."
claimB: Your original body was destroyed, so the person on Mars is someone else, a copy.
verdict: Parfit said the question has no deeper answer. Many philosophers disagree.
whatBreaks: "Whether psychological continuity is enough for survival. Either it is, and the trip is as good as surviving, or it is not, and the trip ends you. Experts split on which."
statusNote: "philosophers disagree on whether the person on Mars is you. Many views based on psychological continuity say yes, views that tie you to your living body say no, and Parfit held that there is no further fact to settle it and that identity is not what matters."
setup: "A booth scans your body, destroys it and sends the details to Mars. Another machine builds an exact copy. The person who steps out has your memories, character and plans. Did you travel to Mars, or die while a copy took your place?"
whyWrong: "In ordinary life, a continuing body and a continuing mind go together. The machine separates them. A second version leaves you alive on Earth while the copy appears on Mars. Both people cannot be numerically the same single person, even though both carry your memories."
resolution:
  - "Parfit argued that survival’s value may lie in the continuity of memories and character, rather than strict identity. On that view the copy can preserve what matters even if the identity question has no further answer."
  - "Other views make the body essential, require a particular kind of mental continuity or posit something beyond both. The scenario forces those views to give different answers."
positions:
  - title: "Mental continuity is enough"
    body: "The person carrying on your memories and character can be you, if the continuity is of the right kind. Cost: copying can produce two successors, and identity cannot simply branch into both."
  - title: "Identity is not what matters"
    body: "Parfit says the preserved mental connections can be about as good as ordinary survival. Cost: critics argue that being that future person matters in a way that similarity and continuity do not replace."
  - title: "You are your living body"
    body: "Destroying the organism ends you; the Martian is a new person. Cost: the exact copy’s memories and character do not make it you."
  - title: "There is a further fact"
    body: "A soul or self beyond the bodily and mental facts determines identity. Cost: this adds something not observable in the scenario, which Parfit saw no reason to posit."
finePrint: "Parfit used the case in Reasons and Persons (1984). Mental-continuity theories differ over whether scanning and copying count as the right kind of connection. His Branch-Line case leaves the Earth person alive but dying, while the replica lives on Mars. The argument is not merely that all exact copies are the same person. It separates numerical identity from what might matter in survival."
