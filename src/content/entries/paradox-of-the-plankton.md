problem: "Dozens of kinds of phytoplankton share the same water and compete for the same few nutrients. Why has one not driven out the rest?"
claimA: "The water stays uniform and steady until competition settles, so no more plankton species can survive than there are scarce resources they need."
claimB: "Dozens of plankton species survive together in the same water on only a few scarce resources."
verdict: "Real plankton communities do not meet the model’s assumptions. Changing conditions, uneven water and unsettled competition can let many species coexist."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that plankton live in a uniform, unchanging world where competition has settled."
statusNote: "the model’s assumptions fail in real communities. Which departures explain the diversity in a particular lake or sea remains open."
setup: "Phytoplankton are microscopic organisms that drift in water and use sunlight to grow. Many need the same few nutrients. A model of settled competition says that, in uniform and unchanging conditions, there cannot be more surviving species than scarce resources. Yet many species live together."
whyWrong: "If they all need the same scarce food, the best competitors should eventually drive the others out. The model’s conclusion follows from its assumptions. The observation therefore challenges whether those assumptions describe the water."
resolution:
  - "They do not. Weather changes, water contains different patches, and predators or viruses affect different species. Competition need not settle into the steady state the model requires."
  - "Even models with constant surroundings can produce species populations that keep rising and falling instead of settling. So there is no contradiction in the competition principle. What fails is applying its settled, uniform setting to all plankton communities."
finePrint: "Hutchinson posed the puzzle in 1961. The competitive exclusion principle is often summarised as one competitor driving another out when they occupy the same niche. The version here uses limiting resources and assumes equilibrium: a settled state. Huisman and Weissing showed in 1999 that competition for several resources can instead produce persistent cycles or chaotic changes. Later laboratory work observed long-lasting chaotic population changes in a food web. Reviews discuss many mechanisms and do not identify one explanation that covers every ecosystem. The agreed failure of the model’s assumptions does not settle which mechanism matters most in each place."
