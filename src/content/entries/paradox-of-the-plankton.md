problem: "Dozens of kinds of phytoplankton share the same water and compete for the same few nutrients. Why has one not driven out the rest?"
claimA: "Phytoplankton live in the conditions the competitive exclusion principle assumes: uniform, steady water, competition only for the same few resources, and time to settle at equilibrium, so no more species survive than there are limiting resources."
claimB: "Dozens of phytoplankton species live side by side in the same water, on only a few limiting resources."
verdict: "Plankton do not live in the steady, uniform, competition-only world the exclusion principle assumes. Changing weather, patchy water, predators and the species’ own rises and falls keep any one species from winning."
whatBreaks: "The first claim: that plankton live in the conditions the competitive exclusion principle assumes, with uniform and steady water, competition only for the same few resources, and time to settle at equilibrium."
statusNote: "the first claim is wrong. The main reviews agree that plankton communities are not in the settled, uniform state the principle assumes. Which departures from it matter most, and in which waters, is still open: a 2007 review by Roy and Chattopadhyay called a universally accepted theory of plankton diversity “still an unachieved goal”."
setup: "Phytoplankton are microscopic organisms, mostly single-celled, that drift in the sunlit upper layers of lakes and seas and live by photosynthesis. They all need much the same things: light and a few nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus, silicon and iron. The competitive exclusion principle, as stated by Hardin in 1960 and going back to Gause’s experiments with yeast in 1932, says that when two species compete for the same resource, only one will persist in the end. Competition theory extends this: the number of species that can live together cannot exceed the number of resources that limit them. Yet lakes and seas hold dozens of phytoplankton species living side by side. In 1961 the ecologist G. E. Hutchinson asked, in the American Naturalist, “how it is possible for a number of species to coexist in a relatively isotropic or unstructured environment”, that is, one that is much the same throughout."
whyWrong: "The principle is not a mistake. It follows from a model of competition, and Gause’s laboratory experiments supported it. Hutchinson noted that “the deduction of the principle implies an equilibrium system”: it says what happens once competitors have settled into a steady state, in surroundings that stay the same. If phytoplankton lived in such conditions, a few species would win and the rest would die out. So either the principle is wrong, or the water does not match its assumptions."
resolution:
  - "The reasoning from the principle is sound, so the observation knocks out its premise: plankton do not live in the conditions it assumes. Hutchinson himself suggested several ways out, among them differences in light and turbulence at different depths, species living off or helping one another, predators that eat some species more than others, and constantly changing conditions. In the reading of Scheffer and colleagues in 2003, his main idea was that weather keeps plankton communities from ever reaching equilibrium."
  - "Later work found several ways the assumptions fail. The environment changes: Scheffer and colleagues say later studies showed that such outside variation can let many species live together. The water is not uniform: even the open ocean has eddies and fronts that divide it into different patches. Other organisms join in: grazing by tiny animals, viruses that kill off whichever species is booming, and toxins made by some phytoplankton have all been proposed."
  - "The most surprising answer is that equilibrium may never come even in steady, uniform water. In 1999 Jef Huisman and Franz Weissing showed that in models of competition for three or more resources, species numbers can rise and fall in endless cycles or chaos, unpredictable swings that never settle. In one of their runs, 12 species coexisted on five resources. In 2008 Elisa Benincà and colleagues kept a food web taken from the Baltic Sea in the laboratory under constant conditions for more than 2,300 days. Species numbers rose and fell enormously, and the swings were chaotic."
finePrint: "This is a puzzle from biology, not a logical contradiction. Scheffer and colleagues list three main kinds of answer: variation from outside, uneven water, and swings the species create themselves. Roy and Chattopadhyay, reviewing more mechanisms in 2007, say each may apply in particular ecosystems but none explains plankton diversity everywhere. So what is settled is that the steady, uniform world of the principle is not the world of plankton. How much each departure contributes in a given lake or sea is not settled."
