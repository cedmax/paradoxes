problem: "Can a rise in a price make people buy more of the thing that has become dearer?"
claimA: "When the price of a good goes up, people buy less of it."
claimB: "For some goods used by the very poor, a rise in price makes people buy more."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that a price rise only ever makes people want less of that good. It can also cut what people can afford, and that effect can win."
statusNote: "a price rise on a staple food can leave a person poorer, so they buy more of it and give up dearer treats. It needs an inferior good with no close substitutes, so clear real-world cases are rare."
setup: "In ordinary markets, a higher price means lower demand. A Giffen good is a rare exception. Alfred Marshall credited the idea to Robert Giffen in the 1895 edition of his Principles of Economics, based on the buying habits of the Victorian poor. George Stigler’s 1947 article corrected an error in Marshall’s attribution."
whyWrong: "A price rise has two effects. People switch to cheaper alternatives, which lowers demand. But the rise also makes them poorer in real terms, and a poorer person may buy more of the cheap staple and less of everything dearer. For most goods the first effect dominates or the two point the same way. For a Giffen good the second effect is stronger."
resolution:
  - "Take a daily food budget of six dollars for three meals, with bread at one dollar a meal and cake at four dollars a meal. The budget buys two meals of bread and one of cake. Now bread rises to two dollars. The same budget buys three meals of bread and no cake. Bread went up in price, and bread eaten rose from two meals to three."
  - "So the first claim is false in some conditions and the second is true. Three conditions are needed: the good is an inferior one, meaning demand rises as income falls; it has no close substitutes; and it takes up a large part of the buyer’s income, though not all of it. Because those conditions are narrow, Giffen goods are hard to find."
finePrint: "A 2007 field experiment by Jensen and Miller, reported in the American Economic Review in 2008, offered subsidised rice in Hunan and wheat in Gansu to randomly chosen very poor households. In Hunan, demand for rice fell sharply among households offered the subsidy, which is Giffen behaviour. The Gansu results were weak evidence. The Irish potato famine is a common textbook example but is contested. Veblen goods, whose demand rises because a high price signals status, are a different thing. Masuda and Newman suggest that Simon Gray described similar behaviour in 1815, and propose the name Gray goods."
