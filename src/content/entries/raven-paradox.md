title: "Raven paradox"
titleAccent: "paradox"
chapter: belief
status: disputed
simplification: light
order: 1
written: true
problem: "Can a green apple be evidence that all ravens are black?"
claimA: "Seeing a thing that is not a raven tells you nothing about the colour of ravens."
claimB: "Evidence for a claim is also evidence for any claim that says exactly the same thing in different words."
verdict: "Philosophers disagree on which rule to loosen, and no answer has won."
whatBreaks: "The idea that a simple, sensible rule can tell us what counts as evidence for a general claim."
statusNote: "experts agree it is a real puzzle but disagree on which claim to give up."
setup: "Take the claim “All ravens are black.” Each black raven you see seems to back it up. A white raven would knock it down. Now notice that “All ravens are black” says exactly the same thing as “Everything that is not black is not a raven.” Anything that is not black and not a raven should therefore back up that second claim. A green apple is not black and not a raven. So a green apple seems to be evidence that all ravens are black. You could do bird research from an armchair, by looking at your fruit bowl."
whyWrong: "Two rules each look reasonable. The first says that only ravens matter: a black raven supports the claim, a non-black raven counts against it, and everything else is irrelevant. The second says that if something is evidence for a claim, it is evidence for any claim that means the same. Put the two together and the apple counts. The paradox is that we accept both rules and reject the result."
resolution:
  - "The puzzle comes from the philosopher Carl Hempel, who worked on it in the 1940s. The first rule goes back to the French philosopher Jean Nicod. Hempel's own answer was to accept the result. He argued that it only feels wrong because we quietly bring in other knowledge, such as how few ravens there are compared with all the non-black things in the world."
  - "Most later responses accept some version of that last point. The statistician I. J. Good argued in 1960 that the apple does count, but by an extremely small amount. Others drop one of the two rules instead. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says no consensus exists."
positions:
  - title: Accept it, the apple counts
    body: "The apple is real evidence, just very weak, because there are vastly more non-black things than ravens. Cost: it needs a careful theory of how much each observation counts, and the result still feels strange."
  - title: Drop the rule about meaning the same
    body: "A black raven backs up “All ravens are black” but not its twin about non-black things. Cost: it gives up a rule that looks obviously fair, and some argue the problem returns in another form."
  - title: Only natural kinds count
    body: "Ideas like “raven” and “black” are fit for drawing general conclusions, while “not a raven” is not. Cost: it is hard to say exactly which ideas are allowed, and the approach has not borne much fruit."
finePrint: "Good also gave a counterexample showing that a black raven does not always support “All ravens are black”, which means even the first rule is not safe in every case. This entry gives three of several proposed answers in plain terms. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy also describes a hypothetico-deductive approach, which this entry leaves out."
