title: "Raven paradox"
titleAccent: "paradox"
chapter: belief
status: disputed
simplification: light
order: 1
written: true
problem: "Can a green apple be evidence that all ravens are black?"
claimA: "Seeing a thing that is not a raven tells you nothing about the colour of ravens."
claimB: "Evidence for a claim is also evidence for any claim that says exactly the same thing in different words."
verdict: "Philosophers still disagree on which rule to give up, though the Bayesian answer is widely favoured."
whatBreaks: "The idea that a simple, sensible rule can tell us what counts as evidence for a general claim."
statusNote: "the popular Bayesian answer gives up the first claim, but Bayesians offer many rival versions of it, and other approaches give up the second claim instead."
setup: "Take the claim “All ravens are black.” Each black raven you see seems to back it up. A white raven would knock it down. Now notice that “All ravens are black” says exactly the same thing as “Everything that is not black is not a raven.” Anything that is not black and not a raven should therefore back up that second claim. A green apple is not black and not a raven. So a green apple seems to be evidence that all ravens are black. You could do bird research from an armchair, by looking at your fruit bowl."
whyWrong: "Two rules each look reasonable. The first says that only ravens matter: a black raven supports the claim, a non-black raven counts against it, and everything else is irrelevant. The second says that if something is evidence for a claim, it is evidence for any claim that means the same. Put the two together and the apple counts. The paradox is that we accept both rules and reject the result."
resolution:
  - "The puzzle comes from the philosopher Carl Hempel, who wrote about it in 1937 and 1945. The first rule is based on an idea of the French philosopher Jean Nicod. Hempel's own answer was to accept the result. He argued that it only feels wrong because we quietly bring in something we already know: that the object is not a raven. His example was a piece of ice held in a flame that does not turn yellow. That seems useless as a test of “All sodium salts burn yellow”, but only because we already know ice contains no sodium salt."
  - "The main later answer is the Bayesian one, perhaps best known in the version the statistician I. J. Good gave in 1960. It accepts that the apple counts, but by an extremely small amount, because there are vastly more non-black things than ravens. Wikipedia calls it one of the most popular answers, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy argues that a Bayesian treatment can explain why a black raven counts for more than a green apple. It is still disputed because, as Wikipedia quotes the philosopher Charles Chihara, there is no single Bayesian solution, only many rival ones, and other responses keep the apple out by dropping one of the two rules instead."
positions:
  - title: "Accept it, the apple counts"
    body: "The apple is real evidence, just very weak, because there are vastly more non-black things than ravens. This is Hempel's conclusion and the usual Bayesian answer. Cost: the answer depends on what background knowledge is assumed, and Bayesians disagree about that. Patrick Maher showed that under some assumptions an apple counts exactly as much as a black raven."
  - title: "Drop the rule about meaning the same"
    body: "A black raven backs up “All ravens are black” but not its twin about non-black things. Israel Scheffler and Nelson Goodman took a view of this kind. Cost: two claims that mean the same always have the same probability, so on this view being evidence can no longer simply mean making a claim more probable."
  - title: "Only natural kinds count"
    body: "Ideas like “raven” and “black” are fit for drawing general conclusions, while “not a raven” is not. W. V. Quine suggested this. Cost: it needs a principled way to sort ideas into those that count and those that do not."
finePrint: "Good also gave a counterexample in 1967 showing that a black raven does not always support “All ravens are black”, which means even the first rule is not safe in every case. This entry gives three of several proposed answers in plain terms. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy also describes a hypothetico-deductive approach, which this entry leaves out."
