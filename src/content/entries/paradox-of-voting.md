problem: "If one vote almost never decides an election, why do so many people vote?"
claimA: "A rational voter who cares only about their own gain votes only if the expected benefit beats the cost."
claimB: "Many people vote, even though one vote is very unlikely to decide the result."
verdict: "People may gain from voting in ways other than deciding the result."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that the only reason to vote is to decide the outcome for oneself."
statusNote: "the first claim rests on a narrow model of why people vote. Wikipedia lists several ways of widening the model, and does not say which one is right."
setup: "The paradox of voting is also called Downs’ paradox, after the economist Anthony Downs, who analysed it in 1957. For a rational voter who cares only about their own gain, the costs of voting, such as time and effort, normally exceed the expected benefits. The chance of casting the deciding vote is tiny compared with any realistic estimate of the private benefit of the result. In the simple model the expected gain from voting is the benefit of a deciding vote times the chance of casting one, minus the cost. Wikipedia reports that Condorcet noted the problem in 1793 and Hegel made a similar remark in 1821."
whyWrong: "The model assumes the only reward for voting is helping one’s favoured side win. Wikipedia says the responses to the paradox include the views that voters vote to express a preference and not to affect the outcome, that voters exercise some degree of altruism, and that the paradox ignores other benefits that come with voting."
resolution:
  - "Wikipedia lists several ways of widening the model. In the altruism theory, voters are rational but not wholly self-interested, and care about others who share their party. That benefit grows with the number of such voters, so voting can make sense even when one vote has little effect. In the civic duty account, a sense of duty adds a benefit to voting that does not depend on casting the deciding vote."
  - "Geoffrey Brennan and Loren Lomasky suggest that voters get “expressive” benefits from backing a candidate, in the way that people cheer on a sports team. Wikipedia also reports that a 2007 laboratory study found the rational voter model’s predictions about turnout were confirmed for factors such as the number of voters and the cost of voting. In that study, a model with bounded rationality fit the observations better than a standard equilibrium prediction, and predicted 17 percent turnout for large elections without added altruism or civic duty."
finePrint: "This entry covers why people vote, not who they should vote for or whether they should. Wikipedia presents the explanations side by side and does not say which is correct, so the diagnosis is partly open. It also reports a 2020 study that found expected competitiveness, based on polls, raised turnout."
