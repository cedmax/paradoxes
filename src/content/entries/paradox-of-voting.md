problem: "If a single vote almost never decides a large election, can it be rational to vote in order to change the result?"
claimA: "Voting to change the result of a large election is irrational, because one vote almost never decides it."
claimB: "Voting to change the result of a large election can be rational, even though one vote almost never decides it."
verdict: "A single vote is very rarely decisive, and whether voting for the sake of the result can still be rational is argued."
whatBreaks: "Experts split on whether a tiny chance of deciding the result can outweigh the cost of voting, for instance when the voter counts the good the result does for others or values being one of its causes, or whether people who vote must have reasons other than changing the result."
statusNote: "experts accept that one vote is very rarely decisive, but disagree on whether voting in order to affect the result can still be rational. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy concludes that whether voting is rational depends on just what voters are trying to do."
setup: "Voting costs something: time, travel, and the effort of learning about the choices. Judged by the result alone, its benefit is how much better the voter thinks one outcome is than the other, multiplied by the chance that this one vote decides the election. Rational choice theory, a common model in economics, says a rational person does something only if its expected value is highest, where expected value weights each possible outcome by its chance. In a large election the chance of deciding the result is tiny. G. Brennan and L. Lomasky (1993) estimated, with a simple model, that the expected benefit of a vote for a good candidate is worth far less than a millionth of a penny. A statistical model by Edlin, Gelman and Kaplan (2007), based on past United States presidential elections, put the chance in a safe state such as California as vanishingly small, and in very close states on the order of 1 in 10 million. So unless the chance times the difference outweighs the cost, voting to change the result looks irrational. Yet very many people vote."
whyWrong: "Each step seems sound. The cost of voting is real, the chance of being decisive really is tiny, and the rule of comparing expected benefit with cost is the usual model of rational choice. Put together, they say that a voter who cares only about changing the result should stay at home. Like the [prisoner's dilemma](prisoners-dilemma), the puzzle sets what seems rational for each person reasoning alone against an outcome that many people's choices produce together. The question is whether one of the steps hides a mistake about what a voter can rationally care about."
resolution:
  - "The worry is old. Wikipedia reports that Condorcet noted in 1793 that in large elections each voter's influence is very small, so citizens may not be interested enough to vote, and that Hegel made a similar point in 1821. In modern public choice theory the problem was analysed by Anthony Downs in his 1957 book An Economic Theory of Democracy, and it is also called Downs' paradox."
  - "Responses go two ways. Some keep the result as the goal and question what counts as its value. If voters care about other people too, the benefit of a better result is shared by very many people, which Wikipedia reports can make voting rational despite the small chance. Others say a voter can be among the votes that together cause the result, and that voting is rational for someone who cares enough about being one of those causes."
  - "Other responses accept that voting rarely pays as a way to change the result and look for other reasons. Brennan and Lomasky argue that voting is expressive, like cheering for a sports team. Some people enjoy taking part for its own sake, and Gerry Mackie (2010) points out that most citizens believe there is a duty to vote. No single answer is agreed."
positions:
  - title: "People vote for other reasons"
    body: "Keep the first claim. Judged by the result alone, a vote is almost never worth its cost, so voting is better explained by expression, enjoyment or a sense of duty. Brennan and Lomasky's expressive account is one version, and Jason Brennan, author of the Stanford Encyclopedia entry, writes that for goals based on the result, voting is irrational, or rational only in unusual circumstances or for a small subset of voters. Cost: it treats voting in order to change the result as a mistake, and the duty version faces what critics call the particularity problem: it must show why a general duty requires voting in particular, rather than some other way of serving the public."
  - title: "Count the good done for others"
    body: "Keep the second claim. Wikipedia reports work by Edlin, Gelman and Kaplan and by Chiang (2012) treating voters as rational but not purely selfish. A better result benefits very many people, so a voter who cares about others can find voting worthwhile despite the small chance. Cost: C. Freiman (2020) argues that votes are highly ineffective forms of altruism, and the encyclopedia notes that, for any vote aimed at the result, voters may not know how much better one result really is."
  - title: "Value being one of the causes"
    body: "Keep the second claim. R. Tuck (2008) and A. Goldman (1999) argue that a voter has a real chance of being among the set of votes that together bring about the result, and so is in part causally responsible for it. Cost: as the encyclopedia puts it, this makes voting rational only provided the voter sufficiently cares about being a cause, so it depends on what each voter values."
finePrint: "The name “paradox of voting” is also used for a different puzzle: majority voting going round in a circle, which is the [Condorcet paradox](condorcet-paradox). The figures for the chance of a decisive vote come from particular models and particular elections, and Wikipedia also reports a study finding that polls predicting a close election raised turnout. Downs himself suggested that voting might be a form of insurance against the collapse of democracy, and the encyclopedia argues that this fails for the same reason, since one vote is unlikely to cross any threshold. The encyclopedia dates the Edlin, Gelman and Kaplan paper to 2007, while Wikipedia gives 2008."
