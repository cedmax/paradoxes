problem: "If richer people are happier, why have countries that grew richer over time not become happier?"
claimA: "If richer people are happier, a country whose people all grow richer over time must become happier."
claimB: "In the data Easterlin reported, a country’s happiness stayed flat while its incomes rose."
verdict: "The facts are disputed. Easterlin says happiness stays flat as incomes rise, and others find that rising income does raise happiness."
whatBreaks: "The contested choice is between accepting that rising income lifts happiness over time, which others say the data show, and accepting that it does not, which needs an explanation."
statusNote: "experts agree the facts about happiness data are puzzling and disagree on what they show. Easterlin has defended the paradox since 1974 and updated the evidence in 2022 with O’Connor, while Stevenson and Wolfers (2008) and others argue the time-trend claim fails."
setup: "In 1974 the economist Richard Easterlin, who was among the first economists to study happiness data, reported two findings. At a given moment, happiness rises with income, both across and within nations. Over time, rising incomes do not produce a rising trend in happiness. Later evidence through 2014 reportedly showed US happiness flat or slightly down while real incomes more than tripled over about seven decades. The page describes the paradox as an empirical generalisation, not a theorem."
whyWrong: "Both findings are about the same people and the same money, so it seems they should agree. Two explanations are offered. One is relative income: happiness depends on how your income compares with what is thought normal, so when everyone gets richer the gain cancels out. The other is adaptation: people get used to higher incomes, much as a new phone stops feeling special."
resolution:
  - "The facts themselves are disputed, and economists have taken three main positions."
positions:
  - title: "The paradox holds"
    body: "Easterlin argues that happiness stays flat as incomes rise, because of relative income and adaptation, and he has repeated the evidence over decades. Cost: critics say his results rest on faulty data, such as changes in survey questions in Japan, and on rising inequality in the United States, where the typical citizen gained little."
  - title: "Income does raise happiness"
    body: "Stevenson and Wolfers (2008) found a significant positive link between average happiness and income over time, once surveys are made consistent. Diener and Veenhoven with collaborators also reported positive time-series links. Cost: Easterlin replied in 2017 that these studies use short series, or only two points, and that the positive link is between fluctuations, not long-run trends."
  - title: "Happiness levels off"
    body: "Happiness rises with income up to a point and then flattens, which would explain flat trends in rich countries. Some cross-sectional data from China and other Asian samples show a curved link. Cost: time series for China and Japan, which started from low incomes, show no such threshold."
finePrint: "The paradox is an empirical generalisation, not a theorem, so it stands or falls on the data. The dispute is about what the long-run data show, not about whether richer people in a given year report being happier."
