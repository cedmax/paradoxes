simplification: heavy

written: true
sourcesVerified: false
problem: "If every truth could in principle be known, does it follow that every truth is already known?"
claimA: "Every truth is knowable, at least in principle."
claimB: "Some truths are not known by anyone."
verdict: "Experts disagree on whether the proof fails, the principle fails or the logic needs changing."
whatBreaks: "Which to give up: the claim that every truth is knowable, the logic used in the proof, or the reach of the claim."
statusNote: "the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says there is no consensus about whether and where the proof goes wrong."
setup: "Suppose some sentence p is true but nobody knows it. Then the sentence “p is true and unknown” is itself true. If every truth can be known, it should be possible to know that “p is true and unknown”. But that cannot be known. As soon as someone knew it, they would know p, and then p would no longer be unknown, so the sentence would be false. Wikipedia sums up the result: if all truths were knowable, it would follow that all truths are in fact known. The proof appeared as a minor theorem in a 1963 paper by Frederic Fitch. The Stanford Encyclopedia says an anonymous referee, later identified as Alonzo Church, gave Fitch the earliest version of the proof in 1945."
whyWrong: "The proof uses only modest assumptions: that knowing a pair of facts means knowing each, and that what is known is true. The Stanford Encyclopedia says the problem is not with those. The disagreement is over where the trouble lies, not over the steps themselves. The argument matters most for anyone who ties truth to what can be verified, since for them “every truth is knowable” is plausible and “every truth is known” is not."
resolution:
  - "The entry for this paradox in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy states there is no consensus about whether and where the proof goes wrong. It sorts the main responses into changing the logic, and restricting which truths the knowability principle covers."
positions:
  - title: Restrict which truths are knowable
    body: "The knowability principle should not apply to every truth. Neil Tennant limits it to “Cartesian” statements, those that cannot be proved to be impossible to know. Michael Dummett limits it to basic statements, with more complex ones built up from them. Either way, the troublesome sentence “p is true and unknown” is left out. Cost: Hand and Kvanvig object that the restriction is not principled, since the only reason given for it is the threat of the paradox. Tennant points out that Dummett’s version also bars complex statements that are clearly knowable, and that it can undermine arguments against using classical logic for complex statements."
  - title: Change the logic of the proof
    body: "Timothy Williamson argues that the result is not a refutation of the view that truth depends on what can be verified, but a reason for its supporters to adopt intuitionistic logic, which does not allow the last step from “no truth is unknown” to “every truth is known”. Cost: Fitch’s reasoning is still valid in that logic up to that point, so the supporter must accept that no truths are unknown. Williamson replies that non-omniscience can still be stated as “not all truths are known”. The Stanford Encyclopedia says a deeper problem is said to remain, from a related argument about statements that are undecided."
  - title: Accept that not every truth is knowable
    body: "Hart and McGinn took the result in the 1970s as a refutation of verificationism, the view that every meaningful statement, and so every truth, can be verified. Mackie and Routley, according to the Stanford Encyclopedia, agreed that the proof refutes the claim that all truths are knowable, and that forms of verificationism are imperilled for related reasons. Cost: it gives up the plausible idea that truth is tied to what non-omniscient agents could come to know, which the Stanford Encyclopedia says is a coherent position and more plausible than the claim that all truths are known."
finePrint: "The three positions are examples from a larger literature that the Stanford Encyclopedia covers in detail. Wikipedia notes that Gödel’s incompleteness theorem makes it hard to say that all truths are knowable, since some statements in rich mathematical systems cannot be settled. It also shows the same proof works if “known” is replaced by other ideas, such as “believed by a rational person”. This entry follows the encyclopedia’s account and does not weigh the positions against each other."
