title: Ellsberg paradox
titleAccent: paradox
chapter: choice-identity
status: disputed
simplification: light
order: 7
written: true
sourcesVerified: true
problem: Why do people prefer a bet with known odds over one with unknown odds, when no single set of odds fits all their choices?
claimA: A rational person acts as though each outcome has some definite probability.
claimB: "A rational person can prefer bets whose odds are known, even when no single set of probabilities fits those choices."
verdict: Whether that preference is a mistake or a reasonable caution is still argued.
whatBreaks: "Either the demand that a rational person’s choices fit a single set of probabilities, or the view that preferring known odds in this way can be rational, has to go. Experts split on which."
statusNote: "how to read the usual choices is contested. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy shows they can be treated as rational by letting a probability be a range rather than a single number, notes this works only if the beliefs really are imprecise, and says this view “is not uncontroversial”, citing Al-Najjar and Weinstein's rival reading."
setup: "An urn holds 90 balls. Exactly 30 are red. The other 60 are black or yellow, in a proportion nobody knows. One ball will be drawn, and each bet pays 100 pounds if it wins. First choice. Bet A wins if the ball is red. Bet B wins if it is black. Second choice. Bet C wins if the ball is red or yellow. Bet D wins if it is black or yellow. Most people choose A over B, and D over C. Daniel Ellsberg set out this kind of case in a 1961 paper in the Quarterly Journal of Economics."
whyWrong: "Look at what each choice reveals. Preferring A to B suggests you think red is more likely than black. Preferring D to C suggests you think black plus yellow is more likely than red plus yellow, which means black is more likely than red. You cannot hold both. No single set of probabilities fits both choices, so the standard theory of rational choice, which says to act on one probability for each outcome, cannot explain them. The choices also break the sure-thing principle. That principle says that if two bets pay the same in some case, that case should not affect which one you choose. Yellow pays the same within each pair, yet the second pair is just the first with yellow added to both bets, and the choice flips."
resolution:
  - "Wikipedia says the result is generally taken to be evidence of ambiguity aversion. A and D have known odds, one third for red and two thirds for black or yellow. This is risk. B and C have odds that depend on the unknown mix of black and yellow. This is ambiguity. People tend to avoid the unknown."
  - "Whether that is a mistake is the dispute. Some say the choices break the rules of rational decision, so people are being inconsistent. Others say it is a reasonable response to not knowing the odds, and that a better theory should allow a range of possible probabilities instead of a single number. The Stanford Encyclopedia describes this second reading as a way to treat the pattern as rational."
positions:
  - title: It is a mistake
    body: "A rational person should assign some probability to each colour and stick to it, so the common pattern is an error. Cost: it calls a very common, consistent pattern irrational, and many careful people show it."
  - title: Probabilities can be a range
    body: "When you know so little, your belief about black may be a range, such as anywhere from nothing to two thirds, and cautious choices follow. Cost: it needs a new theory of choice, with rules for acting on a range that experts do not agree on."
  - title: It is about caution, not belief
    body: "People may simply dislike bets whose odds they cannot check, which is a taste and not a belief. Cost: it can look like an excuse for the same pattern the puzzle was meant to explain."
finePrint: "Wikipedia notes that John Maynard Keynes published a version of the paradox in 1921, before Ellsberg popularised it. The numbers above are the standard 90-ball version, and the same logic holds with an urn of unknown mix compared with an urn known to be half red and half black. This entry simplifies the formal statement of the sure-thing principle. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy discusses the case in its entry on imprecise probabilities."
