problem: "Three judges each reason consistently, yet the majority on the reasons and the majority on the verdict point opposite ways. Which should the court follow?"
claimA: "The court should decide each reason by majority and let the verdict follow from those decisions."
claimB: "The court should decide the verdict itself by majority, whatever the majorities on the reasons."
whatBreaks: "The contested choice: follow the majority on each reason and give up the majority on the verdict, or follow the majority on the verdict and give up the majorities on the reasons."
statusNote: "no rule meets every demand, and each gives something up: voting on the reasons overrides the majority that rejects the verdict, while voting on the verdict leaves the group without agreed reasons for it."
setup: "The court’s rule is simple: a defendant is liable if the contract was both valid and broken, and not liable otherwise. Three judges agree on the rule, but disagree on the facts."
whyWrong: "Two judges say the contract was valid. Two say it was broken. Voting on each reason therefore makes the defendant liable. But two judges say the defendant is not liable. Voting on the final verdict gives the opposite answer."
resolution:
  - "The group cannot follow all these majorities at once. It must choose whether to vote on the reasons or on the conclusion, or change another part of its decision rule."
  - "Theorems show that this is more than an unfortunate example: several attractive demands on combining judgements cannot all be met. The trade-off is proved; the best choice for a court or committee is not settled."
positions:
  - title: "Vote on the reasons"
    body: "Count each reason separately and derive the verdict. Here the defendant is liable. Cost: this overrides the majority of judges who reject that verdict."
  - title: "Vote on the verdict"
    body: "Count the final judgements directly. Here the defendant is not liable. Cost: this leaves the group without agreed reasons matching its verdict."
finePrint: "Kornhauser and Sager described the court puzzle in 1986. Pettit later generalised it to linked questions outside law. List and Pettit’s theorem concerns combining any consistent individual views into a complete and consistent group view, while treating members alike and deciding each question by the same rule from only its own votes. Other responses restrict the permitted views or let the group leave questions unanswered. The [Condorcet paradox](condorcet-paradox) concerns cycling rankings; this puzzle concerns majorities on statements whose conclusions depend on one another."
