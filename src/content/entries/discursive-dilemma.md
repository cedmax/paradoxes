problem: "Three judges each reason consistently, yet the majority on the reasons and the majority on the verdict point opposite ways. Which should the court follow?"
claimA: "The court should decide each reason by majority and let the verdict follow from those decisions."
claimB: "The court should decide the verdict itself by majority, whatever the majorities on the reasons."
verdict: "Majorities on the reasons and on the verdict can clash, and legal theorists and philosophers disagree on which one a group should follow."
whatBreaks: "The contested choice: follow the majority on each reason and give up the majority on the verdict, or follow the majority on the verdict and give up the majorities on the reasons."
statusNote: "that the two majorities can clash is a proven fact, as in the [Condorcet paradox](condorcet-paradox). What is disputed is which majority a court or committee should follow, and a review of the field by Philippe Mongin reports that the legal literature has not reached a clear-cut answer."
setup: "A court of three judges hears a contract case. The defendant must pay damages if, and only if, two things are true: the contract was valid, and the defendant broke it. All three judges accept this rule. Judge A thinks the contract was valid but was not broken, so the defendant is not liable. Judge B thinks the contract was not valid but was broken, so again not liable. Judge C thinks it was valid and was broken, so liable. Each judge is perfectly consistent."
whyWrong: "Count the votes question by question. Was the contract valid? A and C say yes, 2 to 1. Was it broken? B and C say yes, 2 to 1. By the agreed rule, the defendant is liable. Now count the votes on the verdict itself. A and B say not liable, 2 to 1. The same three consistent judges produce a majority for each reason and a majority against the verdict those reasons imply. The court must pick one way of counting, and the two give opposite results. A larger example works the same way. In a town vote on three conditions for a road repair, if three separate groups of 20 percent each reject a different condition, each condition gets 80 percent support, yet only 40 percent accept all three."
resolution:
  - "The legal theorists Kornhauser and Sager described the court problem in 1986 and called it the doctrinal paradox. The philosopher Philip Pettit generalised it in 2001 as the discursive dilemma, a problem for any group that votes on linked questions, not only courts. It is related to the [Condorcet paradox](condorcet-paradox): there, majorities on rankings go round in a circle; here, majorities on linked yes-or-no questions contradict each other."
  - "Christian List and Philip Pettit proved in 2002 that the problem is not a quirk of majority voting. No rule for combining judgements can meet four conditions at once: it accepts any consistent set of views from the members (universal domain), it always produces a complete and consistent group view (collective rationality), it treats all members alike (anonymity), and it decides each question in the same way using only the votes on that question (systematicity). Voting question by question by majority meets every condition except consistency. Later work by Pauly and van Hees (2006) showed that the result still holds if treating all members alike is weakened to having no dictator."
  - "The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy reads the theorem as a statement of trade-offs between conditions, not as a verdict on which to give up. So the mathematics is settled, and the dispute is over which condition a court or committee should give up."
positions:
  - title: "Decide the reasons, then derive the verdict (premise-based)"
    body: "Take a majority vote on each reason and let the verdict follow by the agreed rule. In the example the court finds the defendant liable. Mongin reports that this is often proposed as the solution, by Pettit among others, because it gives the court a stated rationale for its decision. Cost: it overrides a majority of judges who think the defendant is not liable, and Mongin notes that sorting questions into reasons and conclusions can be tricky and that this method too is “fraught with difficulties”."
  - title: "Decide the verdict directly (conclusion-based)"
    body: "Take a majority vote on the verdict and leave the reasons aside. In the example the court finds the defendant not liable. Mongin notes that this method respects the judges’ final views. Cost: it overrides the majorities on both reasons, so the court gives no agreed reason for its decision, and the group takes no view on the questions that led to it."
finePrint: "The Condorcet paradox entry is explained because its question, whether majorities can cycle, has a settled answer. Here the clash is equally settled, but the problem posed is which majority to follow, and on that the sources report no agreement. Researchers have also studied other ways out, such as limiting which combinations of views are allowed, letting the group stay silent on some questions, or replacing yes-or-no judgements with probabilities. Those are not covered here. Pettit has argued that the dilemma bears on holding groups such as corporations responsible. This entry does not take a view on which rule a court or committee should use."
