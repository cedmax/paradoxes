problem: "Can a group of consistent people, voting by majority, hold a view that contradicts itself?"
claimA: "A group’s view on each question is whatever the majority of its members say about that question."
claimB: "A group’s view should be consistent, so it cannot back the reasons for a verdict and reject the verdict they imply."
verdict: "Experts disagree about which condition on group judgement to give up."
whatBreaks: "Which to give up: counting every question by simple majority, a view on every question, or the demand that the group’s view be consistent."
statusNote: "Wikipedia and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy report an impossibility result, and each way round it costs something that its supporters weigh differently."
setup: "Consider a court of three judges deciding whether a lawn caretaker is liable for breaking a contract not to mow over the owner’s roses. They vote on two questions. P: did the caretaker mow over the roses? Q: was there a contract forbidding it? All the judges agree that the caretaker is liable if and only if both P and Q are true. Wikipedia describes how each judge can be consistent and the group still contradict itself. A majority can say P is true and a majority can say Q is true, which suggests the caretaker is liable. At the same time, a majority can say that P and Q are not both true, so the caretaker is not liable."
whyWrong: "Wikipedia explains that a decision procedure can be premise-based or conclusion-based. In a premise-based procedure the judges vote on whether each condition for liability is met. In a conclusion-based one they vote directly on whether the defendant is liable. The two procedures do not always agree, and can give opposite results. Wikipedia’s example of a community voting on road repairs makes the same point: with three groups of 20 percent saying no to different questions, each question gets 80 percent yes, but only 40 percent say yes to all three conditions at once."
resolution:
  - "Wikipedia describes the dilemma as an extension of the voting paradox and Arrow’s theorem to judgements, not preferences, and as a special case of the [Condorcet paradox](condorcet-paradox). Christian List and Philip Pettit showed that the inconsistencies remain for any aggregation method that meets a few natural conditions, which Wikipedia calls the List–Pettit theorem."
  - "Pettit concluded, according to Wikipedia, that there is no simple way to combine individual opinions into a single, coherent group view, which matters for holding groups such as corporations responsible."
positions:
  - title: Limit the cases (relax universal domain)
    body: "If the members’ views are similar enough in a specific way, simple majority voting on each question gives a consistent group view. The simplest condition is unidimensional alignment, where people can be lined up from left to right so that, for each question, those who accept it are all on one side. Cost: the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says this works only for profiles of judgements that meet such a cohesion condition, so it does not cover groups that are divided in other ways."
  - title: Decide by premises, not question by question (relax independence)
    body: "Vote on a set of premises and let logic decide the rest, or decide questions in a fixed order of priority, or pick the group view closest to the members’ views. A premise-based rule gives a consistent group view that is complete if there are no ties. Cost: the Stanford Encyclopedia says the cost of relaxing independence is the loss of strategy-proofness, meaning voters can gain by misreporting their views. It adds that ordered rules depend on the order in which questions are taken, so they are open to agenda manipulation."
  - title: Allow the group to stay silent on some questions (relax completeness)
    body: "Use unanimity, a supermajority, or a rule that votes only on the conclusions. These guarantee consistent group views. Cost: the Stanford Encyclopedia says conclusion-based rules always leave the other questions undecided, and that if collective views must also follow from one another, an impossibility result returns unless a small fixed group, the oligarchs, decide. It says that oligarchic rules either lapse into dictatorship or lead to stalemate."
finePrint: "The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy also covers giving up yes-or-no judgements for probabilities, which is not covered here. This entry is a summary of sources written for specialists and does not take a view on which rule a court or committee should use."
