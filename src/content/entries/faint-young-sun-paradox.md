problem: "If the young Sun was much dimmer than today, why was early Earth not frozen?"
claimA: "Early Earth warmed itself in much the same way as it does now, with a similar atmosphere and cloud cover, so a Sun about 30 percent dimmer would have left it frozen."
claimB: "Early Earth had liquid water, even though the Sun gave off much less heat."
verdict: "Early Earth needed extra warming from somewhere, and scientists disagree about where it came from."
whatBreaks: "The contested choice is which kind of extra warming early Earth had: more of some greenhouse gas, fewer clouds, or something else. Each explanation has its own problem."
statusNote: "experts agree the puzzle is real and disagree on how it is solved. The leading explanation is that greenhouse gases, mainly carbon dioxide, warmed the planet, but how much is still debated."
setup: "Astronomers think the Sun grows brighter as it ages. Carl Sagan and George Mullen raised the puzzle in 1972. Early estimates had the young Sun giving only about 70 percent of today’s heat, and later models use one that was about 30 percent dimmer. On that basis, the early Earth should have been frozen, yet the evidence points to liquid water on the early Earth. The Sun is now about 1.4 times brighter than it was 4.6 billion years ago."
whyWrong: "The first claim hides an assumption, that the early Earth’s surface and atmosphere worked like today’s. If early Earth had much more of some greenhouse gas, or fewer reflective clouds, it could have been warm. The disagreement is over which assumption to change."
resolution:
  - "The predominant idea is that carbon dioxide supplied much of the extra warming. The other ideas below have not won out. None of them is established, and the problem is still listed as unsolved."
positions:
  - title: "Much more carbon dioxide"
    body: "About 1,000 times today’s level is consistent with models of the carbon cycle and the Sun’s evolution. One-dimensional models need at least 0.1 bar of carbon dioxide 4.5 billion years ago, with 10 bar suggested as a plausible upper limit. Methane may also have contributed. This is the predominant theory. Cost: the amount needed is still debated."
  - title: "A warming haze of ammonia"
    body: "Sagan and Mullen proposed ammonia, which warms the air well. Cost: sunlight destroys ammonia quickly, and a haze that shielded it would also cool the surface beneath. A photochemical model tested the idea and discounted it by 2001."
  - title: "Fewer clouds, darker surface"
    body: "With less exposed land there would be fewer particles for clouds to form around, so less sunlight would be reflected. Cost: Goldblatt and Zahnle (2011) found the net effect of clouds was as likely to cool as to warm, warming the surface at most to just above freezing on average."
  - title: "A heavier Sun"
    body: "If the young Sun was more massive and lost mass later, it would have been brighter than thought. Cost: the lunar and meteorite record suggests the strong solar wind needed lasted only about 100 million years, while about a billion years is needed."
finePrint: "Other suggestions include tidal heating, which Heller and colleagues (2021) estimated could raise the temperature by up to five degrees Celsius, which is not enough alone. A role for cosmic rays, proposed by Nir Shaviv, is a minority view, and the mechanism it relies on is reported not to work."
