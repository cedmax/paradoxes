problem: "If the young Sun was much dimmer than today, why was early Earth not frozen?"
claimA: "Early Earth warmed itself much as it does now, so a Sun about 30 percent dimmer would have left it frozen."
claimB: "Early Earth had liquid water, even though the Sun gave off much less heat."
verdict: "Early Earth needed extra warming from somewhere, and scientists disagree about where it came from."
whatBreaks: "The contested choice is which kind of extra warming early Earth had: more of some greenhouse gas, fewer clouds, or something else. Each explanation has its own problem."
statusNote: "experts agree the puzzle is real and disagree on how it is solved. The leading explanation is that greenhouse gases, mainly carbon dioxide, warmed the planet, but how much is still debated."
setup: "The young Sun gave off substantially less heat than today. If early Earth had warmed itself in much the same way as now, it should have been frozen. Yet geological evidence points to liquid water. What supplied the extra warmth?"
whyWrong: "The freezing prediction assumes a surface and atmosphere like today’s. Different greenhouse gases, clouds or reflection could change the result. The disagreement is over which combination fits the early Earth."
resolution:
  - "Extra greenhouse warming, especially from carbon dioxide, is the leading account. The needed amounts and the rest of the climate remain debated."
  - "The dimmer Sun and liquid-water evidence do not by themselves identify one warming mechanism. Rival explanations must also fit the available geological constraints."
positions:
  - title: "More greenhouse gases"
    body: "Extra carbon dioxide, with possible contributions from methane, could retain enough heat. Cost: the needed amounts and their fit with the early atmosphere are still debated."
  - title: "Ammonia and a protective haze"
    body: "Ammonia could warm the air if protected from sunlight. Cost: sunlight destroys it quickly, and a protective haze can also cool the surface."
  - title: "Less reflection"
    body: "A darker surface or different clouds could absorb more sunlight. Cost: cloud changes can cool as well as warm, and the proposed effect may be too small."
  - title: "A brighter young Sun"
    body: "A young Sun that began heavier and later lost mass could have supplied more heat. Cost: the required history faces constraints from lunar and meteorite evidence."
finePrint: "Other suggestions include tidal heating, which Heller and colleagues (2021) estimated could raise the temperature by up to five degrees Celsius, which is not enough alone. A role for cosmic rays, proposed by Nir Shaviv, is a minority view, and the mechanism it relies on is reported not to work."
