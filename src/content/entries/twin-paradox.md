status: resolved
simplification: light
noMaths: true

written: true
problem: "If one twin flies off at high speed and comes back, why is she younger than her twin who stayed home?"
claimA: "Motion is relative, so each twin can say the other one was moving, and each should find the other younger."
claimB: "When they meet again, only one of them can be the younger."
verdict: "The twins are not symmetric: only one of them turns round."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that the two twins' situations are mirror images."
statusNote: "one claim is wrong. The twins are not equal, and special relativity says exactly why."
setup: "Einstein's special relativity says that a moving clock ticks more slowly than a stationary one, as measured by the stationary observer. Take twins, Stella and Terence. Stella flies to a star at high speed and returns. Terence stays on Earth. From Terence's view, Stella's clock runs slow. But from Stella's view, Terence was the one moving away and back, so his clock should run slow. Each should be the younger. That cannot be true when they meet."
whyWrong: "The rule that each sees the other's clock run slow only applies to someone moving at steady speed in a straight line. Terence does that the whole time. Stella does not. To come home she has to turn round, and so she switches from one steady motion to another. She would feel that turn, in the same way you feel a car braking. Terence feels nothing. So the situations are not mirror images."
resolution:
  - "Here is an example. Stella travels at 80 percent of the speed of light to a star 4 light years away, as measured from Earth, and straight back. In Terence's frame, each leg takes 5 years, so 10 years pass on Earth. Stella's clock runs at three fifths of the Earth rate, so only 6 years pass for her. She comes home 4 years younger than her twin."
  - 'From Stella''s side, the same answer comes out. While she is travelling, she sees the distance to the star shrink to 2.4 light years, so each leg takes her 3 years. During the turn, her idea of which moments on Earth happen "at the same time" as her turn changes sharply. Her sense of what Terence''s clock reads at that moment jumps forward by a large amount. That jump is what makes the sums agree. Terence''s clock reads 10 years at the end in both accounts.'
  - "A cleaner way to say it is that clocks measure the length of their path through space and time. The two twins take different paths between the same two meetings, and a clock that goes straight takes the longest time. Experiments with fast-moving particles and clocks agree with this kind of prediction."
finePrint: "The numbers above are an example chosen to be easy, and the turn is treated as instant. The effect does not depend on the acceleration itself. If the turnaround is replaced by a second traveller who passes Stella's clock reading on the way back, with no one accelerating, the age difference is the same. What matters is that the twin who comes back has had two different motions, and the one who stayed has had only one. Langevin described the story in 1911."
