problem: "Why do some studies find that heavier patients with certain diseases die less often than patients of normal weight?"
claimA: "Among people who already have certain diseases, the extra weight itself helps them survive."
claimB: "The extra weight does not help these patients survive. Their lower death rate is produced by who ends up in the studies and how weight is measured."
verdict: "Researchers still disagree whether the survival advantage of heavier patients is real or created by the way studies select and measure people."
whatBreaks: "Experts split over whether the lower death rate of heavier patients reflects something the weight does, or biases in who gets studied and how weight is measured."
statusNote: "the lower death rate is found in some studies of people who are already ill, but researchers disagree over how much of it, if any, is caused by the weight. Proposed explanations range from extra weight being protective to flaws in the studies, and no single bias has been shown to account for all of it."
setup: "Obesity raises the risk of developing heart failure: Wikipedia reports about twice the risk compared with people of normal weight. Yet some studies of people who already have heart failure find that those with a body mass index between 30.0 and 34.9, which counts as obese, die less often than those of normal weight. Body mass index, or BMI, is weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in metres. Similar findings were first described in 1999 in people on kidney dialysis, and have since been reported in some people with other heart and lung diseases and with cancer. This is the obesity paradox."
whyWrong: "Two lines of thought clash. One takes the patient data at face value: if heavier patients survive more often, something about the extra weight may help them. The other starts from the evidence that excess weight raises the risk of disease and death in the wider population, and concludes that the patient data must be misleading. Both cannot be right about the same patients."
resolution:
  - "Several biases could make extra weight look protective. The first is reverse causation: serious illness often makes people lose weight before they die, so a low weight can be a result of approaching death rather than a cause of it. The second is smoking: smokers die sooner and tend to be leaner. In a study of 1.46 million white adults, looking only at people who had never smoked greatly reduced the death rates estimated for the underweight group and strengthened those for the overweight and obese groups."
  - "The third is collider bias, the same mechanism as [Berkson’s paradox](berksons-paradox). The studies include only people who already have the disease. A patient of normal weight who has heart disease must have got it some other way, and those other causes, such as genes or environment, may be more deadly than obesity. Wikipedia calls this one probable explanation for the heart disease version. Robinson, Furberg and Banack give the example of an unmeasured cause, such as hepatitis C, that drives disease in patients who are not obese, and they report that Banack and Kaufman, after reweighting heart failure patients to match the whole population, found obesity appeared to raise the risk of death."
  - "None of this has settled the question. On smoking, Stokes and others report strong confounding, while Flegal and colleagues argued in 2007 that smoking does not account for the pattern. Other explanations have also been proposed, such as heavier patients receiving more aggressive treatment after a heart attack, found in one study by Diercks and colleagues in 2006."
positions:
  - title: "The weight really helps"
    body: "In some patients who are already ill, extra weight may genuinely improve survival. Mechanisms have been proposed, for example by Hong and colleagues in 2011, who suggested that fat tissue may store chemicals that would otherwise harm the body. Cost: studies designed to reduce reverse causation find higher risk of death above a BMI of 25, and critics say studies that also measure waist size, not just BMI, make the paradox itself questionable."
  - title: "The studies mislead"
    body: "The advantage is produced by bias: collider bias from studying only people who are already ill, weight loss caused by illness, and smoking. Cost: no single bias has been shown to explain all of it. Flegal and colleagues argued that smoking does not account for the pattern."
finePrint: "This entry describes what studies report about groups of patients. It is not health advice. The pattern is reported mainly in people who are already ill, not in the population as a whole. Kamyar Kalantar-Zadeh proposed the related term “reverse epidemiology” in 2003. Dixon and colleagues argued in 2015 that there is no paradox to explain if ideal weight ranges differ between groups of people. Critics also note that the studies almost always measure obesity by BMI alone."
