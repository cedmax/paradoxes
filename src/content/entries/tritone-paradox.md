problem: "How can the same two tones sound as if they rise to one listener and fall to another?"
why: "A tritone is an interval of six semitones, half an octave. Each tone here is specially built from six copies of one note, an octave apart, so its note name is clear but its octave is not. Two ordinary piano notes a tritone apart do not do this. With the octave hidden, listeners still hear one tone as higher, but they do not all agree which, so the same pair can sound like a rise to one person and a fall to another. In Diana Deutsch’s studies most listeners showed the effect, whatever their musical training."
finePrint: "The tones are Shepard tones: their strengths follow a smooth hump across six octaves, so no single octave stands out. Deutsch proposed that each listener has a personal sense of which note names sound highest. She found that the direction heard was related to the pitch range of the listener’s speaking voice, and that listeners who grew up in California and in southern England tended to disagree. That speech experience shapes the judgement is a proposed explanation, not an established one. Repp (1997) found that moving the hump to higher or lower frequencies changed many listeners’ answers: some followed the hump entirely, others relied mostly on the note name, and some did a mix. Sounds heard just before a pair could also sway the answer. On that evidence, how high a tone sounds depends on several competing cues, and the note name is only one of them."
