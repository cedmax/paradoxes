problem: "If every truth could be known by someone at some time, must every truth actually be known by someone at some time?"
claimA: "Every truth could be known by someone at some time."
claimB: "Some truths are never known by anyone."
verdict: "A short proof turns “every truth could be known” into “no truth is ever left unknown”. Philosophers disagree on whether to limit the principle or change the logic."
whatBreaks: "Experts split on what to give up: the principle that every truth can be known, at least in its unrestricted form, or the classical logic that the proof uses to reach its conclusion."
statusNote: "the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says there is “no consensus on whether and where it goes wrong”. Wikipedia reports that the knowability principle is the step generally held to be at fault, but those who give it up disagree on whether a restricted version can be saved, and others keep it by revising logic."
setup: "Some philosophers hold that truth is tied to what can be known. On that view, a truth that nobody could ever know makes little sense. Their principle sounds modest: every truth is knowable, meaning that someone could know it at some time, not that anyone does. It also seems obvious that some truths are never known by anyone, such as the exact number of hairs on a long-dead stranger’s head. A short proof shows that these two modest claims cannot both be true. If every truth is knowable, then every truth is actually known by someone at some time."
whyWrong: "Suppose some truth, call it p, is never known. Then the sentence “p is true, and nobody knows p” is itself true. By the knowability principle, it could be known. But try to imagine someone knowing it. They would know p, because knowing a sentence of the form “this and that” means knowing each half. They would also know that nobody knows p, and what is known is true, so nobody knows p. Then p would be both known and not known, which is impossible. So that sentence can never be known, even though it is true. The knowability principle says it could be known. The only way to keep the principle is to deny that there was an unknown truth in the first place. Each step looks harmless, and the conclusion, that every truth is actually known by someone at some time, is absurd."
resolution:
  - "The proof appeared as a minor theorem in a 1963 paper by the logician Frederic Fitch. Its earliest version reached Fitch in 1945 through an anonymous referee report, and archival research by Joe Salerno, published in 2009, showed that the referee was Alonzo Church. The result was rediscovered in the 1970s, by Hart and McGinn in 1976 and by W. D. Hart in 1979, who called it an “unjustly neglected logical gem”."
  - "The link to [Moore's paradox](moores-paradox) is the troublesome sentence. “p, but nobody knows p” can be perfectly true, yet nobody could ever know it, just as “It is raining, but I don’t believe it” can be true yet is absurd to say. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy suggests that Church may have been inspired by Moore’s example. Moore’s puzzle is about what can sensibly be said or believed. Fitch’s is about what can be known, and it turns a curiosity into a proof against a whole theory of truth."
  - "The proof matters most to anti-realists, philosophers who hold that truth cannot outrun what could be known, for whom the knowability principle is central. Timothy Williamson called it “an embarrassment to various brands of antirealism”. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says there is still no consensus on whether and where the proof goes wrong. The main positions are below."
positions:
  - title: "Not every truth is knowable"
    body: "The proof is sound, so the knowability principle is false. Mackie (1980) and Routley (1981), after raising difficulties, both agreed that Fitch’s result refutes the claim that all truths are knowable, and Wikipedia reports that this principle is the step generally blamed. Cost: anti-realists lose a principle at the heart of their view, and Kvanvig, and Brogaard and Salerno, argue that a moderate anti-realism then seems impossible to state apart from the absurd claim that every truth is known."
  - title: "Only some truths are knowable"
    body: "Keep a narrower principle. Neil Tennant limits it to truths that could be known without contradiction, which rules out sentences like “p, but nobody knows p”. Michael Dummett limits it to basic statements. Dorothy Edgington, in 1985, proposed instead that what is actually true could be known in some other possible situation. Cost: Hand and Kvanvig object that Tennant’s limit is not drawn in a principled way, Brogaard and Salerno built a further paradox against both limits, and critics including Williamson question whether knowing in another situation what is actually the case makes sense."
  - title: "Change the logic"
    body: "Williamson argued that the proof gives anti-realists a reason to adopt intuitionistic logic, a logic that does not allow a claim to be proved just by showing that its denial is impossible. That blocks the final step, so “every truth is known” no longer follows. Cost: the anti-realist must then accept that there is no truth that is unknown, and can state that people are not all-knowing only weakly, as “not every truth is known”. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy also notes that the rule this commits them to, that whatever is never known is false, appears to be false for everyday empirical matters."
finePrint: "Fitch himself used the result in a theory of value, not to attack anyone’s view of truth. The proof uses only two facts about knowledge: knowing a conjunction means knowing each part, and only truths can be known. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy reports that dropping the first of these does not help, because variants of the proof survive. It also describes other responses, such as a paraconsistent logic defended by Beall, which this entry leaves out."
