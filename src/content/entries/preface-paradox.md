problem: "Can an author rightly believe every claim in a book and also that the book contains a mistake?"
claimA: "It is reasonable for the author to fully believe each claim in their book, and also that one of them is wrong."
claimB: "A reasonable person's beliefs must all be able to be true together."
verdict: "Philosophers disagree on whether such a set of beliefs can be reasonable."
whatBreaks: "Either their full belief in every claim and in the error, or the demand that a reasonable person's beliefs can all be true together, has to go, and experts split on which."
statusNote: "experts agree the author's beliefs cannot all be true together, but split on whether that can be reasonable, or whether their beliefs should be recast as degrees of confidence."
setup: "An author finishes a long, careful book. They have checked every claim and believe each one. In the preface they write that, in spite of their efforts, there will surely be mistakes, and they take the blame. That is also reasonable, because long books nearly always contain errors. But look at what they now believe. Claim 1 is true, claim 2 is true, and so on to the last. And one of them is false. Those beliefs cannot all be right."
whyWrong: "The [lottery paradox](lottery-paradox) rests on three rules: the high-odds rule (you may believe what is very likely), the combining rule (if you are right to believe each of several things, you are right to believe them all together) and the no-contradiction rule (never believe something you know is a contradiction). This puzzle does not need the high-odds rule. As the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy points out, their belief that the book contains an error does not come from a rule about probability. It comes from knowing that they are fallible, like other authors. But the combining rule turns their beliefs about each claim into the belief that every claim is true. Applied once more, it joins that with their belief that one claim is false into a single belief that they know is a contradiction, which the no-contradiction rule forbids. Together the two rules amount to the second claim: a reasonable person's beliefs must all be able to be true together. Yet they look like a modest, sensible person, not a muddled one."
resolution:
  - "The paradox was published by the logician David Makinson in 1965. It has the same shape as the [lottery paradox](lottery-paradox), but the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy points out that it does not rely on a rule about probability. The author's doubt comes from knowing they are fallible, like other authors."
  - "No answer is agreed. According to the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, many philosophers join the philosopher Henry Kyburg in giving up the combining rule, and conclude that it can be rational to have beliefs that cannot all be true together. Wikipedia, on the other hand, says most philosophers find the combining rule natural, though some, Kyburg among them, do not. Others restate the author's beliefs in terms of probability, or use a logic in which beliefs that cannot all be true do not let everything else follow."
positions:
  - title: "Beliefs need not combine"
    body: "Drop the combining rule. Believing each claim does not commit them to believing them all together, so they never believe the contradiction itself, even though their beliefs cannot all be true together. This is Kyburg's view. Cost: if holding beliefs that cannot all be true together is allowed, it is unclear how a paradox could ever force anyone to change their mind. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy suggests Kyburg could reply that a clash spread thinly over hundreds of claims is bearable, while one packed into a few claims, as in a paradox, is not."
  - title: "Treat it as a matter of odds"
    body: "Replace full belief with degrees of confidence. Each claim is very likely true, but with many claims the chance that at least one is wrong becomes high, and the two statements no longer contradict each other. Cost: they can no longer simply believe what they have written, only be confident in it."
  - title: "Reason with clashing beliefs"
    body: "The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy uses the preface as an example of why rules for revising beliefs may need to work on sets of beliefs that cannot all be true, and says a better account may be based on a paraconsistent logic, one in which a clash between beliefs does not let everything else follow. Like Kyburg's view, it gives up the second claim, but it focuses on how to reason and revise with such beliefs rather than on which beliefs to combine. Cost: it needs a non-standard logic."
finePrint: "The three positions and the rules they discuss are simplified. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says Makinson drew the example from a real preface, written by Raymond Wilder in 1952, and that the 1982 reprint of Wilder's book has three pages of corrections."
