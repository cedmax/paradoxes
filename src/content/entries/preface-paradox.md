problem: "Can an author rightly believe every claim in a book and also that the book contains a mistake?"
claimA: "It is reasonable for the author to fully believe each claim in their book, and also that one of them is wrong."
claimB: "A reasonable person's beliefs must all be able to be true together."
verdict: "Philosophers disagree on whether such a set of beliefs can be reasonable."
whatBreaks: "Whether reasonable beliefs must all be consistent, or a careful author may reasonably believe each claim while expecting an error somewhere."
statusNote: "experts agree the author's beliefs cannot all be true together, but split on whether that can be reasonable, or whether their beliefs should be recast as degrees of confidence."
setup: "An author checks every claim in a book and believes each one. In the preface they also say that at least one is wrong, and believe that too. Careful research and modesty both seem reasonable. Yet those beliefs cannot all be true."
whyWrong: "Combine the beliefs about individual claims and the result is “every claim is true”. Combine that with the belief in an error and a contradiction follows. The author does not know which claim to withdraw."
resolution:
  - "One answer allows beliefs that cannot all be true together, without requiring the author to combine them. Another replaces outright belief with degrees of confidence."
  - "Unlike the [lottery paradox](lottery-paradox), the author’s reasons for believing individual claims need not be just high probabilities. That makes the puzzle harder to dismiss as misuse of an odds rule."
positions:
  - title: "Beliefs need not combine"
    body: "Keep the individual beliefs without forming the combined belief that everything is true. Cost: the reasonable beliefs still cannot all be true together, making it harder to say when a clash should force revision."
  - title: "Use degrees of confidence"
    body: "Be highly confident in each claim while expecting an error among many claims. Cost: this replaces outright belief in the book’s claims with a weaker attitude."
  - title: "Reason despite inconsistency"
    body: "Use rules that let inconsistent beliefs be revised without making every conclusion follow. Cost: this needs a different logic or account of belief revision."
finePrint: "Makinson published the puzzle in 1965, drawing on a preface by Raymond Wilder. It concerns outright belief, not merely assigning a high probability to each claim. A collection of beliefs that cannot all be true is different from explicitly believing a contradiction, and some responses rely on that distinction."
