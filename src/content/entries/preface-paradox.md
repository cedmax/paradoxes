---
title: "Preface paradox"
titleAccent: "paradox"
chapter: belief
status: disputed
simplification: light
order: 3
written: true
problem: "Can an author rightly believe every claim in a book and also that the book contains a mistake?"
claimA: "The author has checked each claim and is right to believe it."
claimB: "The author knows from experience that a long book almost certainly has an error somewhere."
verdict: "Philosophers disagree on whether such a set of beliefs can be reasonable."
whatBreaks: "The idea that a reasonable person's beliefs must all be able to be true together."
statusNote: "experts agree it is a real puzzle but disagree on which claim to give up."
setup: "An author finishes a long, careful book. She has checked every claim and believes each one. In the preface she writes that, in spite of her efforts, there will surely be mistakes, and she takes the blame. That is also reasonable, because long books nearly always contain errors. But look at what she now believes. Claim 1 is true, claim 2 is true, and so on to the last. And one of them is false. Those beliefs cannot all be right."
whyWrong: "We think that someone who believes A and believes B should be willing to believe both together, and that a reasonable person does not hold beliefs that contradict each other. The author seems to break the second rule, yet she looks like a modest, sensible person, not a muddled one."
resolution:
  - "The paradox was published by the logician David Makinson in 1965. It has the same shape as the lottery paradox, but here the author has good evidence for each claim, not just good odds."
  - "No answer is agreed. Some say the author should drop the rule that beliefs combine, as Henry Kyburg did for the lottery. Others say that her belief that there is an error does not fit with believing every claim, and that she is therefore making a small, forgivable mistake. Most philosophers, according to Wikipedia, find the rule that beliefs can be combined natural, though not all."
positions:
  - title: Beliefs need not combine
    body: "Believing each claim does not commit her to believing them all together. Cost: she holds beliefs that cannot all be true, which looks like a flaw."
  - title: Allow a small inconsistency
    body: "When the clash is spread thinly across hundreds of claims, it is a sign of honesty about our limits, and being reasonable is not the same as being flawless. Cost: it lowers the bar for what counts as reasonable."
  - title: Treat it as a matter of odds
    body: "Each claim is very likely true, but with many claims the chance that at least one is wrong becomes high. Cost: it makes belief a matter of degree, and it does not tell her what to say about each single claim."
finePrint: "The three positions are simplified, and several others exist, including one that allows contradictions without letting them spread to everything. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says Makinson drew the example from a real preface, written by Raymond Wilder in 1952."
