problem: "Can a voting rule respect everyone’s private choices and always follow unanimous preferences?"
claimA: "A person’s private right should prevail even when everyone prefers a different complete outcome."
claimB: "An outcome everyone prefers should prevail even when that overrides someone’s private right."
whatBreaks: "The choice between unconditional rights over private decisions and always following unanimous rankings of complete outcomes."
statusNote: "the formal impossibility is established under Sen’s assumptions; the dispute concerns which attractive rule should be restricted."
setup: "Amartya Sen imagined two people with opposite views about reading a controversial book. Each is given a say over their own reading. But each also cares about what the other reads."
whyWrong: "The two private choices can force a social ranking that conflicts with a comparison both people agree on. The problem is not that Sen’s maths is in doubt, but that no ranking can respect every condition at once."
resolution:
  - "Sen showed that some patterns of preferences make even minimal decisiveness over personal choices incompatible with always following unanimous preferences."
  - "Some philosophers favour protecting personal decisions even when that conflicts with a group ranking. Others ask whether rights should be modelled as control over pairs of social outcomes at all. Neither response refutes the theorem."
positions:
  - title: "Protect private rights"
    body: "Sen’s minimal-liberty rule gives each person final say over some comparisons, even if this violates a unanimously preferred complete outcome. Cost: the social ranking can reject something everybody prefers."
  - title: "Preserve unanimous preference"
    body: "Give the preference shared by everyone priority over the unconditional personal comparison. Cost: what seemed to be an individual right can be overridden."
finePrint: "Sen’s 1970 theorem concerns social rankings, not a proof that liberty is impossible. Sen argued for limiting pure preference-based welfare rules; later writers challenged whether his definition captures rights. Restricting meddlesome preferences is another response."
