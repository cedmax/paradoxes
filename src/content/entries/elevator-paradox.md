problem: "Why does the first lift to arrive so often seem to be going the wrong way?"
claimA: "A lift passing a floor once going up and once going down should be equally likely to arrive next in either direction."
claimB: "At a low floor, the next lift is more likely to arrive coming down than going up, even though it passes both ways."
verdict: "Near the bottom of a building, the lift spends most of its route above the waiting floor, so its next arrival is usually downward."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that equal numbers of up and down passes mean equal waits for the next pass."
statusNote: "at a floor near the bottom, the long interval while the lift is above the floor is most often followed by a downward arrival."
setup: "A worker near the bottom of a building notices that a lift coming to their floor often arrives travelling down. Someone near the top usually sees the reverse."
whyWrong: "The lift must pass each floor upward and downward once per complete circuit. But the intervals between successive passes are unequal. A random arrival is more likely to happen during the longer interval."
resolution:
  - "At a low floor, the lift passes heading up, spends most of the trip travelling above that floor, then returns past it heading down. Because the long gap ends in a downward pass, a person arriving at a random moment is more likely to see the next lift going down."
  - "Near the top, the long interval is spent below the floor and is followed by an upward pass. This is related to the [inspection paradox](inspection-paradox): random moments favour longer intervals, but the lift-direction effect adds the geometry of the route."
finePrint: "The classic version is associated with physicists George Gamow and Marvin Stern. The pattern assumes a lift moving through a repeated route. Stops, dispatch systems and multiple lifts change the probabilities."
