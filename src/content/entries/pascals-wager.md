problem: "If reason cannot settle whether God exists, is believing in God the sensible bet?"
claimA: "An infinite reward, even if unlikely, outweighs any finite cost, so betting on God is wise."
claimB: The argument would let any promise of an infinite reward force you to act, and many rival promises exist.
verdict: "Objections about rival gods, infinite rewards and choosing belief have each drawn replies, and philosophers still disagree over whether the wager works."
whatBreaks: "Whether a chance of an infinite reward settles the decision. Either that rule or the objection from rival promises has to give way, and experts split on which."
statusNote: "philosophers still argue over whether the wager even works on its own terms. Critics such as Mackie and Hacking conceded it is valid, Duff and Hájek argue that mixed strategies make it invalid, Monton, partly with Easwaran, replies that it survives that objection, and others have defended repaired versions."
setup: "Blaise Pascal, a French mathematician and writer who lived from 1623 to 1662, left notes that were published after his death as the Pensées in 1670. One passage argues that you cannot settle whether God exists by reason, yet you must still live as if one way or the other. Pascal says you must wager, and it is not optional. Of betting on God he writes: “If you gain, you gain all; if you lose, you lose nothing.” The standard reconstruction sets this out as a table. If you bet on God and are right, you gain an infinitely happy life. If you bet on God and are wrong, you lose only something finite. If you bet against and God exists, you face what Pascal calls “misery”, and interpreters disagree about how bad that is: some read it as an infinite loss, others as a finite one. So, the argument goes, bet on God."
whyWrong: "The argument treats a decision about belief like a bet, and works out which option has the best expected result. This is standard decision theory. What is unusual is that it is asked to handle an infinite reward. Critics argue that infinity breaks the method, so that it no longer singles out betting on God, and that the same reasoning can be run for rival gods. Defenders argue that both problems can be answered. The main arguments on each side are set out below."
resolution:
  - "The main objections are about the decision table, not about whether God exists. The many gods objection asks why bet on this God rather than another, or on a god nobody has thought of, since each could promise or threaten something different. Denis Diderot's version said an imam could argue exactly the same way."
  - "The infinite utility objections go further. If you give the existence of God probability zero, rationality does not require you to wager for God. Several critics of the wager, including Mackie and Hacking, still conceded that the argument is valid if God has some positive probability. Duff (1986) and Hájek (2003) argue that it is not. A mixed strategy, such as betting on God only if a coin lands heads, has the same infinite expected value as betting outright, so on their view the argument cannot single out betting on God."
  - "Monton (2011) replies that if the coin lands tails you are back where you started, so it is rational to toss again, and with probability 1 you end up wagering for God. Robertson (2012) answers that some strategies, in which the chance of wagering for God shrinks fast enough on each try, need not lead there. Easwaran and Monton (2012) counter that if the choice can be repeated at uncountably many times you still end up wagering for God with probability 1, an idealisation that is not physically realisable."
  - "A further worry is whether belief can be chosen at all. Pascal imagines a person who says they are made so that they cannot believe. His reply is to follow the way believers began, “by acting as if they believed, taking the holy water, having masses said”, and to lessen the passions that stand in the way, in the hope that belief follows. William James, in The Will to Believe, objected that faith adopted after such a calculation “would lack the inner soul of faith’s reality”. This entry takes no side on whether God exists."
positions:
  - title: The argument works, with repairs
    body: "Defenders answer the main objections one by one. Against rival gods, Jordan suggests that outlandish ones can be dismissed for having “no backing of tradition”, and Lycan and Schlesinger (1989) argue that when the stakes are all infinite, the more probable god is the better bet. Against mixed strategies, Monton argues that they still end in wagering for God. Cost: it needs a principled reason to rank rival gods below Pascal’s, and whether these reasons succeed is disputed."
  - title: The argument fails
    body: "Because of the many gods and infinite utility problems, it gives no clear instruction. Diderot pressed the rival-gods objection, and Duff and Hájek the mixed-strategy one. Mackie raised the rival-gods problem while conceding that the argument is valid. Cost: Monton and others reply that the mixed-strategy objection fails, and defenders offer ways to rank rival gods."
  - title: Belief is not a bet
    body: "This objection says that belief adopted to win a bet is not genuine belief, which Wikipedia calls the argument from inauthentic belief. Cost: Pascal anticipates it. He does not ask the unbeliever to fake belief, but to act as believers did, “taking the holy water, having masses said”, so that belief follows."
finePrint: "The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says that Pascal apparently presents four arguments in a single section of the Pensées, of which only the third is traditionally called Pascal’s Wager, and it focuses on three main ones. The Pensées are unpublished notes compiled after his death. This entry covers the version most people mean. The question is treated here as one about decisions, and no view is taken on religion."
