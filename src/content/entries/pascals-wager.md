problem: "If reason cannot settle whether God exists, is believing in God the sensible bet?"
claimA: "A chance of infinite reward makes wagering for God the rational choice."
claimB: "Rival promises and rival strategies can offer infinite expected reward too, so that calculation does not make wagering for God the rational choice."
verdict: "Pascal’s calculation promises a decisive reason to wager for God. Critics dispute whether infinite rewards select that action, and defenders offer contested replies."
whatBreaks: "Whether the prospect of infinite reward selects wagering for God, once rival promises and strategies are considered."
statusNote: "critics argue that rival gods and strategies stop the calculation selecting one action. Defenders offer repairs, and there is no settled agreement on whether those repairs work."
setup: "Pascal asks how someone should act when reason has not settled whether God exists. Wagering for God might bring an infinitely happy life. If there is no God, the cost is finite. A positive chance of an infinite reward seems to outweigh any finite cost."
whyWrong: "The calculation seems decisive only if it picks out one action. Other gods could promise different rewards, and a strategy that wagers only after a coin toss can also have infinite expected value. There is also a question about whether belief can be chosen for a reward."
resolution:
  - "The dispute is about the argument’s decision rule, rather than a proof that God exists or does not exist. Infinite rewards make comparison harder than an ordinary financial bet."
  - "Defenders propose ways to rank rival gods or show that repeated opportunities still lead to wagering. Critics challenge those replies. The question of adopting belief adds a separate difficulty."
positions:

    body: "Defenders rank rival promises or argue that repeated chances to wager eventually lead to wagering for God. Cost: those rankings and idealised arguments need justification, and critics dispute them."
  - title: "The calculation does not select an action"
    body: "Critics argue that rival gods or strategies tie on infinite expected reward, or make the comparison undefined. Cost: defenders offer revised comparisons and replies to the proposed ties."
  - title: "Belief is not a bet"
    body: "Critics question whether calculated belief is possible or sincere. Cost: Pascal anticipated this and suggested adopting practices that might lead to belief, rather than simply pretending."
finePrint: "The Pensées were published after Pascal’s death in 1670. This entry covers the familiar infinite-reward reconstruction, not every argument in the notes. Interpretations differ on the loss if someone wagers against God. The argument assumes a positive chance of reward and an appropriate rule for comparing infinite values. Monton’s reply to coin-toss strategies involves repeated opportunities; Robertson challenges it with changing chances, and Easwaran and Monton reply using a stronger idealisation of time. Those technical exchanges do not settle the many-gods objection or the question of choosing belief."
