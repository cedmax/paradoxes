problem: Even if God probably does not exist, is it still the sensible bet to believe?
claimA: "An infinite reward, even if unlikely, outweighs any finite cost, so betting on God is wise."
claimB: The argument would let any promise of an infinite reward force you to act, and many rival promises exist.
verdict: Few accept it as stated, and the objections are about decision-making as well as religion.
whatBreaks: The idea that an unlimited payoff, however unlikely, always settles a decision.
statusNote: "philosophers still argue over whether the wager even works on its own terms. Critics such as Mackie and Hacking conceded it is valid, Duff and Hájek argue that mixed strategies make it invalid, and others have defended repaired versions."
setup: "Blaise Pascal, a French mathematician and writer who lived from 1623 to 1662, left notes that were published after his death as the Pensées in 1670. One passage argues that you cannot settle whether God exists by reason, yet you must still live as if one way or the other. Pascal says you must wager, and it is not optional. Of betting on God he writes: “If you gain, you gain all; if you lose, you lose nothing.” The standard reconstruction sets this out as a table. If you bet on God and are right, you gain an infinitely happy life. If you bet on God and are wrong, you lose only something finite. If you bet against and God exists, you face what Pascal calls “misery”, and interpreters disagree about how bad that is: some read it as an infinite loss, others as a finite one. So, the argument goes, bet on God."
whyWrong: "The argument treats a decision about belief like a bet, and works out which option has the best expected result. That method is respectable. The trouble is that it is being asked to handle an infinite reward, and infinity breaks the method. It stops telling you which option is better and starts saying almost anything is as good as anything else."
resolution:
  - "The main objections are about the decision table, not about whether God exists. The many gods objection asks why bet on this God rather than another, or on a god nobody has thought of, since each could promise or threaten something different. Denis Diderot's version said an imam could argue exactly the same way."
  - "The infinite utility objections go further. If you give the existence of God probability zero, the infinite reward is multiplied by zero and the argument fails. And a mixed strategy, such as betting on God only if a coin lands heads, has the same infinite expected value as betting outright, so the argument cannot favour one clear choice."
  - "A further worry is whether belief can be chosen at all. Pascal imagines a person who says they are made so that they cannot believe. His reply is to act as believers do, for example by attending services, in the hope that belief follows. Others have objected that belief adopted for gain is not real faith. None of this answers whether God exists, and the argument takes no side on that."
positions:
  - title: The argument works, with repairs
    body: "Some defenders restate it so that it avoids the many gods and zero-probability problems, for example by limiting which gods are serious candidates. Cost: it needs a reason to rule out the rivals, which the argument was meant to avoid."
  - title: The argument fails
    body: "Because of the many gods and infinite utility problems, it gives no clear instruction. Cost: it leaves open how to reason about enormous rewards that are very unlikely."
  - title: Belief is not a bet
    body: "Believing something because it pays off is not believing it, and a god who valued honest belief would not be fooled. Cost: this rests on a view about what belief and faith are, which not everyone shares."
finePrint: "The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says that Pascal apparently presents four arguments in a single section of the Pensées, of which only the third is traditionally called Pascal’s Wager, and it focuses on three main ones. The notes were left as rough, disordered pages. This entry covers the version most people mean. The question is treated here as one about decisions, and no view is taken on religion."
