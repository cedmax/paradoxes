titleAccent: "paradox"
chapter: belief
status: disputed
simplification: light

written: true
problem: "Why does “It's raining, but I don't believe it” sound absurd, when it could be true?"
claimA: "The sentence could be true, so saying it contradicts nothing."
claimB: "A sentence that sounds absurd to say must contain some contradiction."
verdict: "There is no generally accepted explanation."
whatBreaks: "The link between saying something, believing it, and being able to report your own beliefs."
statusNote: "experts agree the sentence is consistent yet absurd to say, but disagree on whether the absurdity is a contradiction and, if so, where it lies."
setup: "Suppose someone says, “It is raining, but I don't believe it.” There is nothing impossible about this. It might be raining while a particular person has not noticed, and then the sentence is true of them. A friend could say the same thing about you without any oddity: “It's raining, but he doesn't believe it.” Yet when you say it about yourself, it sounds absurd. A second version has the same problem: “It is raining, but I believe that it is not.”"
whyWrong: "It is not a contradiction in the way “It is raining and it is not raining” is. The two halves can both be true together, so what the sentence says is consistent. Yet saying it feels like contradicting yourself. Most proposed explanations still put a contradiction at the root, but in what the speaker believes or asserts rather than in what the sentence says. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy sums up the common view: the speaker manages to contradict himself without uttering a contradiction. Saying exactly how is the hard part."
resolution:
  - "The philosopher G. E. Moore noticed the oddity, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy dates his remark to 1942. Ludwig Wittgenstein thought it was Moore's most important contribution to philosophy. After Jaakko Hintikka's work, it became standard to split it into the two forms above."
  - "Wikipedia states that there is currently no generally accepted explanation. Proposals include that saying something presents you as believing it, that “I believe it is not raining” works as another way of saying “it is not raining”, and Richard Moran's view that the oddity comes from how we reason about what to believe."
positions:
  - title: "Saying it shows belief"
    body: "To state something is to present yourself as believing it. So the speaker claims a belief and denies it in one breath. Cost: it explains why the sentence is odd to say, but not why it is odd to think."
  - title: "“I believe” is a soft way of saying it"
    body: "“I believe it is not raining” often works as a cautious “it is not raining”, so “It is raining, but I believe it is not” comes down to “it is raining and it is not”. Cost: it only covers this second form, not “It is raining, but I don't believe it”, and “I believe” does not always work that way."
  - title: "It is about thinking, not speaking"
    body: "When we ask ourselves whether to believe something, we look at the facts, not at ourselves. This is Richard Moran's approach. Cost: it denies that the absurdity is a kind of contradiction at all, against most other explanations, which see one at the root."
finePrint: "Philosophers call the two forms omissive (“I don't believe it”) and commissive (“I believe it is not”). The three positions are a plain summary of a larger set of proposals, and the sources do not say that any of them has won. Roy Sorensen, among others, has linked this puzzle to the unexpected hanging paradox."
