problem: "Why does “It's raining, but I don't believe it” sound absurd, when it could be true?"
claimA: "The sentence could be true, so saying it contradicts nothing."
claimB: "A sentence that sounds absurd to say must contain some contradiction."
verdict: "A statement can be consistent yet sound absurd when sincerely asserted. Philosophers disagree about the connection between assertion, belief and awareness of belief."
whatBreaks: "Whether the apparent clash lies in what assertion communicates, in the speaker’s beliefs or in their reasoning about those beliefs."
statusNote: "experts agree the sentence is consistent yet absurd to say, but disagree on whether the absurdity is a contradiction and, if so, where it lies."
setup: "Someone says “It is raining, but I do not believe it”. It could be raining while they do not believe that it is. Another person can report this without oddity. But saying it sincerely about oneself sounds absurd. “It is raining, but I believe it is not” raises a related problem."
whyWrong: "The sentence is not simply “It is raining and it is not raining”. Its parts can both be true. The puzzle concerns what happens when a speaker sincerely presents them together, or thinks them together."
resolution:
  - "Many accounts locate a clash in the speaker’s commitments rather than in the sentence itself. Others explain the oddity without treating it as a contradiction."
  - "An answer needs to cover more than an awkward form of speech. It also needs to explain why holding the corresponding thought seems problematic."
positions:
  - title: "Assertion presents belief"
    body: "Stating that it is raining presents the speaker as believing it, while the second part denies that belief. Cost: this explains the spoken case more readily than the corresponding thought."
  - title: "“I believe” states the opposite"
    body: "In the second version, “I believe it is not raining” can act as a cautious assertion that it is not raining. Cost: this does not cover the version that merely denies belief, and the phrase does not always work that way."
  - title: "Look at how belief is formed"
    body: "Moran focuses on deciding what to believe by looking at the facts, rather than reporting oneself from outside. Cost: this challenges the common view that a contradiction explains the oddity."
finePrint: "Philosophers call the two forms omissive (“I don't believe it”) and commissive (“I believe it is not”). The three positions are a plain summary of a larger set of proposals, and the sources do not say that any of them has won. Roy Sorensen, among others, has linked this puzzle to the [unexpected hanging paradox](unexpected-hanging-paradox)."
