problem: "Why does “It's raining, but I don't believe it” sound absurd, when it could be true?"
claimA: "“It is raining, but I don't believe it” could be true, since the weather and my beliefs are separate things."
claimB: "Nobody could sensibly say it."
verdict: "There is no generally accepted explanation."
whatBreaks: "The link between saying something, believing it, and being able to report your own beliefs."
statusNote: "experts agree it is a real puzzle but disagree on how to explain it."
setup: "Suppose someone says, “It is raining, but I don't believe it.” There is nothing impossible about this. It might be raining while a particular person has not noticed, and then the sentence is true of them. A friend could say the same thing about you without any oddity: “It's raining, but he doesn't believe it.” Yet when you say it about yourself, it sounds absurd. A second version has the same problem: “It is raining, but I believe that it is not.”"
whyWrong: "It is not a contradiction in the way “It is raining and it is not raining” is. The two halves can both be true together. So the usual explanation, that the sentence contradicts itself, does not work. Something else is wrong, and it is hard to say what."
resolution:
  - "The philosopher G. E. Moore noticed the oddity, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy dates his remark to 1942. Ludwig Wittgenstein thought it was Moore's most important contribution to philosophy. After Jaakko Hintikka's work, it became standard to split it into the two forms above."
  - "Wikipedia states that there is currently no generally accepted explanation. Proposals include that saying something presents you as believing it, that “I believe it is raining” works as another way of saying “it is raining”, and Richard Moran's view that the oddity comes from how we reason about what to believe."
positions:
  - title: Saying it shows belief
    body: "To state something is to present yourself as believing it. So the speaker claims a belief and denies it in one breath. Cost: it explains why the sentence is odd to say, but not why it is odd to think."
  - title: “I believe” is a soft way of saying it
    body: "“I believe it is raining” mostly works as a cautious “it is raining”, so the speaker is saying both that it is and that it is not. Cost: “I believe” does not always work that way, so this does not cover every case."
  - title: It is about thinking, not speaking
    body: "When we ask ourselves whether to believe something, we look at the facts, not at ourselves. This is Richard Moran's approach. Cost: it commits you to a particular view of how we know our own minds, which not everyone accepts."
finePrint: "The omissive and commissive names for the two forms come from the literature that followed Hintikka. The three positions are a plain summary of a larger set of proposals, and the sources do not say that any of them has won. Some thinkers, including Chow, have linked this puzzle to the unexpected hanging paradox."
