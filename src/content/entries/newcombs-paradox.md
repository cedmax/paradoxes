title: Newcomb's paradox
titleAccent: paradox
chapter: choice-identity
status: disputed
simplification: light
order: 2
written: true
sourcesVerified: false
problem: "Box A holds $1,000, and a near-perfect predictor has already decided whether Box B holds $1,000,000. Do you take Box B alone, or both?"
claimA: "A rational chooser takes both boxes, because the contents are already fixed."
claimB: "A rational chooser takes one box, because one-boxers reliably end up richer."
verdict: Two respected rules of reasoning give different answers.
whatBreaks: "Experts split on which rule of rational choice to follow: look only at what your choice can cause, which says take both, or look at what your choice is evidence of, which says take one."
statusNote: "the split lines up roughly with two theories of rational choice: causal decision theory says take both boxes and evidential decision theory says take one, though not every one-boxer relies on evidential decision theory. In the 2020 PhilPapers survey, 39.0 percent of philosophers favoured two boxes and 31.2 percent one box, a plurality but no majority."
setup: "There are two boxes. Box A is open and holds $1,000. Box B is closed and holds either $1,000,000 or nothing. You can take both boxes, or take only Box B. Before you decide, a predictor who is almost always right about people like you has already guessed what you will do. If it guessed you would take only B, it put the million in. If it guessed you would take both, it left B empty. The money is already in place."
whyWrong: "There are two arguments, and both look airtight. The first says the boxes are already filled and nothing you do now can change them, so in either case taking both gives you $1,000 more than taking one. The second says people who take only B nearly always find the million, and people who take both nearly always get only the $1,000, so take B. Each argument uses a rule of reasoning people normally trust, and here they point opposite ways, so a rational chooser cannot follow both."
resolution:
  - "Philosophers still disagree about which rule gives way. In the 2020 PhilPapers survey of professional philosophers, 39.0 percent favoured taking both boxes and 31.2 percent favoured taking one, a modest plurality for two boxes but no majority. The rest gave other answers."
positions:
  - title: Take both boxes
    body: "What is in Box B is already settled and your choice cannot change it, so take everything on offer. This is the view of causal decision theory. Cost: the people who reason this way end up with far less money."
  - title: Take one box
    body: "Choose the option that goes with the best outcome, and one-boxers are the ones who get the million. This is the view of evidential decision theory, though some one-boxers argue for one box on other grounds. Wikipedia reports that Gary Drescher, in his book Good and Real (2006), argues for taking only Box B by appealing to what he argues is an analogous case: a rational agent in a deterministic universe deciding whether to cross a potentially busy street. Cost: it treats your choice as evidence about something it cannot actually influence."
  - title: It depends on the predictor
    body: "Wikipedia notes that different accounts of why the predictor is so accurate give different answers. If your choice somehow causes the prediction, for example through backwards causation, taking one box is clearly right, and if the predictor is infallible because your choice is fixed in advance, there may be no real choice to advise on. David Wolpert and Gregory Benford argue that the problem leaves out details, and the two arguments answer two different ways of filling them in. Cost: it can look like a way of dodging the question as asked, since Nozick’s version stipulates that the predictor is almost certainly right, which sidesteps questions of infallibility and causation. Nozick himself, Wikipedia says, defended taking one box in the case of an infallible predictor and two boxes in other cases."
finePrint: The problem was invented by the physicist William Newcomb and first published by the philosopher Robert Nozick in 1969. The puzzle is meant to test theories of rational choice, not to give advice for a game show, since no real predictor is known to be this good.
