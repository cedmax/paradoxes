problem: "Two travellers each name a value for identical lost luggage, and whoever names less gets a small bonus. Why does strict logic push both down to the minimum?"
claimA: "Rational players who know each other to be rational both write 2, the lowest number allowed."
claimB: "Rational players can write a large number, expecting the other to do the same."
verdict: "Formal game theory picks the lowest claim, most people claim high and earn more, and whether claiming high is rational is still argued."
whatBreaks: "Whether shared rationality requires undercutting down to $2, or can support expecting a high claim."
statusNote: "the unique equilibrium is agreed. There is no agreed answer about whether ideal rational players can reasonably reject it."
setup: "An airline loses two identical antiques. Their owners separately claim a value from $2 to $100. Equal claims are paid in full. For unequal claims, both receive the lower amount, but the lower claimant gets a $2 bonus and the higher claimant loses $2."
whyWrong: "If the other person claims $100, claiming $99 pays $101. So each has a reason to undercut $100. Once $100 is ruled out, the same argument rules out $99, then $98, and so on down to $2. Both end up with $2 even though matching claims of $100 would pay each $100."
resolution:
  - "Both claiming $2 is the only outcome in which neither can gain by changing their own claim alone. The calculation is agreed."
  - "The dispute is whether ideal rationality must follow the undercutting argument all the way down. Real participants often claim much more and earn more, but that does not by itself resolve what the ideal players should do."
positions:

    body: "Follow the undercutting argument to its end. Cost: both earn very little compared with players who claim high."
  - title: "Expect a high claim"
    body: "Basu argues that rejecting the bottom and expecting the other player to do likewise has a rational appeal. Cost: this suggestion does not supply a settled replacement for the formal argument."
  - title: "Model real reasoning"
    body: "Allow uncertainty about the other player or model how people think about cooperation. Cost: explaining actual choices leaves the ideal case open."
finePrint: "Undercutting gives weakly better payoffs than the next larger claim while that larger claim remains possible. Repeated elimination produces $2. This uses stronger assumptions about shared rationality than simply saying both people are clever. Writing $3 against $2 pays $0. Basu published the game in 1994; the title retains his American spelling. Experiments find that the size of the bonus and penalty affects how close play comes to the equilibrium. Unlike the [centipede game](centipede-game), both players act once and simultaneously."
