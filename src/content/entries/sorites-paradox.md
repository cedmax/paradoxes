problem: If you remove grains from a heap of sand one at a time, when does it stop being a heap?
claimA: "Taking away one grain never turns a heap into a non-heap."
claimB: "A million grains of sand make a heap, and a single grain does not."
verdict: Philosophers disagree on where the argument breaks.
whatBreaks: "Either the rule that one grain never makes the difference or the claim that a million grains make a heap has to go, and experts split on which, and on how."
statusNote: "the main responses blame the one-grain rule but disagree on how it fails: a sharp edge nobody can know, truth that comes in degrees, or a cut-off at no particular grain. A minority, the nihilists, keep the rule and deny that there are any heaps."
setup: A million grains of sand piled up are a heap. One grain on its own is not. Take a single grain away from a heap and it is still a heap, because nobody can see the difference. But keep doing that, and the heap shrinks to one grain, which is not a heap. Somewhere a heap became not a heap. Where?
whyWrong: "Words like “heap”, “tall”, “bald” and “rich” have no sharp edge. They work fine for clear cases and fall apart in between. The paradox takes that blur and chains it step by step, using the rule that a tiny change can never matter, until the blur swallows the whole pile. If one grain never makes the difference, then a million grains and one grain must both be heaps, or both not be."
resolution:
  - "The epistemic, supervaluationist, degree and contextualist responses all find fault with the one-grain rule, each in a different way. Contextualists, for example, say that what counts as a heap shifts as you go along the series. A few philosophers, the nihilists Peter Unger and Samuel Wheeler (both 1979), keep the rule and give up the other claim instead: words like “heap” apply to nothing, so there were never any heaps to begin with. Others deny that the argument is valid at all."
  - "The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that most theorists of vagueness suppose the paradox is solvable: the argument is defective somewhere, and the defect can be found. The disagreement is over which claim is at fault, and how."
positions:
  - title: There is a sharp edge
    body: "The one-grain rule is false. Somewhere one grain really does turn a heap into a non-heap, but nobody can ever know which grain. This is epistemicism, defended by Timothy Williamson and Roy Sorensen. Cost: it is hard to believe that a single grain can make the difference, and even its defenders grant that the view is counter-intuitive."
  - title: Truth comes in degrees
    body: "Being a heap comes in degrees, and so does the truth of “this is a heap”. Each grain taken away makes it slightly less true, and enough slight changes add up. So the one-grain rule is not fully true. Cost: it needs a logic with more than true and false, and it is unclear what a degree of truth is, or why a sentence should get one number rather than another."
  - title: Some grain matters, but no particular one
    body: "A word like “heap” could be made sharp in many acceptable ways. On every one of them some grain is the last, so the one-grain rule is false, but there is no particular grain of which it is true that it is the cut-off. This is supervaluationism. Cost: it still says a cut-off exists, which looks like the sharp edge it was meant to avoid."
  - title: There are no heaps
    body: "Keep the one-grain rule and give up the other claim. Words like “heap”, “bald” and “rich” apply to nothing, so a million grains are not a heap either. Peter Unger and Samuel Wheeler argued this in 1979. Cost: it denies that there are any heaps, bald people or rich people at all."
finePrint: These are four of several proposed answers, shown in plain terms. Each has defenders, and each faces objections in the literature.
