problem: "An arrow leaves a bow pointing slightly sideways at full draw. Why does it still fly straight at the target?"
claimA: "An arrow can only leave in the direction it pointed when released, which was a little to one side of the target."
claimB: "The arrow flies towards the target."
verdict: "The arrow bends around the bow as it is released, then straightens in flight."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that the arrow stays straight and rigid while it leaves the bow."
statusNote: "the first claim is wrong. The arrow flexes out of the way of the bow and returns to the correct path as it leaves."
setup: "When the string is drawn back on a traditional bow, the arrow points a little to the side of the target. Wikipedia describes the paradox as the arrow travelling in the direction it is pointed at full draw, when it seems it would have to pass through its starting position, where it pointed to the side of the target. Wikipedia reports that the term was first used by E. J. Rendtroff in 1913, and that detailed descriptions appear in archery writing as early as Horace A. Ford’s 1859 book Archery: Its Theory and Practice."
whyWrong: "The arrow is not a rigid rod. Wikipedia says the bending of the arrow when released is the explanation for why the paradox occurs, and should not be confused with the paradox itself. It flexes around and out of the way of the bow as it is shot, as first filmed by Clarence Hickman, and then bends back and forth as it flies to the target."
resolution:
  - "For accuracy, an arrow must have the right stiffness, called its dynamic spine, so that it flexes out of the way of the bow and returns to the correct path as it leaves. Wikipedia says that if the spine is wrong, contact between arrow and bow is unpredictable and accuracy suffers."
  - "Less powerful bows need arrows with less spine, and powerful bows need stiffer arrows. An arrow that is too stiff for the bow will not flex and will be forced off to the side. One that is too flexible will deform too much and be sent to the other side of the target. In extreme cases the arrow may break, which can be a safety hazard."
  - "Wikipedia notes that many modern bows are “centre shot”, with a cutout in the middle of the bow so the arrow always points along its line of flight and shows no paradoxical behaviour. Arrows of the correct spine are still used with them, and the term is often misused for the arrow’s flexing in flight."
finePrint: "Wikipedia reports that a technique called khatra, which involves applying torque to the bow so that it clears the arrow’s path on release, is another way to avoid the problem. The article also gives test methods for measuring spine, which are not covered here."
