problem: "On a traditional bow the arrow rests against the side of the bow and points a little off the target. Why does it still fly to the target?"
claimA: "An arrow stays straight as it leaves the bow, so one pointing to the side of the target misses."
claimB: "An arrow pointing to one side can still hit the target."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that the arrow stays straight while it leaves the bow. It flexes around the grip and comes out on the line to the target."
statusNote: "the first claim is wrong. The arrow bends as the string slips off the fingers, flexes around the grip of the bow and leaves on the line to the target, as high-speed film has shown."
setup: "On a traditional bow, the grip is in the way, so the arrow cannot lie along the middle of the bow. For a right-handed archer it rests on the left side. At full draw the back of the arrow is on the string, in line with the middle of the bow, while the front lies to the left of the grip. So the arrow points a little to the left of the target. It seems it should fly off to the left. Yet a good archer hits the target. The word “paradox” for this was first used by E. J. Rendtorff in 1913, in the magazine Forest and Stream, and archery writers had described the effect much earlier, including Horace A. Ford in his 1859 book Archery: Its Theory and Practice."
whyWrong: "The tempting picture treats the arrow as a stiff rod. A real arrow bends. When the archer lets go, the string slips off the finger tips and moves sideways, so the back of the arrow is pushed sideways too. At the same time the string drives the arrow forwards very hard, with a push that no longer acts straight along it, and the arrow bends. The bend carries the middle of the arrow out and around the grip instead of through it."
resolution:
  - "Clarence Hickman filmed this with high-speed cameras, first in 1930 at 2,000 frames a second. The arrow bends around the bow, leaves the string still curved, and keeps bending back and forth as it flies towards the target. B. W. Kooi and J. A. Sparenberg modelled this motion in 1997, and their results fit high-speed film well."
  - "For this to work, the arrow’s stiffness, called its spine, must suit the bow, so that its bending carries it past the grip at the right moment. A more powerful bow needs a stiffer arrow. An arrow that is too stiff for the bow does not bend enough and is forced off to the side. One that is too flexible bends too much and is sent off to the other side, and in extreme cases it can break, which is a safety hazard."
  - "So the first claim is false and the second is true. Many modern bows are “centre shot”, a design Hickman introduced: a cutout in the middle of the bow lets the arrow lie on the line to the target, so the original puzzle does not arise. The arrow still flexes, mainly because the string moves sideways off the fingers, and arrows of the right spine are still needed. The flexing in flight is often called the archer’s paradox too, though strictly it is the explanation, not the paradox."
finePrint: "Another way round the problem, a technique called khatra, is to twist the bow at the moment of release so that it moves out of the arrow’s path. The 1913 author’s name is spelled Rendtroff on Wikipedia and Rendtorff in Kooi’s history. Directions here are for a right-handed archer and swap for a left-handed one."
