problem: "A coin is tossed while Sleeping Beauty sleeps. When she wakes, how likely is it that it landed heads?"
claimA: "It was a fair coin and she has learned nothing, so the chance is one half."
claimB: "Counting every time she wakes, only one in three follows heads."
verdict: "Both answers have serious defenders, and no side has won."
whatBreaks: "The rule that you only change your odds when you learn something new."
statusNote: "experts agree it is a real puzzle but disagree on which claim to give up."
setup: "On Sunday, Sleeping Beauty is put to sleep. A fair coin is tossed. If it lands heads, she is woken once, on Monday. If it lands tails, she is woken on Monday and again on Tuesday. After each waking she is given a drug that wipes her memory of it, so each waking feels identical. She knows all of this in advance. When she wakes, she is asked: how likely is it that the coin landed heads?"
whyWrong: "There are two arguments. The first says the coin was fair, she knew about the whole set-up beforehand, and nothing she sees on waking tells her anything about the coin. So her answer should stay at one half. The second says to think about all the wakings. Over many runs of the experiment, tails produces two wakings for every one that heads produces. Of 100 runs, about 50 have heads and 50 wakings. About 50 have tails and 100 wakings. So of 150 wakings, only 50 follow heads. That is one in three. Each argument looks sound, and they cannot both be right."
resolution:
  - "There is no agreed answer. The people who say one third are called thirders, and the people who say one half are called halfers. Adam Elga defended one third in a well known paper in 2000. David Lewis replied in 2001 that one half is right. The debate has continued since, and neither side has won."
  - "The disagreement is about what counts as learning something. Halfers say she learns nothing new, so her odds do not move. Thirders say she does learn something, namely that she is awake right now, and that this is more likely to happen if the coin landed tails."
positions:
  - title: "One third"
    body: "Count the wakings. If she were offered a bet on each waking, she would only break even by treating heads as one chance in three. Cost: her odds move from one half to one third with no new fact about the coin, and Lewis argued that she has learned nothing."
  - title: "One half"
    body: "The coin was fair and she has learned nothing, so her belief stays where it was. Cost: if she is then told that it is Monday, she must believe heads has a two in three chance, which seems strange for a fair coin. Critics also argue that halfers can be made to lose money in a series of bets."
  - title: "The question has two meanings"
    body: "Some, such as Berry Groisman, say the question is ambiguous. The chance that the coin landed heads is one half. The chance that this waking follows heads is one third. Cost: it can feel like dodging, since people want one answer to the question as it was asked."
finePrint: "The problem was developed by Arnold Zuboff in the mid 1980s and published in 1990, and it became widely known through Adam Elga's paper in 2000. The two numbers are not in dispute as arithmetic. The dispute is over which one answers the question, and over how beliefs about where you are in time should change. Related versions, such as one where tails brings a million wakings, push people's intuitions further and are still argued over."
