problem: "A coin is tossed while Sleeping Beauty sleeps. When she wakes, how likely is it that it landed heads?"
claimA: "When she wakes, she should give heads one chance in two, since she learns nothing new."
claimB: "When she wakes, she should give heads one chance in three, since two of every three wakings follow tails."
verdict: "Both answers have serious defenders, and no side has won."
whatBreaks: "Whether being awake right now should change her belief about the coin. Experts split, and some say the two answers address different questions."
statusNote: "both sides accept that two of every three wakings follow tails, but philosophers still split over whether that count should set her belief on waking, giving one third, or whether she should stay at one half."
setup: "A fair coin decides Sleeping Beauty’s wakings. Heads means one, on Monday. Tails means two, on Monday and Tuesday. After each waking her memory is erased, so they feel identical. She knows the rules. On waking, what chance should she give heads?"
whyWrong: "One argument says the coin was fair and waking tells her nothing new about it: one half. Another counts wakings. Heads produces one waking per heads result; tails produces two. Over many experiments, only one waking in three follows heads."
resolution:
  - "Halfers such as Lewis say waking gives her no new information about the coin, so she should retain one half. Thirders such as Elga argue that the indistinguishable possibilities should each get one third. They dispute the answer to her belief question, not the arithmetic of repeated experiments."
  - "Elga’s argument uses two symmetry claims. Given tails, Monday and Tuesday should seem equally likely. Given Monday, heads and tails should seem equally likely. Together these make heads-Monday, tails-Monday and tails-Tuesday equally likely. Halfers challenge that reasoning. A separate response says there are two questions; that is one disputed position, not the framework in which all sides agree to debate."
positions:
  - title: "One third"
    body: "Treat this waking as one among the indistinguishable wakings. Bets offered at each waking favour this calculation. Cost: the probability changes without a new fact about the coin, which halfers challenge."
  - title: "One half"
    body: "Keep the fair-coin probability because she learns nothing about its result. Cost: Lewis’s version gives two thirds if she then learns it is Monday; other versions must change familiar updating rules, and betting objections remain."
  - title: "There are two questions"
    body: "Distinguish the probability for an experiment from the share of wakings following heads. Cost: critics say this does not settle the requested belief at this particular waking."
finePrint: "Elga defended one third in 2000 and Lewis one half in 2001, following earlier work by Zuboff. Over many trials, about half the coin tosses are heads but only a third of the wakings follow heads. Lewis’s and double-halfer accounts differ in what happens when Monday is announced. Betting arguments also depend on when bets are offered and whether commitments carry across wakings."
