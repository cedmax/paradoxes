problem: "A coin is tossed while Sleeping Beauty sleeps. When she wakes, how likely is it that it landed heads?"
claimA: "When she wakes, she should give heads one chance in two, since she learns nothing new."
claimB: "When she wakes, she should give heads one chance in three, since two of every three wakings follow tails."
verdict: "Both answers have serious defenders, and no side has won."
whatBreaks: "Whether being awake right now should change her belief about the coin. Experts split, and some say the two answers address different questions."
statusNote: "both sides accept that two of every three wakings follow tails, but philosophers still split over whether that count should set her belief on waking, giving one third, or whether she should stay at one half."
setup: "On Sunday, Sleeping Beauty is put to sleep. A fair coin is tossed. If it lands heads, she is woken once, on Monday. If it lands tails, she is woken on Monday and again on Tuesday. After each waking she is given a drug that wipes her memory of it, so each waking feels identical. She knows all of this in advance. When she wakes, she is asked: how likely is it that the coin landed heads?"
whyWrong: "There are two arguments. The first says the coin was fair, she knew about the whole set-up beforehand, and nothing she sees on waking tells her anything about the coin. So when she wakes, her belief in heads should stay at one half. The second says her belief should follow the count of wakings. Over many runs of the experiment, tails produces two wakings for every one that heads produces. Of 100 runs, about 50 have heads and 50 wakings. About 50 have tails and 100 wakings. So of 150 wakings, only 50 follow heads. So when she wakes, she should give heads one chance in three. Each argument looks sound, and as answers to the same question they cannot both be right."
resolution:
  - "There is no agreed answer. The people who say one third are called thirders, and the people who say one half are called halfers. Adam Elga defended one third in a well known paper in 2000. David Lewis replied in 2001 that one half is right. The debate has continued since, and neither side has won."
  - "The two sides argue in different ways. Halfers, following Lewis, say she gains no new information about the coin when she wakes, so her belief stays at one half. Elga’s argument for one third rests on indifference instead. If she knew the coin had landed tails, she should think Monday and Tuesday equally likely, since the two wakings would feel the same. If she knew it was Monday, she should think heads and tails equally likely, since the coin is fair. Together these make the three possible wakings, heads and Monday, tails and Monday, and tails and Tuesday, equally likely, one third each."
positions:
  - title: "One third"
    body: "Count the wakings. If she were offered a bet on each waking, she would only break even by treating heads as one chance in three. Cost: her odds move from one half to one third with no new fact about the coin, and Lewis argued that she has learned nothing."
  - title: "One half"
    body: "The coin was fair and she has learned nothing, so her belief stays where it was. Cost: on David Lewis's version, if she is then told that it is Monday, she must believe heads has a two in three chance, which seems strange for a fair coin. Double halfers, such as Mikaël Cozic, keep one half on Monday too, but only by changing the usual rule for updating beliefs on facts like it is Monday. Critics also argue that halfers can be made to lose money in a series of bets."
  - title: "The question has two meanings"
    body: "Some, such as Berry Groisman, say the question is ambiguous. The chance that the coin landed heads is one half. The chance that this waking follows heads is one third. Cost: it can feel like dodging, since people want one answer to the question as it was asked."
finePrint: "The problem was developed by Arnold Zuboff in the mid 1980s and published in 1990, and it became widely known through Adam Elga's paper in 2000. The two numbers are not in dispute as arithmetic. The dispute is over which one answers the question, and over how beliefs about where you are in time should change. Related versions include an “extreme” one, formulated by Nick Bostrom, in which tails brings one million and one wakings."
