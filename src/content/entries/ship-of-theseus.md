title: "Ship of Theseus paradox"
format: argument
chapter: identity
status: disputed
simplification: light
noMaths: true
order: 70
sourcesVerified: true
problem: Replace every plank of a ship, one at a time. Is it still the same ship?
claimA: Each swap leaves it the same ship.
claimB: "A ship with none of its original planks is not the original ship."
whatBreaks: "Either the idea that a single swap never changes which ship it is, or the idea that a ship with none of its original planks is a different ship, has to go. Experts split on which."
statusNote: "views that value continuity make the repaired ship the original, which weakens the rebuilt ship’s claim, views that make every part essential end the original at the first repair, and the verbal view gives no single identity judgement. Hobbes’s rebuilt ship is what splits them."
setup: "A ship’s worn planks are replaced one at a time. Eventually none of the original timber remains. Each repair seems to leave the same ship, but the final vessel has none of its original material. Is it still Theseus’s ship?"
whyWrong: "Nobody disagrees about the planks. Everyone agrees which ones were swapped and when. In daily life people judge “the same ship” by two tests at once: one continuous object that changes bit by bit, and an object made of particular materials. Normally these go together. Here they come apart. Some philosophers hold that one test is the right one, while others suggest the dispute is partly about how the words are used."
resolution:
  - "Add a second ship made by collecting and rebuilding all the discarded planks. The continuously repaired ship has the history; the rebuilt ship has the original material. Both cannot be the same single original ship."
  - "The disagreement is over what makes an object persist: its continuity, its parts or how the word “same” is used. Different theories sacrifice different ordinary judgements."
positions:
  - title: "Continuity matters"
    body: "The ship survives gradual repairs, so the continuously maintained vessel is the original. Cost: the vessel rebuilt from the original planks loses its apparently strong claim."
  - title: "Every part is essential"
    body: "Strictly, losing any part ends that particular object; ordinary talk tracks a series of successors. Cost: the original ship ends at the first repair, and ordinary objects would change identity at tiny alterations."
  - title: "There is no unique verbal answer"
    body: "Different acceptable uses of “same ship” track different features of the agreed history. Cost: this does not supply a single identity judgement, and only some philosophers accept it."
finePrint: "These are three of several proposals, simplified. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on material constitution covers them, along with others such as temporal parts."
