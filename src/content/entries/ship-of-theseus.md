---
title: Ship of Theseus
chapter: choice-identity
status: disputed
simplification: light
order: 1
written: true
problem: Replace every plank of a ship, one at a time. Is it still the same ship?
claimA: Each swap leaves it the same ship.
claimB: At the end, none of the original remains.
verdict: It depends on what 'same' means, and philosophers disagree.
whatBreaks: The idea that being the same thing over time has a single clear meaning.
statusNote: experts agree it is a real puzzle but disagree on which claim to give up.
setup: An ancient story, told by the Greek writer Plutarch, says the Athenians kept the ship of the hero Theseus in harbour for centuries. As planks rotted they were replaced with new ones, until no original plank was left. Was it still Theseus's ship?
whyWrong: Nobody disagrees about the planks. We all know which ones were swapped and when. The disagreement is about the words. In daily life, "the same ship" means two things at once, one continuous object that changes bit by bit, and an object made of particular materials. Normally these go together. Here they come apart.
resolution:
  - "Later thinkers, Thomas Hobbes among them, made it worse. Suppose someone collects every discarded plank and rebuilds the ship from them. Now there are two candidates for the original, and they cannot both be it."
  - "Philosophers still disagree about which step to give up. Each answer costs something."
positions:
  - title: Continuity makes it the same
    body: "A thing stays the same through gradual change if the changes keep its form and function. The repaired ship is the original. Cost: the ship rebuilt from the old planks has a strong claim too, and this view says it is not the original."
  - title: The material makes it the same
    body: "A thing is its parts. Change them all and it is a different thing. Cost: every repaired object stops being itself after the first replacement, which makes ordinary talk about things lasting through repairs false."
  - title: There is no single answer
    body: "Whether it is the same ship depends on why you are asking, for example who owns it, or what a museum should display. Cost: it treats identity as a convenient label, and many philosophers think things have real identity."
finePrint: These are three of several proposals. The puzzle also has a long run of variations, from repaired bodies to rebuilt cities, and the same split shows up in each.
