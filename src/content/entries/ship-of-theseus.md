title: Ship of Theseus
chapter: choice-identity
status: disputed
simplification: light
order: 1
written: true
sourcesVerified: false
problem: Replace every plank of a ship, one at a time. Is it still the same ship?
claimA: Each swap leaves it the same ship.
claimB: "A ship with none of its original planks is not the original ship."
verdict: "Philosophers disagree on what keeps a thing the same: its unbroken history or its original material."
whatBreaks: "Experts split on what keeps a thing the same over time: an unbroken history of gradual change, which keeps the first claim, or its original material, which keeps the second. Some add that the dispute is partly about words."
statusNote: "philosophers disagree on whether a thing survives the gradual replacement of all its parts, and, once Hobbes’s rebuilt ship is added, on which of the two ships is the original."
setup: "An ancient story, told by the Greek writer Plutarch, says the Athenians kept the ship of the hero Theseus for centuries. As the old planks decayed they took them away and put in new timber, and philosophers argued over whether it was still the same ship. Later tellings push this to the limit: the planks are replaced one by one until no original plank is left. Is it still Theseus’s ship?"
whyWrong: "Nobody disagrees about the planks. We all know which ones were swapped and when. In daily life we judge “the same ship” by two tests at once: one continuous object that changes bit by bit, and an object made of particular materials. Normally these go together. Here they come apart. Some philosophers hold that one test is the right one, while others suggest the dispute is partly about how we use the words."
resolution:
  - "In the modern era Thomas Hobbes made it worse. Suppose a custodian collects every discarded plank and puts them back together in the original arrangement. Now there are two ships. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy sets out the split: some say the original is the repaired ship, since boats can survive the gradual replacement of parts, and others say it is the custodian’s, since things can survive being taken apart and put back together. As the entry puts it, “Both arguments seem compelling, but together they imply that the ship survives twice over.”"
  - "Philosophers still disagree about which step to give up. Each answer costs something."
positions:
  - title: Continuity makes it the same
    body: "A thing stays the same through gradual change if the changes keep its form and function. The repaired ship is the original. Cost: the ship rebuilt from the old planks has a strong claim too, and this view says it is not the original."
  - title: The material makes it the same
    body: "A thing is its parts. Roderick Chisholm defended the view that nothing can survive losing any of its parts. Cost: every repaired object stops being itself after the first replacement, which makes ordinary talk about things lasting through repairs false."
  - title: There is no single answer
    body: "Everyone agrees on what happened to every plank, so some philosophers suggest the dispute is partly about words: there may be equally good ways of using “same ship” that give different answers. Cost: only some philosophers accept this, and it still leaves open which way of speaking to use."
finePrint: "These are three of several proposals, simplified. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on material constitution covers them, along with others such as temporal parts. The puzzle also has a long run of variations, from repaired bodies to rebuilt cities, and the same split shows up in each."
