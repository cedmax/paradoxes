title: Ship of Theseus
chapter: choice-identity
status: disputed
simplification: light
order: 1
written: true
sourcesVerified: true
problem: Replace every plank of a ship, one at a time. Is it still the same ship?
claimA: Each swap leaves it the same ship.
claimB: "A ship with none of its original planks is not the original ship."
verdict: "Philosophers disagree on what keeps a thing the same: its unbroken history or its original material."
whatBreaks: "Either the idea that a single swap never changes which ship it is, or the idea that a ship with none of its original planks is a different ship, has to go. Experts split on which."
statusNote: "philosophers disagree on whether a thing survives the gradual replacement of all its parts, and, once Hobbes’s rebuilt ship is added, on which of the two ships is the original."
setup: "An ancient story, told by the Greek writer Plutarch, says the Athenians kept the ship of the hero Theseus down to the time of Demetrius of Phalerum, an Athenian statesman of the late fourth century BC. As the old planks decayed they took them away and put in new timber, and philosophers argued over whether it was still the same ship. Later tellings push this to the limit: the planks are replaced one by one until no original plank is left. Is it still Theseus’s ship?"
whyWrong: "Nobody disagrees about the planks. Everyone agrees which ones were swapped and when. In daily life people judge “the same ship” by two tests at once: one continuous object that changes bit by bit, and an object made of particular materials. Normally these go together. Here they come apart. Some philosophers hold that one test is the right one, while others suggest the dispute is partly about how the words are used."
resolution:
  - "In the modern era Thomas Hobbes made it worse. Suppose a custodian collects every discarded plank and puts them back together in the original arrangement. Now there are two ships. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy sets out the split: some say the original is the repaired ship, since boats can survive the gradual replacement of parts, and others say it is the custodian’s, since things can survive being taken apart and put back together. As the entry puts it, “Both arguments seem compelling, but together they imply that the ship survives twice over.”"
  - "Philosophers still disagree about which step to give up. Each answer costs something."
positions:
  - title: Continuity makes it the same
    body: "Boats can survive the gradual replacement of their parts, so the repaired ship is the original. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy gives this as one side of Hobbes’s puzzle, and its entry on identity over time reports that J. Mooney, writing in 2025, holds that the ship rebuilt from the old planks is not a candidate to be the original but only a replica. Cost: the ship rebuilt from the old planks has a strong claim too, and this view says it is not the original."
  - title: Every part is essential
    body: "Roderick Chisholm defended the view that a thing has its parts essentially, so strictly speaking nothing survives losing any of its parts. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy classes this as a form of eliminativism: it denies that there are objects, like the ship, that survive the replacement of parts. Ordinary talk can still come out true in what Chisholm calls a loose and popular sense, in which a series of closely related successor objects counts as one ship. Cost: in the strict sense the ship stops existing at the first replacement, and so would you if you lost a single particle."
  - title: There is no single answer
    body: "Everyone agrees on what happened to every plank, so some philosophers suggest the dispute is partly about words: there may be equally good ways of using “same ship” that give different answers. Cost: only some philosophers accept this, and it still leaves open which way of speaking to use."
finePrint: "These are three of several proposals, simplified. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on material constitution covers them, along with others such as temporal parts."
