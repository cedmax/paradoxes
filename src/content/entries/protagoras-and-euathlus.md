problem: "A teacher sues his student for unpaid fees. Who wins?"
claimA: "If Protagoras wins, the court makes Euathlus pay; if Euathlus wins, the contract does."
claimB: "Until Euathlus wins a case he owes nothing, so this court has nothing to make him pay."
verdict: "Euathlus should win this case, since nothing is owed yet. Protagoras could then sue again and win. This is the usual answer in the literature."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that winning this very case can make the fee due in this very case."
statusNote: "when the suit is brought Euathlus has not yet won a case, so nothing is owed and he should win. Peter Suber reports that the small literature on the case all suggests Euathlus should have won, and that most commentators observe Protagoras could then sue a second time and win."
setup: "Protagoras, a teacher of the ancient Greek world, agreed to teach a student named Euathlus the law. According to Gellius, Euathlus paid half the fee before the lessons began, and agreed to pay the rest on the day he first pleaded a case in court and won it. Euathlus then went into politics instead of the law. Protagoras sued him for the fee. Protagoras argued: if I win, the court orders you to pay. If you win, you have now won your first case, so the contract says you must pay. Euathlus replied: if I win, the court says I owe nothing. If you win, I still have not won a case, so the contract says I owe nothing."
whyWrong: "Both arguments work from rules we normally trust: do what the court orders, and do what the contract says. The case is about whether Euathlus must pay on a contract that depends on the result of that very case, so the ruling and the contract each seem to claim the last word."
resolution:
  - "The story comes from the Roman writer Aulus Gellius, in his Attic Nights. He reports that the judges found the arguments uncertain and insoluble. They feared that whichever way they ruled, the ruling would undo itself, so they put the case off."
  - "The usual answer is simpler than the judges feared. The contract makes the fee due once Euathlus wins a case. When Protagoras sues, Euathlus has not won one, so nothing is owed yet, and he should win. The contract is not ignored: it is applied as it stands on the day of the suit."
  - "That win is Euathlus's first. Now the contract's condition is met, so Protagoras can sue a second time and win. Peter Suber, in his book The Paradox of Self-Amendment, reports that the small literature on the case all suggests Euathlus should have won, and that most commentators then observe that Protagoras could have sued again and won."
  - "So Protagoras's argument is the one that fails. Its second half counts Euathlus's win in this case as triggering the fee in this same case, when it can only trigger it afterwards. Euathlus's own argument is no sounder. Its second half says that if Protagoras wins in court, Euathlus still owes nothing, which ignores the court's order just as Protagoras's second half ignores the contract. Suber notes that neither man gives a reason to win other than the contradiction in the other's position. The answer that holds is neither man's argument but the plain reading above: the court should rule for Euathlus now, because on the day of the suit nothing is yet due."
finePrint: "Suber notes some loose ends. A few writers suggest Euathlus might then sue Protagoras in turn, for malicious prosecution, and they disagree on who should win that third case. Suber also points out that law has tools logic lacks. A judge could reread the contract in light of the situation or “trade usage”, reform the contract in equity, send the logical difficulty to a jury, or appeal openly to a policy. Without policies or principles of that kind, he writes, it is difficult to see how the case could be decided, since neither side gives a reason to win other than the contradiction in the other's argument. If the two arguments weigh the same, he adds, the side with the burden of proof loses, and that works against Protagoras, who brought the suit. Gellius wrote roughly 600 years after Protagoras lived, and Suber notes that accounts differ on details, such as whether Protagoras sued as soon as the lessons ended or waited until it was clear Euathlus was taking no cases."
