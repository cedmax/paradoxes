problem: "A teacher sues his student for unpaid fees. Who wins?"
claimA: "If the student loses, the court says pay, so he pays."
claimB: "If the student wins, the deal says he owes nothing yet, so he does not pay."
verdict: "The court's ruling and the contract's terms point in opposite directions."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that a court's ruling and the contract it is judging cannot contradict each other."
statusNote: "experts agree it is a real puzzle but disagree on how to resolve it."
setup: "Protagoras, a teacher of the ancient Greek world, agreed to teach a student named Euathlus the law. They agreed that Euathlus would pay after he won his first court case. Euathlus then went into politics instead and never took a case. Protagoras sued him for the fee. Protagoras argued: if I win, the court orders you to pay. If you win, you have now won your first case, so the contract says you must pay. Euathlus replied: if I win, the court says I owe nothing. If you win, I still have not won a case, so the contract says I owe nothing."
whyWrong: "Both arguments work from rules we normally trust: do what the court orders, and do what the contract says. The case is about whether Euathlus must pay on a contract that depends on the result of that very case, so the ruling and the contract each claim the last word."
resolution:
  - "The story comes from the Roman writer Aulus Gellius, in his Attic Nights. He reports that the judges found the arguments uncertain and insoluble. They feared that whichever way they ruled, the ruling would undo itself, so they put the case off."
  - "People who have studied it since have offered answers, which fall into two groups: legal ones, which look at how a court and a contract should work, and logical ones, which look at the self-reference. There is no single accepted answer, which is why the entry is marked Disputed."
positions:
  - title: Rule by the court
    body: "The judge should decide on the case in front of them, and the court's decision overrides the contract's wording for now. Cost: the contract was clear and the ruling then ignores it."
  - title: Rule by the contract
    body: "The contract is the thing being judged, so its terms decide. Cost: it makes the court's own ruling count for nothing, since a win for Euathlus would then trigger the fee."
  - title: Refuse to rule yet
    body: "Treat the case as premature. Euathlus has not yet won a case, so the fee is not due. Cost: it gives Euathlus the win on this occasion, though it leaves open whether Protagoras can sue again."
finePrint: "The sources I read agree on the story and on Gellius's account of the court, but they do not name who proposed which solution, so the positions above are a plain description of the main options and not attributed to named scholars. The story is told as a real lawsuit, and it is not clear that it ever happened."
