problem: "A teacher sues his student for unpaid fees. Who wins?"
claimA: "If Protagoras wins, the court makes Euathlus pay; if Euathlus wins, the contract does."
claimB: "Right now Euathlus owes nothing: he has not yet won a case, and the court should rule that way."
verdict: "The first claim is wrong. Euathlus should win this case, and Protagoras can then sue again and win."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that winning this very case can make the fee due in this very case."
statusNote: "when the suit is brought Euathlus has not yet won a case, so nothing is owed and he should win. The writers on the case agree, and add that Protagoras could then sue a second time and win."
setup: "Protagoras, a teacher of the ancient Greek world, agreed to teach a student named Euathlus the law. They agreed that Euathlus would pay after he won his first court case. Euathlus then went into politics instead of the law. Protagoras sued him for the fee. Protagoras argued: if I win, the court orders you to pay. If you win, you have now won your first case, so the contract says you must pay. Euathlus replied: if I win, the court says I owe nothing. If you win, I still have not won a case, so the contract says I owe nothing."
whyWrong: "Both arguments work from rules we normally trust: do what the court orders, and do what the contract says. The case is about whether Euathlus must pay on a contract that depends on the result of that very case, so the ruling and the contract each seem to claim the last word."
resolution:
  - "The story comes from the Roman writer Aulus Gellius, in his Attic Nights. He reports that the judges found the arguments uncertain and insoluble. They feared that whichever way they ruled, the ruling would undo itself, so they put the case off."
  - "The usual answer is simpler than the judges feared. The contract makes the fee due once Euathlus wins a case. When Protagoras sues, Euathlus has not won one, so nothing is owed yet, and he should win. The contract is not ignored: it is applied as it stands on the day of the suit."
  - "That win is Euathlus's first. Now the contract's condition is met, so Protagoras can sue a second time and win. Peter Suber, in his book The Paradox of Self-Amendment, reports that the small literature on the case all suggests Euathlus should have won, and that most commentators then observe that Protagoras could have sued again and won."
  - "So Protagoras's argument is the one that fails. Its second half counts Euathlus's win in this case as triggering the fee in this same case, when it can only trigger it afterwards."
finePrint: "Suber notes some loose ends. A few writers suggest Euathlus might then sue Protagoras in turn, for malicious prosecution, and they disagree on who should win that third case. Suber also points out that a court has tools logic lacks, such as reading the contract in light of what both sides meant. Gellius wrote roughly 600 years after Protagoras lived, and the details of the story vary between tellings."
