problem: "A Cretan says that all Cretans are liars. Can that be true, and can it be false?"
claimA: "If the statement is false, then every Cretan is honest."
claimB: "Epimenides is a Cretan, so if every Cretan is honest, his statement is true."
verdict: "The opposite of “all Cretans are liars” is “some Cretan is honest”, which causes no clash."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that the opposite of “all Cretans are liars” is “all Cretans are honest”."
statusNote: "the first claim is wrong. Wikipedia reports that if the statement is false, only some Cretan must be honest, and that need not be Epimenides."
setup: "Wikipedia says the paradox is named after Epimenides of Knossos, a Cretan philosopher who lived around 600 BC. A well-known description, in Douglas Hofstadter’s book Gödel, Escher, Bach, has Epimenides saying: “All Cretans are liars.” It is often claimed that a paradox of self-reference arises when we ask whether Epimenides could have spoken the truth. Wikipedia quotes Thomas Fowler’s 1869 version: Epimenides is a Cretan, so if what he says is untrue, then the Cretans are truthful, and then what he says is true, and so on in a circle."
whyWrong: "Wikipedia says Fowler’s version contains a logic error. If what Epimenides said is untrue, it does not follow that all Cretans are honest. The opposite of “all Cretans are liars” is “not all Cretans are liars”, which means that there is at least one honest Cretan. That honest Cretan need not be Epimenides."
resolution:
  - "So the statement can simply be false, made by a lying Cretan who knows at least one honest Cretan. From the assumption that the statement is false, it does not follow that it is true, and there is no paradox. Wikipedia says this mistake was made by Fowler and by many others."
  - "Wikipedia gives further points. For “all Cretans are liars” to be true, it need not mean that every Cretan lies all the time, since a liar can be someone prone to deception for gain. It also notes that the original phrase was an opinion about his people, and not an absolute statement, and that the idea of the phrase as a paradox dates only from the 19th century."
  - "Wikipedia adds that the paradox can be modified to block this solution, as in the first paradox of Eubulides. That version leads to an unavoidable contradiction and is a form of the [Liar paradox](liar-paradox). Wikipedia says the Epimenides paradox is usually classified as a variation on the Liar, and that the two are sometimes not distinguished."
finePrint: "This entry covers only the logic. Wikipedia’s article also covers the origin of the phrase, including ancient poems and other early sources, and a story from Greek mythology about why Cretans were called liars. Those parts are not covered here."
