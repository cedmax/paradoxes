problem: "A Cretan says that all Cretans are liars. Can that be true, and can it be false?"
claimA: "The statement cannot be false without a contradiction, because if it is false, every Cretan, Epimenides included, is honest, and then what he said is true."
claimB: "The statement can be false without any contradiction, because it only needs one honest Cretan, who need not be Epimenides."
verdict: "Denying that all Cretans are liars only says that some Cretan is honest. So a Cretan who says it can simply be wrong, and there is no paradox."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that the opposite of “all Cretans are liars” is “all Cretans are honest”."
statusNote: "the belief that the statement cannot be false is wrong. Its opposite is only that some Cretan is honest, so it can be false with no contradiction."
setup: "Epimenides was a Cretan who lived around 600 BC. A typical modern telling, in Douglas Hofstadter’s book Gödel, Escher, Bach, has him say: “All Cretans are liars.” That is not an exact quote. The line that has come down, through the Epistle to Titus in the New Testament, is “The Cretians are always liars, evil beasts, slow bellies”, and the epistle says it was spoken by a prophet of the Cretans themselves. It is often claimed that a paradox of self-reference arises when one asks whether Epimenides could have spoken the truth. Thomas Fowler set out the reasoning in 1869: Epimenides is himself a Cretan, so if what he says is untrue, the Cretans are truthful, and then what he says is true, and so on in a circle."
whyWrong: "Fowler’s version contains a logic error. If what Epimenides said is untrue, it does not follow that all Cretans are truthful. The opposite of “all Cretans are liars” is not “all Cretans are honest” but “not all Cretans are liars”, which only means that at least one Cretan is honest. That honest Cretan need not be Epimenides. The slip feels natural because “all are liars” and “all are honest” sound like opposites, but they are not the only two options."
resolution:
  - "So the statement can simply be false, said by a lying Cretan while some other Cretan is honest. Assuming it is false does not lead back to it being true, and the circle never starts. Fowler made this mistake, and so have many others."
  - "This is why the entry is Explained, while the [Liar paradox](liar-paradox) is Unreconciled. “This sentence is false” leaves no way out: whatever answer is given, the reasoning sends it to the other, and there is no slip to point to. “All Cretans are liars” talks about every Cretan, not only about itself, and the facts about other Cretans give it room to be false. The paradox only seemed to arise because of a wrong step in the reasoning."
  - "The puzzle can be repaired. Bertrand Russell, opening his 1908 paper on the theory of types, wrote that “Epimenides the Cretan said that all Cretans were liars, and all other statements made by Cretans were certainly lies.” With that extra fact, the only way for the statement to be false is for Epimenides’ own statement to be true, and if it is true, his own statement is one of the lies. So the way out is closed. The ancient philosopher Eubulides gave a version, “A man says that he is lying”, that leads to a contradiction with no extra facts at all. Versions like these are forms of the Liar, not of the original Cretan line, and they share the Liar’s status."
finePrint: "Even read strictly, as “every statement made by a Cretan is false”, the line cannot be true, since then Epimenides’ own statement would be false. It can only be false, and that is no contradiction, as long as some Cretan has said something true. Read loosely, it can even be true: a liar can be someone prone to deceiving for gain, who still often tells the truth. The line was probably meant as an opinion about the Cretans, not a puzzle, and it has been treated as a paradox only since the 19th century. A sentence like Russell’s, whose trouble depends on facts outside it, is sometimes called a contingent liar. The Epimenides paradox is usually classed as a variation on the Liar, and the two are sometimes not told apart. The origins of the phrase, including ancient poems and stories about why Cretans were called liars, are not covered here."
