problem: "A whale has about 1,000 times more cells than a human. Do large animals really get no more cancer than small ones?"
claimA: "Large animals do not have higher cancer prevalence than small animals, despite having many more cells."
claimB: "Large animals have higher cancer prevalence than small animals, consistent with the number of cells contributing to cancer risk."
verdict: "Researchers disagree on whether large animals really escape the extra cancer their cell numbers suggest. Earlier studies found no link between body size and cancer, while two large recent studies found cancer rising with size."
whatBreaks: "Which claim to give up: that large animals get no more cancer than small ones, or that their extra cells show up as more cancer."
statusNote: "researchers disagree about whether Peto’s paradox holds across species. A 2024 study of more than 16,000 necropsies (animal autopsies) of 292 vertebrate species, from zoos and aquariums, found that cancer prevalence rises with body size, though only slightly. A 2025 study by George Butler and colleagues re-examined that same dataset and also found higher cancer prevalence in larger land vertebrates, so the two are not independent confirmations. Both rest on animals in human care, which may not represent wild populations. They challenge the classic claim that cancer does not rise with body size, but do not show that cancer risk scales directly with cell number."
setup: "Cancer starts when a cell picks up harmful changes in its DNA, usually while dividing. So more cells, and more cell divisions over a longer life, should mean more chances for cancer. In work published in 1975 and 1977, Richard Peto compared mice and humans: humans have about 1,000 times more cells than mice, yet cell for cell they are much less prone to cancer. The paradox named after him is that, across species, cancer rates do not seem to follow the number of cells in the body. A whale has about 1,000 times more cells than a human. If each cell carried the same risk, a whale would be expected to get far more cancer."
whyWrong: "Within one species, size does seem to matter. A 25-year study of 17,738 male British civil servants, published in 1998, and a 2011 study of over a million British women found that taller people get more cancer, even after other risk factors were taken into account. A 2011 analysis of 74,556 dogs found the least cancer in smaller breeds. Across species, the pattern breaks down. A 2015 study by Abegglen and colleagues, using zoo records for 36 mammal species from a 51-gram mouse to a 4,800-kilogram elephant, found no significant link between body size and cancer. Larger later studies, of 292 vertebrate species in 2024 and of land vertebrates in 2025, found that cancer does rise with body size, though only slightly. So whether the paradox holds depends on which studies are trusted, and how much weight zoo records can bear."
resolution:
  - "Those who take the paradox at face value follow Peto’s suggestion that evolution made the risk per cell differ between species. Reviewing the evidence in 2017, Marc Tollis, Amy Boddy and Carlo Maley concluded that stronger-than-human cancer suppression has evolved many times across the tree of life."
  - "Some defences have been found. Elephants carry about 20 copies of the tumour suppressor gene TP53, where humans have one. The gene’s protein pauses a cell with damaged DNA while it is repaired, or makes it destroy itself if the damage is too great, and elephant cells exposed to radiation are much more likely to destroy themselves. A 2018 study from Vincent Lynch’s group found that a gene called LIF6, a broken copy of a gene that regained a working on-switch, is turned on by TP53 in elephants and kills damaged cells. The authors say how it does this is still unclear, and that it is not enough on its own to explain how elephants became so large. Whales seem to manage differently: Tollis and colleagues report no evidence that whales evolved extra copies of any tumour suppressor gene."
  - "Others read the newest data as showing there is no paradox to explain. The two main positions are below."
positions:
  - title: "The paradox holds"
    body: "Across species, cancer does not rise with body size the way cell numbers suggest, as the 2015 zoo study of 36 mammals found, and large animals have evolved defences such as the extra TP53 genes of elephants. Cost: two larger recent studies, of 292 vertebrate species in 2024 and of land vertebrates in 2025, found that cancer prevalence does rise with body size."
  - title: "There is no paradox"
    body: "George Butler and colleagues found in 2025 that larger species have higher cancer prevalence across amphibians, birds, mammals and squamate reptiles (lizards and snakes), and concluded that there is no evidence for Peto’s paradox in land vertebrates. Cost: the rise found in the recent studies is smaller than a simple cell count would predict, and the data come mostly from zoo animals, which may not represent animals in the wild."
finePrint: "The 2024 study found that cancer prevalence fell with longer gestation time. Its link between adult body mass and malignancy prevalence was only apparent when gestation time was controlled for. This is a puzzle from biology, not a logical contradiction. The defences above are partial: no single one is known to explain the pattern across all large species, and they are documented whichever position is right about the overall pattern. Maciak and Michalak pointed to cell size and metabolism: larger animals have larger cells that divide more slowly, and lower metabolic rates, which may mean fewer chances for harmful mutations. Why medium-sized animals lack such defences is also unsettled. One suggestion is that, for them, the benefit was outweighed by costs such as reduced fertility."
