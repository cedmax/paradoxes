problem: "Larger animals have more cells that could become cancerous. Do they actually get more cancer?"
claimA: "Large animals do not have higher cancer prevalence than small animals, despite having many more cells."
claimB: "Large animals have higher cancer prevalence than small animals, consistent with the number of cells contributing to cancer risk."
whatBreaks: "Which claim to give up: that large animals get no more cancer than small ones, or that their extra cells show up as more cancer."
statusNote: "elephants’ extra copies of a gene that responds to damaged DNA suggest evolved defences, which one camp says explains why big animals get no more cancer, while recent analyses find that larger species do get more."
setup: "Cancer starts with harmful changes in cells. More cells seem to mean more opportunities for cancer, so large animals should face a much bigger risk if every cell has the same chance. Yet comparisons between species have often found much less extra cancer than that simple prediction suggests."
whyWrong: "A whale has far more cells than a human. Why does it not suffer vastly more cancer? One answer is that evolution gave large species stronger defences. But newer analyses also challenge the original claim that cancer does not rise with body size at all."
resolution:
  - "Large species do have documented defences. Elephants, for example, have extra copies of a gene involved in responding to damaged DNA. That supports the idea that cancer risk per cell can differ between species."
  - "It does not settle the overall pattern. Studies disagree on the relationship between size and cancer, and a modest rise with size is different from risk rising directly in proportion to the number of cells."
positions:
  - title: "The paradox holds"
    body: "Earlier studies found no clear link, and evolved defences could explain why more cells do not bring proportionally more cancer. Cost: larger recent analyses find cancer prevalence rising with size."
  - title: "The classic claim fails"
    body: "Recent analyses find more cancer in larger species, challenging the claim of no size relationship. Cost: the rise is modest, and data from animals in human care may not describe the wild."
finePrint: "Cancer prevalence means the fraction found to have cancer in a sample, not the lifetime risk per cell. A 2015 study covered 36 mammal species. A 2024 study used more than 16,000 animal autopsies across 292 vertebrate species, and Butler and colleagues reanalysed the same dataset in 2025. These are not independent confirmations. The 2024 study found lower prevalence with longer gestation; the adult body-mass link to malignancy appeared only after controlling for gestation. Both recent analyses concern animals in human care. Elephants’ extra TP53 copies and LIF6 are partial defences, not a complete explanation for all large animals. Whether or not the no-size-link claim survives, a modest increase is not proof of direct scaling with cell number."
