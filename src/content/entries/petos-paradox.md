problem: "A whale has about 1,000 times more cells than a human. Why does it not get far more cancer?"
claimA: "Every cell has the same small chance of turning cancerous, so a body with far more cells should get far more cancer."
claimB: "Large animals such as whales and elephants do not get cancer more often than small ones."
verdict: "Large animals have evolved better defences against cancer, so the chance per cell is lower."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that the chance of a cell turning cancerous is the same in every species."
statusNote: "the first claim is wrong. The chance per cell differs between species, though Wikipedia reports that research on the reasons is still active."
setup: "Richard Peto, an English statistician and epidemiologist, noted in 1977 that, cell for cell, humans were much less prone to cancer than mice. The paradox named after him is the observation that, across species, the rate of cancer does not appear to follow the number of cells in the body. The cancer rate in humans is much higher than in whales, even though a whale has about 1,000 times more cells. If the chance of a cell becoming cancerous were the same everywhere, whales should get more cancer than humans."
whyWrong: "Within one species, size does seem to matter. Wikipedia cites a 25-year study of 17,738 male British civil servants published in 1998, and a 2011 study of over a million British women, that found taller people have more cancer even after other risk factors were controlled for. A 2011 analysis of 74,556 dogs found less cancer in smaller breeds. Across species, though, the pattern breaks down. A 2015 San Diego Zoo survey of 36 mammal species, from a 51-gram mouse to a 4,800-kilogram elephant, found no significant link between body size and cancer incidence."
resolution:
  - "Peto suggested that evolution made the chance of cancer per cell differ between species. Wikipedia reports evidence for this. Two studies in October 2015 found that African elephants carry 20 copies of the tumour suppressor gene TP53, and Asian elephants 15 to 20, where humans and most other mammals have one. The gene’s protein either pauses a damaged cell while it is repaired or triggers its death if the damage is too great."
  - "Wikipedia also describes a 2014 paper by Maciak and Michalak, who emphasised cell size and metabolism. Larger animals have larger, more slowly dividing cells and lower metabolic rates. Fewer cell divisions mean fewer chances for cancer-causing mutations. Wikipedia says these factors, combined, may explain much of the apparent paradox."
  - "Wikipedia notes the evolutionary side too. Larger and longer-lived bodies needed more protection against cancer. The reason medium-sized animals have fewer of these protective genes may be that, for them, the benefit was outweighed by costs such as reduced fertility."
finePrint: "This is an observation from biology, not a logical contradiction. Wikipedia says the explanations are still being studied and describes them with words such as “may explain” and “suggest”. The article also covers medical research on elephant-like TP53 in mice, which is not covered here."
