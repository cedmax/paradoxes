problem: "A whale has about 1,000 times more cells than a human. Why does it not get far more cancer?"
claimA: "Every cell has the same small chance of turning cancerous in every species, so a body with far more cells should get far more cancer."
claimB: "Across species, cancer rates rise only slightly with body size, if at all, so whales and elephants do not get far more cancer than smaller animals."
verdict: "The chance of a cell turning cancerous is not the same in every species. Large animals have evolved stronger defences against cancer."
whatBreaks: "The first claim: that every cell has the same chance of turning cancerous, whatever the species."
statusNote: "the first claim is wrong. Researchers agree that large, long-lived animals have a lower cancer risk per cell. Which defences explain it is still being studied, and recent studies disagree on whether cancer rises slightly with body size or not at all."
setup: "Cancer starts when a cell picks up harmful changes in its DNA, usually while dividing. So more cells, and more cell divisions over a longer life, should mean more chances for cancer. In work published in 1975 and 1977, Richard Peto compared mice and humans: humans have about 1,000 times more cells than mice, yet cell for cell they are much less prone to cancer. The paradox named after him is that, across species, cancer rates do not seem to follow the number of cells in the body. A whale has about 1,000 times more cells than a human, but humans have a much higher cancer rate."
whyWrong: "Within one species, size does seem to matter. A 25-year study of 17,738 male British civil servants, published in 1998, and a 2011 study of over a million British women found that taller people get more cancer, even after other risk factors were taken into account. A 2011 analysis of 74,556 dogs found the least cancer in smaller breeds. Across species, the pattern breaks down. A 2015 study by Abegglen and colleagues, using zoo records for 36 mammal species from a 51-gram mouse to a 4,800-kilogram elephant, found no significant link between body size and cancer. Larger later studies, of 292 vertebrate species in 2024 and of land vertebrates in 2025, found that cancer does rise with body size, but only slightly, far less than the number of cells would predict. Either way, if every cell had the same chance of turning cancerous, large animals would get far more cancer than they do."
resolution:
  - "The reasoning from cell numbers is sound, so the observation knocks out its premise: the risk per cell must be lower in large species. Peto suggested that evolution made the risk per cell differ between species. Reviewing the evidence in 2017, Marc Tollis, Amy Boddy and Carlo Maley concluded that stronger-than-human cancer suppression has evolved many times across the tree of life."
  - "Some defences have been found. Elephants carry about 20 copies of the tumour suppressor gene TP53, where humans have one. The gene’s protein pauses a cell with damaged DNA while it is repaired, or makes it destroy itself if the damage is too great, and elephant cells exposed to radiation are much more likely to destroy themselves. A 2018 study from Vincent Lynch’s group found that a gene called LIF6, a broken copy of a gene that regained a working on-switch, is turned on by TP53 in elephants and kills damaged cells. The authors say how it does this is still unclear, and that it is not enough on its own to explain how elephants became so large."
  - "Whales seem to manage differently. Tollis and colleagues report no evidence that whales evolved extra copies of any tumour suppressor gene. The genome of the bowhead whale, which can live over 200 years, showed changes in genes that may be linked to long life. Maciak and Michalak pointed to cell size and metabolism: larger animals have larger cells that divide more slowly, and lower metabolic rates, which may mean fewer chances for harmful mutations."
finePrint: "This is a puzzle from biology, not a logical contradiction. The data come mostly from zoo animals, which may not represent animals in the wild. A 2025 study led by George Butler, titled “No evidence for Peto’s paradox in terrestrial vertebrates”, argued that cancer does rise with size in land vertebrates. It also found the rise was smaller than expected, so the risk per cell is still lower in large animals. The mechanisms above are partial: no single one is known to explain the pattern across all large species. Why medium-sized animals lack such defences is also unsettled. One suggestion is that, for them, the benefit was outweighed by costs such as reduced fertility."
