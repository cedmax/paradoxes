problem: "Are there as many square numbers as there are whole numbers?"
claimA: "Squares are only some of the whole numbers, so there must be fewer of them."
claimB: "Every whole number has exactly one square, so there must be exactly as many."
verdict: "Under the standard definition there are exactly as many squares as whole numbers, because “as many” means they can be matched one to one."
whatBreaks: "The idea that a part is always smaller than the whole. Under the standard one-to-one measure of size this only holds for finite collections, though the theory of numerosities keeps it for infinite ones too."
statusNote: "the first claim is wrong under the standard mathematical definition of size, which compares infinite collections by matching them one to one. That definition is a choice, not the only possible one."
setup: "The squares are 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 and so on. Most whole numbers are not squares: 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8 and so on. So the squares look like a thin slice of all the whole numbers, and there should be fewer of them. But pair them off. Each whole number has exactly one square, and each square has exactly one root. Nobody is left without a partner on either side. That suggests the two lists are the same size."
whyWrong: "With finite collections, a part is always smaller than the whole, and matching up one for one always gives the same count. Both rules work every time. With an endless collection they pull in opposite directions."
resolution:
  - "Standard mathematics compares these infinite sizes by matching. Every positive whole number n pairs with its square n², and every square appears once. By that test the collections are the same size."
  - "The squares still become sparser: there are 10 up to 100 and 100 up to 10,000. Matching and density answer different questions. The assumption that every proper part must have a smaller size fails for infinite collections under the matching definition."
  - "This does not make all infinities equal. Some infinite collections cannot be matched one to one. [Hilbert’s hotel](hilberts-hotel) shows another use of an infinite collection matching a part of itself."
finePrint: 'Strictly, this is a choice of definition. Both ideas of "as many" are natural, and for infinite collections they disagree. Cantor sided with matching, and that is the standard choice in mathematics. It is not the only coherent one. In the early 2000s Benci, di Nasso and Forti built a theory of "numerosities" that agrees with ordinary counting on finite sets and keeps the rule that the whole is bigger than the part for infinite sets too. So the first claim is wrong in standard set theory, not in every possible theory. Duns Scotus compared even numbers to all numbers around 1302, long before Galileo.'
