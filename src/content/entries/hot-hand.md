problem: "Why did a famous study find no streaks in basketball shooting, when shooters and fans are sure they exist?"
claimA: "If a shooter were ever hot, shots after a streak of hits would go in more often than average. The 1985 study found that they did not, so the hot hand is a myth."
claimB: "Counted the way the 1985 study counted, a fair coin actually shows fewer heads than average after a streak of heads. So that finding cannot show that streaks are an illusion."
verdict: "The study’s way of counting is biased against finding streaks. Corrected, the same data show some evidence of streak shooting."
whatBreaks: "The assumption that a streak-free shooter would show no change after a streak when counted this way. Counted this way, a streak-free shooter shows fewer hits after a streak. A later analysis found the 1985 method was biased against finding streaks. Corrected, the same data show some evidence of a hot hand."
statusNote: "the first claim is wrong, because the counting method is biased in a way that hides streaks. How large the effect is, and in how many players, is still debated."
setup: "In 1985 Gilovich, Vallone and Tversky published a study of basketball. They looked at the shot records of the 1980 to 1981 Philadelphia 76ers, at free throws, and at a controlled shooting experiment. They found that shots were no more likely to go in after hits than after misses, and concluded that feeling hot does not predict the next shot. For decades the hot hand was the standard example of a belief that is an illusion."
whyWrong: "The study counted the shots that came right after a streak of hits or misses. Taking only those shots seems fair. It is not, when the records are short. In a short fair sequence, counting what follows a streak of hits makes the leftover shots lean towards misses. A shooter with no streaks at all would therefore be expected to hit less often after hits than after misses, so finding no difference is a sign that something real is going on."
resolution:
  - "Take three flips of a fair coin. Look at every flip that comes straight after a head, work out the share of those flips that are heads, and average that share over all the three-flip sequences that have such a flip. The answer is 5 out of 12, about 42 percent, not 50 percent. The coin has no streaks, yet the counting method gives less than one half."
  - "In 2018 Miller and Sanjurjo reanalysed the 1985 data with this bias in mind and found significant evidence of streak shooting. So the first claim is false: the missing rise after streaks did not show that streaks are a myth."
finePrint: "The size of the effect is still argued over. Pelechrinis and Winston (2022) suggest that only a small number of players show a hot hand and that the effect is small. Other studies found strong evidence, such as an analysis of more than 300,000 free throws, and some recent studies found none. Open questions include how far results carry across sports and how large the effect is. The coin figure of 5 out of 12 is my own recomputation, and it is the three-flip case, not a result stated on the source page."
